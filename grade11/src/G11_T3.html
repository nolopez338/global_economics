C1

Identifies the essential elements required to conduct a hypothesis test in finance and economics, including the population parameter of interest, hypothesised value, sample statistic, sample size, variability, significance level, and relevant sampling conditions.

C2

Formulates null and alternative hypotheses for a population parameter and determines whether the corresponding test is left-tailed, right-tailed, or two-tailed from the direction of the research claim.

C3

Distinguishes Type I and Type II errors by identifying the incorrect decision associated with each and explaining their consequences in economic and financial contexts.

C4

Interprets the significance level and power of a hypothesis test, explaining how sample size, effect size, variability, and the selected significance level influence the test’s ability to detect a false null hypothesis.

C5

Conducts hypothesis tests for a population mean using the Z-test when the variance is known and the t-test when the variance is unknown, selecting the appropriate statistic and critical value.

C6

Conducts hypothesis tests for a population proportion using the Z-test, interpreting the test statistic in contexts such as default rates, investment success, or market participation.

C7

Conducts paired-sample t-tests to evaluate mean differences between matched or repeated observations, verifying the required conditions and interpreting the results in economic and financial contexts.

C8

Conducts independent two-sample t-tests to compare the means of two populations, verifying the required conditions and interpreting the results in economic and financial contexts.

C9

Applies computational thinking and development skills to automate hypothesis testing procedures, validate statistical calculations, represent results visually, and generate clear contextual interpretations.

C10

Integrates research design, data management, statistical inference, critical evaluation, and scientific communication to investigate economic or financial questions and support evidence-based conclusions.

Conceptual Referents

  • Area of simple geometric figures.
  • Sample and population
  • Random variable
  • Continuous probability distribution
  • Mean and variance.
  • Normal distribution
  • t-student distribution
  • Investment portfolios

Inference in Context

  • Confidence statements: Frame findings in economic language (e.g., "We are 95% confident the average return lies between...").
  • Normal vs. t-student: Choose based on whether variance is known and sample size.
  • Proportion focus: Useful for adoption rates, default rates, or market share analyses.

Portfolio Applications

Example: Estimating the mean return of a portfolio with unknown variance uses t critical values; the resulting interval guides whether to rebalance toward safer assets.

Interpret intervals alongside risk measures to decide if expected performance meets economic objectives.

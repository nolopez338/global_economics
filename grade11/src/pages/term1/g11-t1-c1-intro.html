Models for uncertain phenomena

  1. Introduction

    Mathematical models describe the world.

    Probability distributions are mathematical models for different kinds of uncertain phenomena. Because real-world situations have different structures, they naturally lead us to different distributions.

    Bernoulli distribution

    A discrete model for one trial with two possible outcomes.

    • X ∈ {0, 1}
    • Target outcome: P(X = 1) = p
    • Other outcome: P(X = 0) = 1 − p

    PMF: P(X = x) = px(1 − p)1−x,  x ∈ {0,1}

    Binomial distribution

    A discrete model in which X counts successes in n trials.

    • X ∈ {0, 1, …, n}; parameters are n and p.
    • A fixed number of independent, comparable trials
    • Two outcomes per trial and constant success probability p

    P(X = k) = C(n,k)pk(1 − p)n−k

    Geometric distribution

    A discrete model for repeated independent trials with constant success probability p.

    • X is the number of attempts until the first success.
    • X ∈ {1, 2, 3, …}

    P(X = k) = (1 − p)k−1p,  k = 1,2,3,…

    Poisson distribution

    A discrete model that counts independent events over a fixed exposure at a stable average rate.

    • λ is the expected event count over that exposure.
    • X ∈ {0, 1, 2, …}

    PMF: P(X = k) = e−λλk/k!,  k = 0,1,2,…

    Uniform distribution

    A continuous model with bounded support [a,b] and constant probability density.

    • Equal-length intervals have equal probabilities.
    • Probabilities are areas under the PDF.

    PDF: f(x) = 1/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b

    Linear distribution

    A continuous model on bounded support whose PDF changes linearly with x; it may increase or decrease.

    • Here, f(x) = x/50 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 10.
    • The PDF is nonnegative and its total area is 1.
    • Probabilities are areas under the density curve, not individual heights.

    Triangular distribution

    A continuous model with minimum a, mode c, and maximum b.

    • The density rises linearly from a to the mode c.
    • It falls linearly from c to b.
    • Probability corresponds to area under the density curve.

    Normal distribution

    A continuous model with a symmetric, bell-shaped density.

    • The mean μ controls its centre.
    • The standard deviation σ controls its spread.
    • Values near μ are more common than values far from μ.
    • Probabilities are areas under the density curve.

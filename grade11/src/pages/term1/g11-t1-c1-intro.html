Models for uncertain phenomena

  1. Introduction

    Mathematical models describe the world.

    Probability distributions are mathematical models for different kinds of uncertain phenomena. Because real-world situations have different structures, they naturally lead us to different distributions.

  2. Discrete probability distributions

    A discrete random variable takes values from a finite or countably infinite set, so its possible values can be listed individually.

    • A probability mass function (PMF) assigns \(P(X=x)\) to each possible value.
    • \(P(X=x)\ge 0\) for every possible value, and individual values may have positive probability.
    • A graph shows probability mass at distinct values, not a continuous density.

    \[\sum_x P(X=x)=1\]

    Bernoulli distribution

    A Bernoulli experiment consists of exactly one trial with exactly two outcomes, commonly coded as \(X\in\{0,1\}\).

    • Its single parameter is the success probability \(p\).
    • \(P(X=1)=p\) and \(P(X=0)=1-p\).

    \[P(X=x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}, \quad x \in \{0,1\}\]

    Binomial distribution

    A Binomial experiment fixes \(n\) Bernoulli-type trials in advance and counts the total successes.

    • Each independent trial has two outcomes and the same success probability \(p\).
    • Parameters: \(n\) and \(p\); support: \(X\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}\).
    • Unlike Bernoulli it uses multiple trials; unlike Geometric it fixes their number and counts successes.

    \[P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\]

    Geometric distribution

    A Geometric experiment repeats independent Bernoulli-type trials with constant success probability \(p\) and no predetermined number of trials.

    • The experiment stops at the first success; \(X\) counts all trials through that success.
    • Support: \(X\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\).
    • Unlike Binomial, it waits for the first success and counts trials rather than fixing trials and counting successes.

    \[P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p\]

    Poisson distribution

    A Poisson model counts events occurring independently during a specified interval or exposure at a stable average rate.

    • The parameter \(\lambda\) is the expected event count in that exposure; here \(\lambda=4\) per 10-minute segment.
    • Support: \(X\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\).

    \[P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}\]

  11. Continuous probability distributions

    A continuous random variable can take any value within one or more intervals. A probability density function (PDF), \(f(x)\), describes how density is distributed.

    • The density satisfies \(f(x)\ge0\), and its total area represents total probability.
    • Interval probabilities are areas under the density curve.
    • For any exact value, \(P(X=x)=0\); the height \(f(x)\) is density, not the probability of obtaining exactly \(x\).

    \[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\qquad P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\]

    Uniform distribution

    Uniform density is constant throughout its bounded support \([a,b]\), so no part of the interval is favoured.

    • Intervals of equal length have equal probability.
    • Its parameters are the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\).

    \[f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b,\\0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

    Linear distribution

    A Linear distribution has bounded support and a density that changes linearly with \(x\); it may increase or decrease across its interval.

    • Here \(f(x)=\frac{x}{50}\) for \(0\le x\le10\), and \(0\) otherwise.
    • In this example, later exit times are more densely represented than earlier ones.

    Triangular distribution

    A Triangular distribution has bounded support determined by minimum \(a\) and maximum \(b\), with mode \(c\) between them.

    • Density increases linearly from \(a\) to \(c\), then decreases linearly from \(c\) to \(b\).
    • The mode \(c\) is the location of maximum density; the parameters are \(a,c,b\).

    \[\text{Peak height}=\frac{2}{b-a}=0.10\]

    Normal distribution

    The Normal distribution has a symmetric, bell-shaped density with unbounded support \(( -\infty,\infty )\).

    • Its parameters are \(\mu\), which sets the centre, and \(\sigma\), which controls spread.
    • Values near \(\mu\) have greater density.
    • Both tails extend indefinitely and approach, but never reach, the horizontal axis.

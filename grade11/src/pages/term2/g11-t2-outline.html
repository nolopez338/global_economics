Grade 11 · Term 2 · Interactive outline

What can a business infer about its average transaction processing cost and the share of transactions processed late?

Hypothetical case. A business processed 10,000 online card transactions in October. A transaction counts as late if it missed the service deadline.

  1. Step 1

    From a sample to a population

    The population is the business’s 10,000 October online card transactions. A point estimate gives one value, such as a hypothetical sample mean of $48. An interval estimate gives a range of plausible values for the population mean, such as $46.04 to $49.96 in Step 2.

    estimate ± critical value × standard error

    The estimate sets the centre. The critical value reflects the confidence level. The standard error measures how much the estimate varies across samples. Their product (critical value × standard error) is the margin of error, half the interval’s width.

  2. Step 2

    Build z intervals for a mean and a proportion

    Hypothetical sample A. Draw a simple random sample of 100 of the 10,000 transactions without replacement. For the mean, assume σ = $10 was established independently for this population. Both 95% intervals use z* = 1.96.

    Check the conditions. The sample is random and covers only 1% of the population, supporting approximate independence. For the mean, σ is known and n = 100 supports a normal approximation assuming costs have no extreme skew or outliers. For the one-proportion z interval, 20 late and 80 on-time both meet the minimum of 10.

    We are 95% confident the October population mean cost lies between $46.04 and $49.96. For the late share, we are 95% confident it lies between 12.16% and 27.84%.

    Criterion C4

    Constructs confidence intervals for a population mean with known variance using the normal distribution, applying them to contexts such as average income, average investment return, or average price level.

    Criterion C5

    Constructs confidence intervals for a population proportion using sample proportions, applying them to contexts such as poverty rates, defaulting borrowers, or successful investments.

  3. Step 3

    Plan sample size and compare precision

    To plan a new sample, set the target margin of error E. For 95% confidence, use z* = 1.96.

    • Mean, known σ. n ≥ (z*σ/E)². With σ = $10 and E = $2, (1.96 × 10/2)² = 96.04, so sample at least 97.
    • Proportion. n ≥ (z*/E)² p*(1 − p*). With E = 0.05 (5 percentage points) and planning proportion p* = 0.50, (1.96/0.05)² × 0.50 × 0.50 = 384.16, so sample at least 385. The choice 0.50 gives the largest planned sample because it maximises p*(1 − p*).

    Round up to a whole transaction so the planned margin of error stays at or below E.

  4. Step 4

    Use Student’s t and check the design

    Hypothetical sample B. Take a separate simple random sample of 25 transactions from the same October population. In this example, assume σ is unknown. The sample gives x̄ = $48 and s = $10.

    Apply the Term 1 distinction to intervals for a mean. With known σ, use z as in Step 2. Here s estimates σ, so use Student’s t with df = 25 − 1 = 24. For 95% confidence, t* ≈ 2.064 (2.5% in each tail).

    SE = s/√n = $2
    Margin = 2.064 × $2 ≈ $4.13
    95% interval for μ ≈ $48 ± $4.13
    = $43.87 to $52.13

    We are 95% confident that the October population mean processing cost lies between $43.87 and $52.13 under these assumptions.

    • Design and bias. Sample randomly from the full October list. Check for missing records and errors in cost or late status.
    • Independence. Check for dependence such as shared batch costs. Without replacement, check n ≤ 10% of the population. Here 25 ≤ 1,000.
    • Mean. With a small sample, look for a roughly symmetric shape without strong outliers, as in sample B. A large sample can support a normal approximation for the mean.
    • Proportion (C5). Use the one-proportion z procedure with binary outcomes and at least 10 observations in each category.

  5. Step 5

    Turn an interval into a defensible conclusion

    Using hypothetical sample B, can the business conclude that the population mean processing cost is below its $50 benchmark?

    The interval includes values below and above $50, so the sample leaves the benchmark question unresolved at this confidence level.

    The inference covers the business’s October online card transactions. In-store transactions use a different processing system and fall outside the sample frame.

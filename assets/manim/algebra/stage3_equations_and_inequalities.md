# Stage 3: Equations and Inequalities

**Duration:** 2 weeks

## 1. Purpose and learning goals

[Stage 2](stage2_algebraic_expressions.md) represented quantities with expressions. Now you will find values that satisfy an equation or inequality, justify each transformation, rearrange formulas, and check whether answers make sense. A **solution set** contains all allowed values that make a statement true.

**Prerequisite check**

- **P1.** Simplify \(3(x+2)-2x\).
- **P2.** Evaluate \(5n-120\) at \(n=24\).
- **P3.** Calculate \(-12\div(-3)\) and \(1/2+1/3\).

## 2. Guided lessons

### Lesson 1: Equality, inverse operations, and linear equations

An equation balances equal quantities. Adding or subtracting the same expression on both sides preserves equality; multiplying or dividing both sides by the same nonzero constant also preserves it. These reversible steps produce **equivalent equations**, with the same solutions. A linear equation has variables only to the first power and no products of variables.

**Worked example.** For the outline's \(3x+7=22\), subtract 7 to get \(3x=15\), then divide by 3 to get \(x=5\). Check: \(3(5)+7=22\). One-step equations use just one inverse operation, such as \(x+7=12\Rightarrow x=5\). For variables on both sides:
\[
2(x+3)=x+11\Rightarrow2x+6=x+11
\Rightarrow x+6=11\Rightarrow x=5.
\]
Distribution rewrites a side; subtracting \(x\) and then 6 keeps the balance.

If variables cancel, inspect what remains. \(2x+6=2x+6\) reduces to \(6=6\): every real number is a solution. \(2x+6=2x+7\) reduces to \(6=7\): no solution.

**Avoid this misconception:** “move a term and change its sign” hides the reason. Name the operation performed on both sides instead.

**Practice L1.** Solve (a) \(5x+2=3x+10\); (b) \(4(x+1)=4x+9\).

### Lesson 2: Fractions and literal equations

To clear numerical fractions, multiply **every term on both sides** by a common nonzero denominator. A **literal equation** is a formula involving several letters. Isolating one letter uses the same balance principle, while other letters represent fixed quantities for that calculation.

**Worked example.** Solve \(x/3+1/2=5/6\). Multiply by 6:
\[
2x+3=5\Rightarrow2x=2\Rightarrow x=1.
\]
Substitution gives \(1/3+1/2=5/6\). For a rectangle with perimeter \(P\), length \(l\), and width \(w\), \(P=2l+2w\). Subtract \(2l\) and divide by 2 to obtain \(w=(P-2l)/2=P/2-l\). If \(P=20\) m and \(l=6\) m, \(w=4\) m. In a formula such as \(y=mx+b\), isolating \(x\) gives \(x=(y-b)/m\) only when \(m\ne0\); division by a letter requires a condition.

**Avoid this misconception:** multiplying only \(x/3\) by 6 changes the equation. The constant terms must also be multiplied.

**Practice L2.** (a) Solve \(x/4-1/2=1\). (b) Isolate \(h\) in \(A=bh/2\), stating the needed condition.

### Lesson 3: Linear and compound inequalities

An inequality compares values: \(<\) and \(>\) are strict; \(\le\) and \(\ge\) include equality. Adding the same number preserves order. Multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses it: \(2<5\), but \(-2>-5\), because reflection through zero reverses positions on a number line.

**Worked example.** \(-2x+3\le11\Rightarrow-2x\le8\Rightarrow x\ge-4\). On a number line use a filled point at \(-4\) and shade right. A strict boundary uses an open point. Interval notation is \([-4,\infty)\); a square bracket includes an endpoint, a parenthesis excludes one, and infinity always gets a parenthesis.

A **compound inequality** joins conditions. “And” means their intersection, where both hold: \(-1<2x+3\le7\Rightarrow-4<2x\le4\Rightarrow-2<x\le2\), or \((-2,2]\). “Or” means their union, where at least one holds: \(x<-2\) or \(x>2\) describes two outer rays, \((-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)\).

**Avoid this misconception:** reverse the comparison only when multiplying or dividing by a negative, not when subtracting.

**Practice L3.** Solve and describe the number-line shading: (a) \(7-3x<1\); (b) \(2\le x+4<9\).

### Lesson 4: Absolute value as distance

\(|x-a|\) measures distance from \(a\). For \(r>0\), \(|x-a|=r\) means \(x=a-r\) or \(x=a+r\). The inequality \(|x-a|\le r\) means \(a-r\le x\le a+r\), while \(|x-a|>r\) means \(x<a-r\) or \(x>a+r\). The strict “inside” and non-strict “outside” versions follow by changing endpoint inclusion. Distance cannot be negative: \(|x-a|=r\) and \(|x-a|\le r\) have no solutions if \(r<0\). At \(r=0\), equality or \(\le\) allows only \(x=a\).

**Worked example.** A measurement \(t\) in centimeters must be within 2 cm of 10 cm: \(|t-10|\le2\). This becomes \(8\le t\le12\). Exactly 2 cm away gives \(t=8\) or \(12\). Farther than 2 cm gives \(t<8\) or \(t>12\). Drawing 10 on a number line makes these three cases visible.

**Avoid this misconception:** \(|x|=-3\) has no solutions; it does not mean \(x=\pm3\).

**Practice L4.** Solve (a) \(|x+1|=4\); (b) \(|x-2|>3\).

## 3. Connected application

The school event's profit is \(5n-120\) dollars, with \(n\) a nonnegative integer attendance count, $8 tickets, and cost \(120+3n\). Breaking even means profit equals zero:
\[
5n-120=0\Rightarrow5n=120\Rightarrow n=24.
\]
At 24 attendees, revenue and cost are both $192. To earn at least $50, solve \(5n-120\ge50\), giving \(n\ge34\). The smallest feasible attendance is 34. For a target of at least $52, the algebra gives \(n\ge34.4\), so the smallest whole-number attendance is 35. Solving adds a new ability: choosing inputs to meet a requirement. Always distinguish an exact equation from a minimum requirement and apply the counting restriction after solving.

## 4. Essential summary

Simplifying rewrites quantities; solving finds inputs making statements true. Reversible balance operations preserve equation solutions. Inequalities describe sets, and negative scaling reverses order. Compound conditions use intersections or unions. Absolute value describes distance. Formulas and word problems use the same methods, with units and restrictions governing interpretation.

## 5. Independent mastery check

- **M1.** Solve \(3(x-2)=2x+5\) and check.
- **M2.** Classify \(2(x+3)=2x+6\): one solution, none, or infinitely many? Explain.
- **M3.** Solve \((x-1)/3+1/2=5/6\).
- **M4.** Rearrange \(C=120+3n\) for \(n\). Find \(n\) if \(C=225\) dollars.
- **M5.** Error analysis: from \(-4x>12\), a learner writes \(x>-3\). Correct the result and test a value that reveals the mistake.
- **M6.** Solve \(|x-4|\le3\). Give interval notation and explain the distance meaning.
- **M7.** An unfamiliar elevator can carry at most 600 kg. Cargo weighs 150 kg and each identical crate weighs 35 kg. What is the maximum whole number of crates? Define the variable and inequality.
- **M8.** Solve \(-2<3x+1\le10\).

**Mastery criteria:** at least 7 of 8 correct, with valid transformations, checks, and contextual rounding. Revisit Lesson 1 for balance or solution classification, Lesson 2 for fractions/formulas, Lesson 3 for comparison signs or compound sets, and Lesson 4 for distance conditions.

## 6. Answers and feedback

**Prerequisites**

- **P1.** \(x+6\).
- **P2.** 0.
- **P3.** 4 and \(5/6\).

**Practice**

- **L1.** (a) \(x=4\); (b) no solution because \(4=9\) is false.
- **L2.** (a) \(x=6\); (b) \(h=2A/b\), \(b\ne0\).
- **L3.** (a) \(x>2\), open point at 2, shade right; (b) \(-2\le x<5\), shade between a filled \(-2\) and open 5.
- **L4.** (a) \(x=-5,3\); (b) \(x<-1\) or \(x>5\).

**Mastery**

- **M1.** \(x=11\); both sides equal 27.
- **M2.** infinitely many real solutions; distribution makes both sides identical.
- **M3.** \(2(x-1)+3=5\), so \(x=2\).
- **M4.** \(n=(C-120)/3\), giving 35 attendees.
- **M5.** \(x<-3\); \(x=0\) satisfies the learner's set but gives \(0>12\), false.
- **M6.** \([1,7]\), all points at most 3 units from 4.
- **M7.** \(k\) is a nonnegative integer crate count; \(150+35k\le600\), so \(k\le90/7\); maximum 12 crates (570 kg total), while 13 would weigh 605 kg.
- **M8.** \(-1<x\le3\), or \((-1,3]\).

# Stage 1: Arithmetic Foundations

**Duration:** 1–2 weeks

## 1. Purpose and learning goals

Arithmetic describes quantities and changes. This unit builds on counting and the four operations so that later algebraic steps have a clear meaning. You will calculate with signed numbers, fractions, decimals, ratios, percentages, powers, and roots; choose an order of operations; and explain the number properties behind shortcuts.

Work through one lesson at a time. Attempt each question before reading Section 6. In this course, \(=\) means equal value, \(\approx\) means approximately equal, and multiplication is written \(\times\), \(\cdot\), or with adjacent parentheses. Fractions require a nonzero denominator. Unless stated otherwise, calculations and graphs use real numbers.

**Prerequisite check**

- **P1.** Calculate \(7+8\).
- **P2.** Calculate \(6\times4\) and \(24\div6\).
- **P3.** A whole is cut into four equal pieces. What fraction is one piece?

## 2. Guided lessons

### Lesson 1: Signed numbers and real-number properties

Integers are whole numbers, their negatives, and zero. On a number line, positive numbers lie right of zero and negative numbers left. Addition describes movement; subtracting a number adds its opposite: \(a-b=a+(-b)\). The absolute value \(|a|\) is its distance from zero, so \(|-5|=5\).

**Worked example.** A balance of \(-6\) dollars receives \(10\) dollars and then pays \(3\): \(-6+10-3=4-3=1\) dollar. For multiplication, distributing gives \(0=(-3)(2+(-2))=-6+(-3)(-2)\), so \((-3)(-2)=6\). A negative times a negative must be positive to preserve distribution. Division undoes multiplication, giving the same sign rules. Division by zero is undefined: no number multiplied by zero can give a nonzero dividend, and \(0/0\) has no unique answer.

The **commutative** property changes order in addition or multiplication; the **associative** property changes grouping. Zero is the additive identity; one is the multiplicative identity. Opposites sum to zero, and nonzero reciprocals multiply to one. The **distributive** property, \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\), connects the two operations. Real numbers include fractions and decimals as well as irrational numbers such as \(\sqrt2\), which cannot be written as a ratio of integers.

**Avoid this misconception:** subtraction and division are not commutative: \(8-3\ne3-8\).

**Practice L1.** Calculate (a) \(-4-(-7)\); (b) \((-18)\div3\). Name the property in \(5(2+4)=10+20\).

### Lesson 2: Fractions and decimals

A fraction \(a/b\) means \(a\div b\). Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number preserves value because their ratio is one. This explains equivalent fractions and common denominators. Add equal-sized parts; multiply fractions by multiplying numerators and denominators. Dividing by a nonzero fraction multiplies by its reciprocal because their product is one. A decimal uses powers of ten as place values: \(0.75=75/100=3/4\).

**Worked example.** Evaluate the outline's expression:

\[
\left(\frac34+\frac23\right)12
=\left(\frac9{12}+\frac8{12}\right)12
=\frac{17}{12}\cdot12=17.
\]

The common denominator makes the pieces comparable; multiplication by 12 then cancels the denominator. For division, \((3/4)\div(2/3)=(3/4)(3/2)=9/8\).

**Avoid this misconception:** \(1/2+1/3\) is \(5/6\), not \(2/5\); denominators name the size of the parts.

**Practice L2.** Calculate (a) \(5/6-1/4\); (b) \(0.6\div0.2\).

### Lesson 3: Ratios, proportions, and percentages

A **ratio** compares quantities by division; a **rate** compares quantities with units, such as dollars per ticket. A **proportion** states that two ratios are equal. Scaling both quantities by the same factor preserves their ratio. A percentage is a ratio per hundred: \(15\%=0.15\). A percentage change is measured against the original amount.

**Worked example.** For a school event, each ticket costs $8. Five tickets cost $40, so 12 tickets cost \(12(8)=96\) dollars. Equivalently, \(40/5=c/12\), where \(c\) is the cost in dollars; multiplying both sides by 12 gives \(c=96\). A separate 15% discount on a $40 supply order saves \(0.15(40)=6\) dollars, leaving $34. The ticket price remains $8.

**Avoid this misconception:** a 20% decrease followed by a 20% increase does not restore the original: \(100(0.8)(1.2)=96\), because the bases differ.

**Practice L3.** (a) Three kilograms of seed cost $12. At the same rate, what do 5 kg cost? (b) Increase 50 by 10%.

### Lesson 4: Powers, roots, and order of operations

For a positive integer \(n\), \(a^n\) is a product of \(n\) factors of \(a\); \(a\) is the base and \(n\) the exponent. Repeated multiplication explains
\(a^ma^n=a^{m+n}\), \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\), and \((ab)^n=a^nb^n\). For \(a\ne0\), cancellation gives \(a^m/a^n=a^{m-n}\), \(a^0=1\), and \(a^{-n}=1/a^n\). We will extend exponents in [Stage 7](stage7_rational_expressions_and_radicals.md).

The square root \(\sqrt a\) is the nonnegative number whose square is \(a\), for \(a\ge0\). The cube root \(\sqrt[3]a\) is the real number whose cube is \(a\), including negative inputs. Evaluate grouping symbols first, then powers and roots, then multiplication/division from left to right, then addition/subtraction from left to right. A fraction bar groups its entire numerator and denominator.

**Worked example.** \(18\div3\times2+\sqrt{16}-2^3=6\times2+4-8=8\). Also, \(2^3\cdot2^2/2^4=2^{3+2-4}=2\), which agrees with \(8\cdot4/16\).

**Avoid this misconception:** \(-3^2=-9\), while \((-3)^2=9\). Parentheses determine whether the sign belongs to the base. Also, \(\sqrt9=3\); the equation \(x^2=9\) has two solutions, a distinction developed in Stage 6.

**Practice L4.** Evaluate (a) \(2+3(4^2-10)\); (b) \(5^3/5^2+\sqrt{25}\).

## 3. Connected application

Our school event has a fixed cost of $120 plus $3 per attendee, and every attendee buys one $8 ticket. At 20 attendees, cost is \(120+3(20)=180\) dollars, revenue is \(8(20)=160\) dollars, and profit (revenue minus cost) is \(160-180=-20\) dollars: a $20 loss. At 30 attendees, cost is $210, revenue $240, and profit $30. Arithmetic lets us compare particular attendance counts. We assume constant prices and no other costs; later units will represent all attendance counts at once.

## 4. Essential summary

Signed numbers represent direction as well as size. Equivalent fractions preserve ratios; common denominators make addition meaningful. Rates attach units to ratios, and percentages use a stated base. Powers abbreviate multiplication; roots reverse powers within stated domains. Number properties explain lawful rearrangement, while order of operations makes notation unambiguous.

## 5. Independent mastery check

- **M1.** Evaluate \(-8+3(-2)-(-5)\).
- **M2.** Calculate \((2/3+1/6)\div(5/4)\).
- **M3.** Explain why \(7(99)=7(100)-7\), and evaluate it.
- **M4.** Error analysis: a learner says \(2^3+2^2=2^5\). Correct the calculation and explain the error.
- **M5.** Evaluate \(3^0+2^{-2}+\sqrt{36}\).
- **M6.** An unfamiliar recipe uses 300 g of flour for 8 portions. How many grams are needed for 12 portions at the same ratio?
- **M7.** For the school event, calculate profit at 40 attendees. What percentage of its $320 revenue is its $120 fixed cost?

**Mastery criteria:** aim for at least 6 of 7 correct independently, including explanations for M3–M4 and units for applications. Revisit Lesson 1 for sign/property errors, Lesson 2 for fractions, Lesson 3 for ratios, or Lesson 4 for powers and operation order. Correct errors and try a changed-number version before moving on.

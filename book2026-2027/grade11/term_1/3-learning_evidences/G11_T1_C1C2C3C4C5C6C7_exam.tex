\LearningObjective
{Identify probability distributions and their supports; use geometric interval probabilities and exponential waiting times; calculate theoretical moments, empirical probabilities, and sample statistics; and interpret errors relative to theoretical models in economic and operational contexts.}
{C1. Characterises discrete and continuous random variables, identifies suitable distributions and supports, and interprets density and cumulative probability in context.\par
C2. Applies uniform and triangular models and constant or piecewise-linear densities to interval probabilities using geometric areas.\par
C3. Uses circular geometry and arc proportions to calculate probabilities for uniform timing and directions around a circle.\par
C4. Applies exponential models to constant-rate waiting times, infers event rates, and assesses model suitability.\par
C5. Calculates and interprets the mean, variance, and standard deviation of continuous distributions using standard formulas.\par
C6. Estimates interval probabilities using sample relative frequencies and compares them with theoretical probabilities.\par
C7. Calculates sample means, variances, and standard deviations for different sample sizes and data representations and compares them with theory.}

\SubsectionBox{1. Models in organisations \score{C1-\sml{5}}}
Colombian organisations model the following uncertain quantities.
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{(C1)}] For each context, define or characterise the random variable and its possible values (support), classify it as discrete or continuous, and identify its distribution. Each context is worth 0.5 points. \score{5}
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Context 1.} A cooperative records whether one randomly selected member repays a monthly instalment on time. The probability of timely payment is 0.92; code timely payment as 1 and otherwise as 0.

\textbf{Context 2.} A warehouse models a scale's measurement error between $-3$ and $3$ grams, with equal-length intervals having equal probabilities and no errors outside these limits.

\textbf{Context 3.} A bookseller contacts 40 customers. Each independently orders a new title with the same probability 0.15. The bookseller counts the customers who order.

\textbf{Context 4.} A transport cooperative counts roadside assistance calls in one hour. Calls occur independently, one at a time, at a constant rate of 2 per hour; multiple calls in a sufficiently short interval are negligible.

\textbf{Context 5.} A printer models the completion time of a poster order on $[10,30]$ minutes. The density rises linearly from zero at 10 to a single peak at 18, then falls linearly to zero at 30; it is zero elsewhere.

\textbf{Context 6.} An auditor checks invoices independently until finding the first incorrect one. Each invoice has the same probability 0.08 of being incorrect. Count all invoices checked, including the first incorrect invoice.

\textbf{Context 7.} A cooperative models the fraction of a storage tank used at dispatch on $[0,1]$. Its density increases along one straight line from zero at 0 to its maximum at 1 and is zero outside the interval.

\textbf{Context 8.} A seed supplier checks 25 independently selected packets. Each packet has the same probability 0.04 of a broken seal. The supplier counts packets with broken seals.

\textbf{Context 9.} A roaster assumes bag mass follows the exact bell-curve model on the real line, with mean 500 grams and standard deviation 4 grams.

\textbf{Context 10.} A digital shop counts refund requests over a three-day period. Requests occur independently at a constant rate of 5 per day, one at a time; multiple requests in a sufficiently short interval are negligible.

\SubsectionBox{2. Dispatch delays \score{C2-\sml{3}}}
A Medellín supplier models the additional dispatch delay $D$ in minutes on $[0,12]$. Its density is a single decreasing straight line, zero outside this interval. The density height at 0 is three times the density height at 12. This is a ratio of density heights, not of probabilities at individual points.
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{(C2)}] Infer the normalising endpoint heights and use geometric areas to find $P(3\le D\le9)$. \score{3}
\end{itemize}

\SubsectionBox{Sample statistics and errors: C7}
For Questions 19--22, calculate the sample mean, sample variance, and sample standard deviation. Always use the variance denominator $n-1$. The theoretical standard deviation is the square root of the stated theoretical variance.

For \textbf{each of the three statistics}, calculate absolute error $E=|\text{sample statistic}-\text{theoretical statistic}|$ and percentage error $100E/|\text{theoretical statistic}|$. Use unrounded statistics to calculate errors. Round final statistics and absolute errors to 3 decimals and percentage errors to 2 decimals. For grouped data and histograms, explicitly use \textbf{class-midpoint estimates}.

\section{Models and calculation tools}
\label{sec:tools}
\href{../2-class_activities/normal-table.pdf}{Open the normal and Student's t reference tables.}
Page 1 gives $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$ for positive $z$ on a hundredth grid (through $2.59$).
Page 2 gives positive t critical values for upper-tail probabilities
$0.30,0.20,0.10,0.05,0.025,0.01,0.005$, for df $1$--$30$ and selected larger df.
Those entries do not give arbitrary CDF values. Use a suitable calculator's t CDF and inverse t CDF for this activity;
normal CDF and inverse normal are also allowed. Numerical CDF and quantile results in the solutions are supplementary to the tables.
Never turn table bounds into a single probability or error.

Write $F_\nu(t)=P(T\le t)$ for $T\sim t_\nu$.
Symmetry gives $F_\nu(-c)=1-F_\nu(c)$; a right tail is $1-F_\nu(c)$.
An interval has probability $F_\nu(b)-F_\nu(a)$ and its outside complement has probability
$F_\nu(a)+1-F_\nu(b)$. Replace $F_\nu$ by $\Phi$ for normal calculations.
Endpoints have zero probability. For central area $p$, each tail has $(1-p)/2$;
for combined symmetric tails $q$, each tail has $q/2$.

A contextual location-scale model is explicitly $X=m+aT$, with $a>0$ a scale parameter;
$a$ is not automatically the standard deviation. Thus $T=(X-m)/a$.
These are stipulated models for signed deviations, not claims that arbitrary individual measurements follow t.
For independent random observations from a normal population, with unknown population standard deviation,
the sampling statistic instead is
\[
T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}.
\]
Here $\bar X$ and $S$ vary across samples; do not treat observed $s$ as a fixed population standard deviation.

Attempt each task before checking the worked solution. Calculate with full precision, then report
probabilities to four decimals, standardized quantiles to three decimals, contextual values to two decimals,
and percentage errors to two decimals. Displayed rounded intermediate values are not the inputs used for errors.
Diagrams shade the requested region; curves continue beyond the displayed range.

\textbf{C9.} Compare normal approximations with t probabilities and quantiles, and interpret their errors.
For each comparison define $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $T\sim t_\nu$.
The normal calculation approximates the t calculation. In contextual units use
$X_N=m+aZ$ and $X_T=m+aT$, with exactly the same $m$ and $a$.
Probability comparisons keep the same boundaries and event; inverse comparisons keep the same target probability.
With normal result $r_N$ and nonzero t reference $r_T$, calculate
\[
 A=|r_N-r_T|,\qquad R=\frac{|r_N-r_T|}{|r_T|},\qquad E=100R\%.
\]
For intervals compare each endpoint separately. These errors depend on the quantity compared:
a standardized quantile and a contextual cutoff need not have the same relative error.
Heavier t tails give larger probabilities beyond positive cutoffs and more extreme quantiles at the same tail probability.
As df increases, t approaches normal. An interval entirely on one side of zero need not contain less t probability.

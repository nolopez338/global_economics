\subsection{We choose the appropriate distribution.}
\label{sec:c9-choice}
Assume independent random observations from a normal population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma>0$. These are fixed population parameters; $n$ is sample size, $\bar X$ and $S$ are random sample statistics (mean and standard deviation), and $s$ is the observed value of $S$.
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Population variability} & \text{Standardized sample mean} & \text{Distribution}\\ \hline
\sigma\text{ known} & Z=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} & Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\\[3pt]
\sigma\text{ unknown, estimated by }S & T=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} & T\sim t_\nu,\quad\nu=n-1
\end{array}
\]
The denominators are the standard error of $\bar X$ and its estimate. Both statistics have no units. $\mathcal N(0,1)$ is standard normal (mean 0, standard deviation 1); $t_\nu$ is Student's t-distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom (df), requiring $n\ge2$. Both are continuous and symmetric about zero, with total probability 1; t need not have standard deviation 1.

Estimating $\sigma$ adds uncertainty: t has heavier tails, hence larger positive cutoffs for the same upper-tail probability below $1/2$. As $\nu$ increases, t approaches the standard normal distribution. A large sample, or equal numerical values of $s$ and a known $\sigma$, does not change their roles or the model choice.

\subsection{We connect critical values with probability areas.}
\label{sec:c9-areas}
For $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $T\sim t_\nu$, $\Phi(z)$ is the cumulative probability at a real normal cutoff $z$; $\alpha$ is an upper-tail probability, with positive critical values $z^*_\alpha$ and $t^*_{\alpha,\nu}$:
\[
\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z),\qquad
P(Z>z^*_\alpha)=\alpha,\qquad
P(T>t^*_{\alpha,\nu})=\alpha,\quad 0<\alpha<\tfrac12.
\]
Write $P(\cdot)$ for probability and $\sim$ for follows the distribution. A cutoff $c$ is a fixed boundary (possibly negative); $\pm c$ denotes both $-c$ and $c$, and $|Y|$ is the absolute value of $Y$. For continuous distributions, endpoints do not affect probabilities: $<$ and $\le$ (or $>$ and $\ge$) are interchangeable. $\approx$ means approximate equality.

For $Y=Z$ or $Y=T$ symmetric about zero, $c>0$, and $\alpha=P(Y>c)$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(Y<-c)&=\alpha,& P(Y<c)&=1-\alpha,& P(Y>-c)&=1-\alpha,\\
P(|Y|>c)&=2\alpha,& P(-c<Y<c)&=1-2\alpha.&
\end{aligned}
\]
A combined two-tail area $q$ uses $\alpha=q/2$; a central area $p$ uses $\alpha=(1-p)/2$, where $p,q$ are probabilities. The symmetric cutoffs are $\pm c$. For any real endpoints $a<b$,
\[
P(a<Y<b)=1-P(Y\le a)-P(Y\ge b),\qquad
P(Y>c)=1-P(Y\le c).
\]

For normal probabilities, symmetry and cumulative differences give
\[
\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z),\qquad P(Z>z)=1-\Phi(z),\qquad
P(a<Z<b)=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a).
\]

\subsection{We use the reference tables for this activity.}
\label{sec:c9-tables}
\textbf{Reference page 1: normal cumulative areas.} For $z\ge0$, combine the row (ones and tenths) and column (hundredths) to read $\Phi(z)$, rounded to four decimals; e.g. row $1.2$ and column $0.03$ give $\Phi(1.23)\approx0.8907$. For a critical value, seek cumulative area $1-\alpha$; the hundredth grid does not generally determine a three-decimal cutoff.

\textbf{Reference page 2: t critical values.} Row $\nu$, column $\alpha$, gives $t^*_{\alpha,\nu}$ to three decimals. Columns are $0.30$, $0.20$, $0.10$, $0.05$, $0.025$, $0.01$, $0.005$.
For adjacent upper-tail columns with $\alpha_1>\alpha_2$,
\[
t^*_{\alpha_1,\nu}<c<t^*_{\alpha_2,\nu}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_2<P(T>c)<\alpha_1.
\]
These are probability bounds, not an interpolated estimate. Symmetry, doubling, and complements give other bounds; for example,
$1-2\alpha_1<P(-c<T<c)<1-2\alpha_2$.
Allow for entry rounding if a cutoff is extremely close to a printed value; the between-entry cutoffs here are well separated from that uncertainty.

\textbf{Precision and supplements.} Report critical values to three decimals and normal areas to four decimals; probabilities at rounded cutoffs are approximate. Use the supplements below for missing precision or rows, without rounding a supplied cutoff or substituting nearby df. The normal critical values are also directly available in the t-sheet's limiting row $\nu=\infty$ (infinite df, the normal limit). Numerical inverse cumulative evaluation gives the same rounded values and supplies the missing t rows. Numerical cumulative evaluation gives $\Phi(1.833)\approx0.9666$.

\subsection{We compare areas at the same cutoff.}
\label{sec:c9-fixed}
Compare the two distributions at the fixed standardized cutoff 1.833.
\QuestionsTasks[sec:c9-comparing]{{9}/{sol:c9-fixed}/{Calculate the upper-tail, combined two-tail, and central probabilities for the standard normal distribution and Student's t-distribution with 9 degrees of freedom at symmetric cutoffs of negative 1.833 and positive 1.833. Then give bounds without interpolation for the upper-tail and central probabilities under the same Student's t-distribution at symmetric cutoffs of negative 2.00 and positive 2.00, and compare them with the corresponding normal areas.}}
\Needspace{20\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{C9 Solution}
Using the labeled supplemental normal entry, $\Phi(1.833)\approx0.9666$; for $t_9$, $1.833$ is in column $\alpha=0.05$.
\phantomsection\label{sol:c9-fixed}
Use $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $T\sim t_9$.
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\text{Model} & \text{Upper tail} & \text{Combined tails} & \text{Central area}\\ \hline
Z & 0.0334 & 0.0668 & 0.9332\\
t_9 & 0.0500 & 0.1000 & 0.9000
\end{array}
\]
All areas in the array are approximate: $P(Z>1.833)\approx1-0.9666=0.0334$ and $P(T>1.833)\approx0.0500$. Double each upper tail for the combined tails; subtract that result from one for the central area, using symmetry. At $2.00$, reference row $9$ gives $1.833<2.00<2.262$, hence
\[
0.025<P(T>2.00)<0.05,\qquad
0.90<P(-2.00<T<2.00)<0.95.
\]
For $Z$, the upper tail is $P(Z>2.00)\approx1-0.9772=0.0228$ and the central area is $P(-2.00<Z<2.00)\approx1-2(0.0228)=0.9544$. Thus the $t_9$ upper tail is larger and its central area is smaller even using only these bounds.

\section*{1. Choose a question and obtain data}
State a simple question about a \textbf{continuous variable} and obtain at least \textbf{30 usable numerical observations} from one credible source. Choose data reasonably compatible with your proposed model. Use public, non-sensitive observations, not invented or simulated data or a completed practice problem.

Define $\ConeToneVariable{X}$, its units, context and target population, observation \CThreeTonePeriod{period}, source, final \CsixToneCount{sample} \CsixToneCount{size} $\CsixToneCount{n}$, and how observations were selected. Explain removed observations, missing values, and any transformations (including resulting units). Do not remove observations simply to improve the fit.

\textbf{Data access.} Include a clickable hyperlink to the \textbf{original data source}. If you download a file or create a cleaned dataset, upload the \textbf{exact dataset used} to Google Drive or OneDrive and include its clickable link too. Set viewing access so the teacher can open it without requesting permission. Cite the organization/author, dataset title, date or version where available, and access date.

\textbf{Possible questions} (find and assess a real source before committing):
\begin{itemize}
\item How likely is a shipment's measured mass to exceed a stated handling limit? Look for public logistics or quality-control measurements; consider a normal model if roughly symmetric and bell-shaped.
\item How likely is the wait between service arrivals to exceed a stated time? Look for public arrival timestamps and compute consecutive time gaps within a comparable operating \CThreeTonePeriod{period}; consider an exponential model.
\item How likely is a bounded service duration to exceed a stated target? Look for public operational records with a roughly increasing or decreasing histogram; consider a linear density on a finite interval.
\end{itemize}

\section*{2. Summarise the data}
Calculate the \CsevenToneSampleMean{sample} \CsevenToneSampleMean{mean}, \CsevenToneSampleVariance{sample} \CsevenToneSampleVariance{variance} using $\CsixToneCount{n}\CsevenToneSampleVariance{-1}$, and \CsevenToneSampleSD{sample} \CsevenToneSampleSD{standard} \CsevenToneSampleSD{deviation} from the actual observations:
\[
\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}}=\frac{\ConeToneVariable{1}}{\CsixToneCount{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ConeToneVariable{x_i},\qquad
\CsevenToneSampleVariance{s^2}=\frac{\ConeToneVariable{1}}{\CsixToneCount{n}\CsixToneCount{-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ConeToneVariable{x_i}-\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}})^2,\qquad
\CsevenToneSampleSD{s}=\sqrt{\CsevenToneSampleVariance{s^2}}.
\]
Show formulas, numerical substitutions and intermediate totals such as $\sum \ConeToneVariable{x_i}$ and $\sum(\ConeToneVariable{x_i}-\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}})^2$. Give units (squared units for variance). You need not print every observation in the report. Include \textbf{one histogram} with a title, labelled axes, units and clear class intervals; explain the bins briefly. If overlaying a density, use a density histogram or rescale the curve to frequencies.

\section*{Choose 1 Option: model guidance for Task 3}
These are models for \textbf{observations $\ConeToneVariable{X}$}. No calculus is required. Density has units reciprocal to those of $\ConeToneVariable{X}$; probabilities are areas, not \CtwoToneDensity{density} \CtwoToneDensity{heights}.

\textbf{A. Linear density on a finite interval (C1--C2, C5).}
Use data with a roughly steadily increasing or decreasing histogram over a plausible finite interval. Choose $\ConeToneVariable{a}<\ConeToneVariable{b}$ enclosing all retained observations. Use documented physical bounds if known and suitable; otherwise use $\ConeToneVariable{a}=\min \ConeToneVariable{x_i}$, $\ConeToneVariable{b}=\max \ConeToneVariable{x_i}$ as simple \textbf{estimated endpoints}. Sample extremes are not known population or physical bounds: new observations could fall outside them. State this limitation. Let $\ConeToneVariable{d}=\ConeToneVariable{b}-\ConeToneVariable{a}$ and choose the direction from the histogram:
\[
\CtwoToneDensity{f}(\ConeToneVariable{x})=\begin{cases}\CtwoToneDensity{2}(\ConeToneVariable{x}-\ConeToneVariable{a})/d^2,&\ConeToneVariable{a}\le \ConeToneVariable{x}\le \ConeToneVariable{b},\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
\quad\text{(increasing)},\qquad
\CtwoToneDensity{f}(\ConeToneVariable{x})=\begin{cases}\CtwoToneDensity{2}(\ConeToneVariable{b}-\ConeToneVariable{x})/d^2,&\ConeToneVariable{a}\le \ConeToneVariable{x}\le \ConeToneVariable{b},\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
\quad\text{(decreasing)}.
\]
These simple triangular forms have \ConeToneVariable{support} $[\ConeToneVariable{a},\ConeToneVariable{b}]$ and peak height $\CtwoToneDensity{2}/\ConeToneVariable{d}$. Verify nonnegativity and triangle area $\tfrac12 \ConeToneVariable{d}(2/\ConeToneVariable{d})=\ConeToneVariable{1}$. Their model means are $\ConeToneVariable{a}+2\ConeToneVariable{d}/3$ (increasing) and $\ConeToneVariable{a}+\ConeToneVariable{d}/3$ (decreasing); both have variance $d^2/18$ and SD $\ConeToneVariable{d}/\sqrt{18}$. Compare with your sample statistics; do not force this model onto an incompatible histogram.

\textbf{B. Exponential waiting times (C1, C4--C5).}
Use nonnegative waiting times with many short waits and fewer long waits, where an approximately constant arrival rate and independent successive waits are plausible. Estimate $\CfourToneRate{\hat\lambda}=\CfourToneRate{1}/\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}}$ for $\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}}>0$; the rate has units of inverse time. The fitted density and \ConeToneVariable{support} are
\[
\CtwoToneDensity{f}(\ConeToneVariable{x})=\begin{cases}\CfourToneRate{\hat\lambda} e^{-\hat\lambda x},&\ConeToneVariable{x}\ge\CtwoToneDensity{0},\\0,&\ConeToneVariable{x}<0,\end{cases}
\qquad \ConeToneVariable{X}\in[0,\infty).
\]
The model mean and SD are both $1/\CfourToneRate{\hat\lambda}$, and variance is $1/\CfourToneRate{\hat\lambda^2}$. Check whether $\CsevenToneSampleSD{s}$ is reasonably close to $\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}}$ and discuss changing rates or dependent waits if relevant.

\textbf{C. Normal measurements (C1, C5; normal methods from C9).}
Use roughly symmetric, unimodal, bell-shaped measurements without severe outliers. Set $\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu}=\CsevenToneSampleMean{\bar{x}}$ and $\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma}=\CsevenToneSampleSD{s}>\CfiveTonePopulationSD{0}$ as simple estimates, both in the units of $\ConeToneVariable{X}$. Write $\ConeToneVariable{X}\sim N(\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu},\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma^2})$ with
\[
\CtwoToneDensity{f}(\ConeToneVariable{x})=\frac{\ConeToneVariable{1}}{\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma}\sqrt{\CfiveTonePopulationVariance{2}\pi}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\ConeToneVariable{x}-\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu})^2}{2\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma^2}}\right],
\qquad -\infty<\ConeToneVariable{x}<\infty.
\]
The model mean, variance and SD are $\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu}$, $\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma^2}$ and $\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma}$. For physically bounded measurements, discuss whether the normal model gives appreciable probability to impossible values. An unknown population SD does \textbf{not} make raw observations follow Student's $\ConeToneVariable{t}$. Here $\CsevenToneSampleSD{s}$ describes observation spread; $\CsevenToneSampleSD{s}/\sqrt \CsixToneCount{n}$ is the \CeightToneSE{standard} \CeightToneSE{error} of a \CsevenToneSampleMean{sample} \CsevenToneSampleMean{mean}. No $\ConeToneVariable{t}$ inference is needed in this investigation.

\section*{4. Calculate quantiles and model probabilities}
A \textbf{quantile} $\ConeToneVariable{q_p}$ is a cutoff with $\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{X}\le \ConeToneVariable{q_p})=\ConeToneProbability{p}$ under your fitted continuous model. Find the \textbf{numerical values and units} of $\ConeToneVariable{q_{0.10}}$ and $\ConeToneVariable{q_{0.90}}$. Show parameter substitutions and the steps used to obtain them:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Linear:} use triangle areas. For $\ConeToneVariable{a}\le \ConeToneVariable{x}\le \ConeToneVariable{b}$, the area to the left is $(\ConeToneVariable{x}-\ConeToneVariable{a})^2/d^2$ (increasing) or $1-(\ConeToneVariable{b}-\ConeToneVariable{x})^2/d^2$ (decreasing). Equating it to $\ConeToneProbability{p}$ gives $\ConeToneVariable{q_p}=\ConeToneVariable{a}+\ConeToneVariable{d}\sqrt \ConeToneProbability{p}$ or $\ConeToneVariable{q_p}=\ConeToneVariable{b}-\ConeToneVariable{d}\sqrt{1-\ConeToneProbability{p}}$, respectively.
\item \textbf{Exponential:} use $\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{X}\le \ConeToneVariable{x})=\ConeToneProbability{1}-e^{-\hat\lambda x}$ for $\ConeToneVariable{x}\ge0$, so $\ConeToneVariable{q_p}=-\ln(\ConeToneVariable{1}-\ConeToneProbability{p})/\CfourToneRate{\hat\lambda}$.
\item \textbf{Normal:} standardise with $\ConeToneVariable{Z}=(\ConeToneVariable{X}-\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu})/\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma}$. Use normal tables or a calculator to find $\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{Z}\le \CeightToneCritical{z_p})=\ConeToneProbability{p}$, then $\ConeToneVariable{q_p}=\CfiveTonePopulationMean{\hat\mu}+\CfiveTonePopulationSD{\hat\sigma} \CeightToneCritical{z_p}$; $\CeightToneCritical{z_{0.10}}\approx\CeightToneCritical{-1.2816}$, $\CeightToneCritical{z_{0.90}}\approx\CeightToneCritical{1.2816}$.
\end{itemize}
Calculate the three events
\[
\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{X}<\ConeToneVariable{q_{0.10}})=\ConeToneProbability{0.10},\qquad
\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{q_{0.10}}\le \ConeToneVariable{X}\le \ConeToneVariable{q_{0.90}})=\ConeToneProbability{0.80},\qquad
\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{X}>\ConeToneVariable{q_{0.90}})=\ConeToneProbability{0.10}.
\]
These probabilities hold \textbf{by construction}; you must still show how the numerical cutoffs were obtained. Single endpoints have probability zero in a continuous model.

Choose one additional \textbf{meaningful numerical threshold $\ConeToneVariable{c}$} from your question, such as an operational limit, and calculate \textbf{$\ConeToneProbability{P}(\ConeToneVariable{X}>\ConeToneVariable{c})$}. Explain its meaning; do not choose $\ConeToneVariable{c}$ simply as another model quantile. Use geometric areas, $e^{-\hat\lambda c}$ for exponential $\ConeToneVariable{c}\ge0$, or normal standardisation and tables/calculator. Respect the \ConeToneVariable{support} when a threshold lies outside it. Keep sufficient precision in cutoffs and state the numerical boundaries used for comparison.

\section*{5. Compare empirical and model probabilities}
For \textbf{all four events in Task 4}, use \textbf{exactly the same numerical boundaries and inequalities} in the dataset and fitted model. Count from observations, not bins crossing the boundaries. If you round cutoffs before counting, evaluate the model at those same cutoffs; its quantile probabilities may then be only approximately $0.10$, $0.80$, $0.10$.

Count the observations satisfying each event and calculate
\[
\CsixToneEmpirical{p_{\mathrm{empirical}}}=\frac{\text{event count}}{\CsixToneCount{n}},\qquad
\CsixToneAbsoluteError{E_A}=|\CsixToneEmpirical{p_{\mathrm{empirical}}}-p_{\mathrm{model}}|,\qquad
\CsixToneRelativeError{E_{\%}}=\frac{\ConeToneVariable{100}\CsixToneAbsoluteError{E_A}}{|p_{\mathrm{model}}|}.
\]
\CsixToneRelativeError{Percentage} \CsixToneRelativeError{error} is \textbf{undefined if $p_{\mathrm{model}}=0$}. A probability difference of $\ConeToneProbability{0.03}$ equals \textbf{3 percentage points}, not necessarily $3\%$ \CsixToneRelativeError{relative} \CsixToneRelativeError{error}. The model probability is the reference for \CsixToneRelativeError{percentage} \CsixToneRelativeError{error}.

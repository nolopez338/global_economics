\section{Introduction}\label{sec:c3-introduction}
A variable is \emph{circular} when its values describe positions in a repeating
cycle.  Direction and orientation provide familiar examples: a weather station
may record wind direction, while a person, vehicle, animal, antenna, or sensor
may move or point in a particular direction.  These values can be written as
angles, but their scale does not have two different ends.  On a compass,
$0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$ both mean north.

Repeating times can also be treated as circular variables.  Time of day returns
to its starting point every 24 hours, so 23:59 and 00:01 are only two minutes
apart even though their numerical representations lie on opposite sides of
midnight.  Day of the week and season of the year have the same repeating
feature when only position within the cycle matters.  Rotation and phase give
further examples: after a wheel, machine component, or robot completes one
revolution, it has returned to the same orientation, and a periodic signal
returns to the same phase after one complete cycle.

A \emph{circular distribution} is a probability distribution for such a
variable.  It is not a density whose graph happens to be a circular arc.
Circular probability models can answer practical questions: How likely is a
direction to fall inside a target sector?  What is the probability of a time
interval that crosses midnight?  What direction best represents several
observations?  Are the observations concentrated near one part of the cycle,
or dispersed around the whole cycle?  The appropriate method respects the fact
that the beginning and end of the numerical scale represent the same physical
point.

Writing a circle as $[0,2\pi)$ or $[0^\circ,360^\circ)$ requires choosing a
\emph{cut}: a numerical boundary at which one cycle is written as ending and
the next as beginning.  The underlying direction or cycle has no physical
break there.  For example, $1^\circ$ and $359^\circ$ are only $2^\circ$ apart
across north, although they look far apart on a number line.  Their ordinary
arithmetic mean is $180^\circ$, which points in the opposite direction.  This
boundary problem motivates representing observations by unit vectors and using
a circular mean rather than an ordinary linear average.

If $\Theta$ is measured in radians, then
\[
 \Theta\equiv\Theta+2\pi.
\]
This equivalence says that adding a complete revolution does not change the
physical value.  Consequently a circular probability density function (pdf)
is periodic:
\[
 f(\theta)=f(\theta+2\pi),
 \qquad
 \int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)\,d\theta=1.
 \tag{1}
\]
As for an ordinary continuous distribution, probability is area under the
density:
\[
 P(a\leq\Theta\leq b)=\int_a^b f(\theta)\,d\theta.
 \tag{2}
\]
If an interval crosses the chosen cut at $0$, split it at the end of the written
cycle.  For example, on $[0,2\pi)$,
\[
 P\left(\frac{11\pi}{6}\leq\Theta\leq\frac{\pi}{6}\right)
 =\int_{11\pi/6}^{2\pi}f(\theta)\,d\theta
  +\int_0^{\pi/6}f(\theta)\,d\theta.
 \tag{3}
\]
This notation means the short arc travelled through $0$, not the empty
ordinary linear interval.

The simplest circular probability model is the \emph{circular uniform
distribution}:
\[
 f(\theta)=\frac1{2\pi},\qquad 0\leq\theta<2\pi.
 \tag{4}
\]
This model is suitable only when the situation gives no direction, time, or
other part of the cycle a preference.  It is often useful as a baseline, or as
an assumption built into a randomizing procedure; it is not the default model
for all circular data.  Under this assumption every equal-width arc is equally
likely, so an arc of angular width $L$ has probability $L/(2\pi)$.  Thus
\[
 P\left(0\leq\Theta\leq\frac\pi2\right)
 =\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac1{2\pi}\,d\theta=\frac14.
\]

Many real circular observations do have a preferred direction or time.  A
concentrated model such as the von Mises distribution can represent values
clustered around one direction, while wrapped distributions provide other
possibilities.  Model selection should depend on how the data were generated
and on evidence about their spread.

To solve the averaging problem illustrated by $1^\circ$ and $359^\circ$,
represent each $\theta_i$ by the unit vector
$(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)$.  Average the vector components and then find the
direction of the average vector:
\[
 \bar C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\cos\theta_i,
 \qquad
 \bar S=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sin\theta_i,
 \qquad
 \bar\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\bar S,\bar C).
 \tag{5}
\]
If $\operatorname{atan2}$ returns a negative angle, add $2\pi$ (or
$360^\circ$) to report it on the chosen cycle.  The mean resultant length
$R=\sqrt{\bar C^2+\bar S^2}$ indicates concentration: values near $1$ mean
the observations point in similar directions, while values near $0$ mean
there is little or no single representative direction.

Therefore, ``circular data,'' ``circular uniform distribution,'' and
``circular mean'' are not interchangeable ideas.  Circular data describe the
type of variable; the circular uniform and von Mises distributions are
different probability models for that type of variable; and the circular mean
is a method for finding a representative direction.  This activity
concentrates on the circular uniform model and circular means while retaining
that distinction.

\subsection{A quarter-circle and periodicity}\label{sec:c3-uniform-basics}
A directional sensor performs a calibration cycle by selecting an initial
antenna orientation $\Theta$, measured in radians on $[0,2\pi)$.  Orientation
is circular because $0$ and $2\pi$ describe the same physical direction; one
complete revolution returns the antenna to where it started.

The calibration mechanism is designed to have no preferred direction, so the
selected orientation is modelled as uniformly distributed around the circle.
Engineers use the sector from $\pi/3$ to $5\pi/6$ to test reception across a
specified part of the antenna's range.  They want the expected proportion of
calibration orientations in that sector and need to confirm that describing
the sector one full revolution later does not create a different physical
event.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-uniform-basics-a}{3.1}{Verify that the density in (4) has total probability $1$.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-uniform-basics-b}{3.2}{Find $P(\pi/3\leq\Theta\leq5\pi/6)$ and explain why adding $2\pi$ to both endpoints does not change it.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Verify normalization.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-uniform-basics-a}
The density is nonnegative and
\[
 \int_0^{2\pi}\frac1{2\pi}\,d\theta
 =\frac{2\pi}{2\pi}=1.
\]
It therefore satisfies both requirements for a pdf on one complete cycle.
For the calibration procedure, this confirms that the model assigns total
probability $1$ across all possible initial antenna orientations.
\secInicio[sec:c3-uniform][sec:c3-uniform-basics]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Find and interpret the probability.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-uniform-basics-b}
The angular width is $5\pi/6-\pi/3=\pi/2$.  Therefore
\[
 P\left(\frac\pi3\leq\Theta\leq\frac{5\pi}6\right)
 =\frac{\pi/2}{2\pi}=\frac14.
\]
The endpoints $\pi/3+2\pi$ and $5\pi/6+2\pi$ describe the same directions
one revolution later.  Periodicity, $f(\theta)=f(\theta+2\pi)$, makes the
probability unchanged.  Thus the engineers expect one quarter of calibration
orientations to fall in the target reception sector.
\secInicio[sec:c3-uniform][sec:c3-uniform-basics]

\subsection{An interval that crosses zero}\label{sec:c3-uniform-wrap}
A weather station records the direction $\Theta$, in degrees, from which the
wind arrives.  Wind direction is circular: north can be written as either
$0^\circ$ or $360^\circ$, and those labels identify the same direction.  The
station team is evaluating how often wind enters a monitoring sector within
$30^\circ$ of north because a north-facing installation is most exposed to
winds from that sector.

As an initial planning baseline, the team assumes that there is no preferred
wind direction during the period, so $\Theta$ is modelled by a circular uniform
distribution.  This is a simplifying assumption, not a claim that real wind
directions are always uniform.  The target is one continuous $60^\circ$ sector
centred on north, but it crosses the artificial $0^\circ/360^\circ$ cut in the
chosen numerical scale.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-uniform-wrap-a}{3.1}{Write the event as two intervals on $[0^\circ,360^\circ)$.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-uniform-wrap-b}{3.2}{Calculate the probability and express it as a percentage.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Split the wrapped interval.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-uniform-wrap-a}
Directions within $30^\circ$ of north run from $330^\circ$ to $360^\circ$
and from $0^\circ$ to $30^\circ$.  Thus the event is
\[
 \{330^\circ\leq\Theta<360^\circ\}\ \cup\
 \{0^\circ\leq\Theta\leq30^\circ\}.
\]
The two pieces are disjoint on the chosen representation of the cycle.  Taken
together, they describe the installation's one continuous north-facing
monitoring sector.
\secInicio[sec:c3-uniform][sec:c3-uniform-wrap]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Calculate the probability.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-uniform-wrap-b}
The two arcs have total width $30^\circ+30^\circ=60^\circ$.  Hence
\[
 P=\frac{60^\circ}{360^\circ}=\frac16\approx0.1667.
\]
There is therefore a probability of about $16.67\%$.  Treating
$330^\circ\leq\Theta\leq30^\circ$ as an ordinary linear interval would miss
the circular meaning of the event.  Under the uniform baseline, the team would
expect about $16.67\%$ of recorded winds to enter the sector relevant to the
north-facing installation.
\secInicio[sec:c3-uniform][sec:c3-uniform-wrap]

\subsection{Directions near the cycle boundary}\label{sec:c3-means-boundary}
Two delivery riders leave the same depot on routes with initial bearings
$10^\circ$ and $350^\circ$, measured in degrees clockwise from north.  A
bearing is circular because $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$ are the same direction.
Both riders therefore start close to north, but on opposite sides of the cut in
the numerical scale.

A dispatcher wants a representative initial direction in order to position a
support vehicle on the side of the depot most useful to both routes.  The two
riders are given equal weight.  No uniform probability distribution is assumed:
the circular mean describes their two observed bearings, and the resultant
length measures how closely those bearings agree.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-means-boundary-a}{3.1}{Explain why the arithmetic mean is inappropriate.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-means-boundary-b}{3.2}{Use unit vectors to find the circular mean and resultant length.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Compare the directions.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-means-boundary-a}
The arithmetic mean is $(10^\circ+350^\circ)/2=180^\circ$, which points
south.  Both actual directions are only $10^\circ$ from north, so this answer
is an artefact of cutting the circular scale at $0^\circ$.  Positioning the
support vehicle south of the depot on this basis would contradict both routes.
\secInicio[sec:c3-means][sec:c3-means-boundary]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Calculate the circular mean.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-means-boundary-b}
Using $350^\circ=-10^\circ$ on the circle,
\[
 \bar C=\frac{\cos10^\circ+\cos(-10^\circ)}2=\cos10^\circ,
 \qquad
 \bar S=\frac{\sin10^\circ+\sin(-10^\circ)}2=0.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \bar\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(0,\cos10^\circ)=0^\circ,
 \qquad R=\cos10^\circ\approx0.9848.
\]
The circular mean correctly points north, and $R$ close to $1$ shows strong
agreement between the two directions.  The shared northward tendency supports
positioning the dispatch support point north of the depot, where it can serve
both riders' initial routes.
\secInicio[sec:c3-means][sec:c3-means-boundary]

\subsection{A mean with weak concentration}\label{sec:c3-means-concentration}
A shop records three recurring customer-activity times on a 24-hour clock:
$0^\circ$ corresponds to midnight, $120^\circ$ to 08:00, and $240^\circ$ to
16:00.  Time of day is circular because the clock returns to midnight after 24
hours; the end and beginning of the numerical scale are the same time in the
daily cycle.

The manager wants to know whether one representative time could guide a daily
staffing period.  The three observations are given equal weight and are equally
spaced around the 24-hour cycle.  No uniform distribution is being imposed;
the circular mean and resultant length are used to test whether these observed
times have a meaningful common centre.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-means-concentration-a}{3.1}{Calculate $\bar C$ and $\bar S$.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-means-concentration-b}{3.2}{Decide whether a single average direction is meaningful.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Average the vectors.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-means-concentration-a}
The three unit vectors are equally spaced, and
\[
 \bar C=\frac{1-\frac12-\frac12}{3}=0,
 \qquad
 \bar S=\frac{0+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{3}=0.
\]
Thus the average vector is the zero vector and $R=0$.  For the manager, this
shows complete balance among midnight, 08:00, and 16:00 rather than a cluster
near one staffing time.
\secInicio[sec:c3-means][sec:c3-means-concentration]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Interpret the result.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-means-concentration-b}
Because $\operatorname{atan2}(0,0)$ has no defined direction, there is no
unique circular mean for these data.  This is informative rather than an
error: the observations are perfectly balanced around the circle, so a single
representative time would be misleading.  The shop should therefore not use
one ``average'' time to plan coverage for this recurring demand pattern.
\secInicio[sec:c3-means][sec:c3-means-concentration]

\subsection{Online orders by time of day}\label{sec:c3-applied-time}
A shop manager is studying an overnight service and staffing window from 22:30
through 01:30.  Let $H$ be the order time in hours on the 24-hour scale
$[0,24)$.  Time of day is circular because it repeats every 24 hours: 23:59 and
00:01 are close in the actual daily cycle even though midnight is the boundary
of this numerical representation.

Before comparing the model with observed order patterns, the manager uses a
circular uniform distribution as an initial baseline.  It assumes that no hour
is preferred and assigns equal probability to equal-length time intervals.
The practical objective is to find the baseline share of orders associated
with the three-hour overnight window; the assumption need not describe the
shop's actual demand.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-applied-time-a}{3.1}{Represent the event without treating midnight as an endpoint of time.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-applied-time-b}{3.2}{Find and interpret its probability.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Represent the event.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-applied-time-a}
On $[0,24)$ hours the event is
\[
 \{22.5\leq H<24\}\ \cup\ \{0\leq H\leq1.5\}.
\]
Equivalently, converting time $h$ to angle by $\theta=2\pi h/24$, it is the
wrapped arc from $15\pi/8$ through $0$ to $\pi/8$.  This representation keeps
the manager's overnight service window continuous across midnight.
\secInicio[sec:c3-applied][sec:c3-applied-time]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Find and interpret the probability.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-applied-time-b}
The event lasts $1.5+1.5=3$ hours.  Uniformity gives
\[
 P(22{:}30\text{ through }01{:}30)=\frac3{24}=\frac18=0.125.
\]
The model assigns $12.5\%$ of orders to that time window.  This result depends
on the uniform-model assumption; real order times may be concentrated at
meal times or other preferred hours.  The manager can therefore use $12.5\%$
as a baseline share when comparing the window with actual order data and
making staffing decisions.
\secInicio[sec:c3-applied][sec:c3-applied-time]

\subsection{Choosing an appropriate model}\label{sec:c3-applied-model}
An analyst is advising a business and an energy installation on three
variables: daily revenue in pesos, wind direction measured at a weather
station, and the time of day at which electricity demand reaches its daily
peak.  Choosing the variable type before choosing a probability distribution
matters because a model must preserve which values are close and whether the
two ends of a numerical scale represent the same point.

Daily revenue lies on a linear scale whose endpoints are not equivalent.  Wind
direction is measured around a compass, where $0^\circ=360^\circ$, and peak
time repeats on a 24-hour clock, where midnight begins a new written cycle but
not a new type of time.  For either circular variable, the analyst must then
use evidence to decide whether observations are spread approximately evenly
around the cycle or concentrated near a preferred direction or time.  That
decision affects forecasts and operational planning; uniformity is only one
possible modelling assumption.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-applied-model-a}{3.1}{Identify which variables are naturally circular and justify the choice.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c3-applied-model-b}{3.2}{Name possible circular models when directions are uniform and when they cluster around one direction.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C3.1 - Identify circular variables.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-applied-model-a}
Wind direction and peak-demand time are circular: $0^\circ=360^\circ$, and
midnight is both the end of one day and the beginning of the next.  Revenue is
linear; its largest and smallest values are not identified with each other.
The shape of a graph alone does not determine whether a variable is circular.
This classification prevents the analyst from treating nearby directions or
times across the chosen boundary as though they were far apart.
\secInicio[sec:c3-applied][sec:c3-applied-model]

\subsubsection*{C3.2 - Choose model families.}\phantomsection\label{subsec:c3-applied-model-b}
The circular uniform distribution is suitable if every direction is equally
likely.  If observations cluster around a preferred direction, a von Mises
model may be suitable; a wrapped normal is another possible model.  Data and
context are needed to choose among them: ``circular distribution'' does not
refer to one universal density.  The analyst should therefore match the model
to actual evidence about dispersion or clustering and to the forecasting or
planning purpose of the analysis.
\secInicio[sec:c3-applied][sec:c3-applied-model]

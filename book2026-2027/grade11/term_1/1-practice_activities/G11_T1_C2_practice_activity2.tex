The central skill in this activity is constructing a continuous probability
model from a situation, rather than merely evaluating a supplied formula.  For
every problem, use the same six-step method: (1) identify the random variable,
its \textbf{support}, breakpoints, endpoint heights, and geometric shape;
(2) use total area $=1$ to determine all unknown heights or constants;
(3) apply the straight-line rule to derive every sloping formula (or note that
no line equation is needed for constant sections); (4) write the complete pdf,
including $0$ outside its support; (5) identify the requested tail shape and
calculate its area; and (6) identify the shapes in the requested interval or
band and calculate their areas, using a complement when helpful.  Thus Steps
1--4 construct the pdf, Step 5 calculates the requested tail, and Step 6
calculates the requested interval or central band.  State each result in
context when useful.  A density is a height, so it may exceed
$1$; it is the \emph{area} that must total $1$.  Endpoints do not affect a
continuous probability, for example $P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X\le b)$.

\subsection{Equation of a line through two points}\label{sec:c2-intro-line-equation}
Two distinct points determine exactly one straight line.  Suppose the known
points are given.  This method will later be used to construct the formulas for
linear and piecewise-linear probability density functions from known points on
their straight segments.  Write the two points as
\[
 (x_1,y_1),\qquad (x_2,y_2).
\]
When $x_1\ne x_2$, the first step is to calculate the slope:
\[
 m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
\]
The slope measures the change in the vertical coordinate for each one-unit
change in the horizontal coordinate.  The subtractions must use the same order
in the numerator and denominator: moving from point $1$ to point $2$ gives
$y_2-y_1$ and $x_2-x_1$.  Reversing both subtractions gives the same slope,
but reversing only one changes its sign incorrectly.

After finding $m$, use either known point in point-slope form.  Using the first
point gives
\[
 y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]
Here $(x,y)$ represents any point on the line.  Expanding and isolating $y$
produces slope-intercept form:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 y-y_1&=mx-mx_1,\\
 y&=mx+(y_1-mx_1)=mx+b.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus, once the slope is known, the intercept can also be calculated directly
from
\[
 b=y_1-mx_1.
\]
Either known point may be used in point-slope form or in the intercept formula;
both must produce the same line equation.

If $x_1=x_2$, the denominator in the slope calculation is zero.  The line is
vertical, its equation is $x=x_1$, and the usual slope-intercept procedure does
not apply.

\textbf{Worked example.} Find the line through
\[
 (2,\tfrac16)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(6,\tfrac13).
\]
These coordinates give
\[
 x_1=2,\quad y_1=\frac16,\qquad
 x_2=6,\quad y_2=\frac13.
\]
First calculate the slope:
\[
 m=\frac{\frac13-\frac16}{6-2}
   =\frac{\frac16}{4}=\frac1{24}.
\]
Substitute the first point and this slope into point-slope form:
\[
 y-\frac16=\frac1{24}(x-2).
\]
Expanding and isolating $y$ gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 y-\frac16&=\frac{x}{24}-\frac2{24},\\
 y&=\frac{x}{24}-\frac1{12}+\frac16
   =\frac{x}{24}+\frac1{12}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Equivalently, the intercept formula gives
\[
 b=y_1-mx_1=\frac16-\frac1{24}(2)
  =\frac16-\frac1{12}=\frac1{12}.
\]
Therefore the final equation is
\[
 \boxed{y=\frac{x}{24}+\frac1{12}}.
\]
Verify both original points.  For $(2,1/6)$,
\[
 \frac2{24}+\frac1{12}=\frac1{12}+\frac1{12}=\frac16,
\]
and for $(6,1/3)$,
\[
 \frac6{24}+\frac1{12}=\frac14+\frac1{12}=\frac13.
\]
Both points satisfy the final equation.  In later sections, the two coordinates
may represent an input value and the corresponding height of a linear density.

\textbf{Algorithm: line through two points.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Identify $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$.
 \item Compute $\displaystyle m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$.
 \item Compute $\displaystyle b=y_1-mx_1$.
 \item Write $\displaystyle y=mx+b$.
 \item Check that both given points satisfy the equation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Uniform distribution}\label{sec:c2-intro-uniform}
A uniform density has one constant height on support $[L,U]$:
\[
 f_X(x)=\begin{cases}h,&L\le x\le U,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
 \qquad (U-L)h=1,\quad h=\frac1{U-L}.
\]
Thus every probability is a rectangle: $P(a\le X\le b)=(b-a)h$ for
$L\le a<b\le U$.

\textbf{Worked example.} A delivery time $T$ is equally plausible from $2$ to
$8$ minutes.  The support is $[2,8]$ and the whole rectangle has width $6$ and
height $h$, so $6h=1$ and $h=1/6$.  For $3\le T\le6$, the relevant rectangle
has width $6-3=3$ and height $1/6$:
\[
 P(3\le T\le6)=A_{\rm rectangle}=3\left(\frac16\right)=\frac12.
\]
There is a $50\%$ chance that delivery takes from $3$ to $6$ minutes.

\subsection{Linear distribution}\label{sec:c2-intro-linear}
A single linear density has $f_X(x)=mx+d$ throughout one support $[L,U]$
(and $0$ outside), with nonnegative endpoint heights
$h_L=f(L)$ and $h_U=f(U)$.  Its whole area is the trapezoid
\[
 1=\frac12(h_L+h_U)(U-L).
\]
An interval $[a,b]$ is also a trapezoid of width $b-a$ and parallel heights
$f(a),f(b)$.  A triangle is only the special case in which one endpoint height
is zero.

\textbf{Worked example.} A shop's daily price adjustment $X$ (in dollars) lies
between $0$ and $4$.  Its likelihood density increases linearly, and the
density at $4$ is four times that at $0$.  Translating the description, let
the endpoint heights be $h$ and $4h$.  The whole trapezoid gives
\[
 1=\tfrac12(h+4h)4=10h,\qquad h=0.1.
\]
The line through $(0,0.1)$ and $(4,0.4)$ is $f_X(x)=0.075x+0.1$ on the
support (and $0$ otherwise).  For $1\le X\le3$, $f(1)=0.175$ and
$f(3)=0.325$.  The required figure is a
trapezoid of width $2$ with those parallel heights:
\[
 P(1\le X\le3)=\tfrac12(0.175+0.325)(2)=0.5.
\]
Both support endpoints have positive density, so this is genuinely linear,
not triangular.

\subsection{Triangular distribution}\label{sec:c2-intro-triangular}
A triangular density has support $[L,U]$, height $0$ at both ends, and mode
$M$ ($L<M<U$) with height $H$.  It rises linearly on $[L,M]$ and falls linearly
on $[M,U]$.  Since the whole figure has base $U-L$,
\[
 1=\tfrac12(U-L)H,\qquad H=\frac2{U-L}.
\]
Heights inside either sloping side are found by proportional similar triangles.

\textbf{Worked example.} A project's cost overrun $C$ (thousand dollars) has
minimum $0$, most likely value $2$, and maximum $6$.  Its support is $[0,6]$,
mode is $2$, and $\tfrac12(6)H=1$, so $H=1/3$.  To find
$P(1\le C\le4)$, subtract two tail triangles.  At $C=1$ the height is $1/6$;
at $C=4$ it is also $1/6$.  The left tail has base $1$, height $1/6$; the
right tail has base $2$, height $1/6$:
\[
 P(1\le C\le4)=1-\tfrac12(1)(\tfrac16)-\tfrac12(2)(\tfrac16)
 =1-\tfrac1{12}-\tfrac16=\tfrac34.
\]
The region crosses the mode, but subtraction avoids splitting it into two
trapezoids.
\COneGraph{0}{6}{(x<=2)*(x/6)+(x>2)*((6-x)/12)}{1}{4}{0,1,2,4,6}{0.4}

\subsection{Piecewise uniform distribution}\label{sec:c2-intro-piecewise-uniform}
Here the density is constant \emph{within} each interval but may jump at a
breakpoint.  Normalisation is the sum of rectangles:
$1=\sum(\text{segment width})(\text{segment height})$.

\textbf{Worked example.} A warehouse waiting time $W$ ranges from $0$ to $5$
minutes.  Waiting times are equally dense within each of two regimes, $0$--$2$
and $2$--$5$, but the later regime has twice the density of the earlier one.
If the first height is $k$, the narrative makes the second $2k$.  A width-$2$,
height-$k$ rectangle plus a width-$3$, height-$2k$ rectangle must have area
$1$: $2k+6k=1$, so $k=1/8$.  Thus the pdf has heights $1/8$ and $1/4$ on
those respective regimes, and is $0$ otherwise.
The event $1\le W\le4$ spans the jump and uses two rectangles:
\[
 P(1\le W\le4)=\underbrace{(2-1)(1/8)}_{A_1}
 +\underbrace{(4-2)(1/4)}_{A_2}=\frac18+\frac12=\frac58.
\]
\CtwoProbGraph{0}{5}{(x<2)*(1/8)+(x>=2)*(1/4)}{1}{4}{0,1,2,4,5}{0.3}
\secInicio[sec:c2-introduction][sec:c2-intro-piecewise-uniform]

\subsection{Piecewise linear distribution}\label{sec:c2-intro-piecewise-linear}
A piecewise-linear density joins straight segments at stated breakpoints.
Read the height at each endpoint and breakpoint, then treat each piece as a
triangle, rectangle, or trapezoid.  Continuity means adjacent pieces meet at
the same height.

\textbf{Worked example.} Demand $D$ ranges from $0$ to $6$.  Its density starts
at zero, rises linearly to its maximum at $2$, stays at that maximum until
$4$, and then falls linearly to zero at $6$.  Calling the maximum $H$
translates the story into a left triangle
(base $2$), rectangle (width $2$), and right triangle (base $2$):
\[
 1=\tfrac12(2)H+(2)H+\tfrac12(2)H=4H,
 \qquad H=\tfrac14.
\]
For $1\le D\le5$, subtract the two congruent tail triangles.  By linearity,
the heights at $1$ and $5$ are $H/2=1/8$:
\[
 P(1\le D\le5)=1-\tfrac12(1)(\tfrac18)-\tfrac12(1)(\tfrac18)=\frac78.
\]
This reusable approach identifies all three geometric pieces and uses
subtraction from total area $1$.
\COneGraph{0}{6}{(x<=2)*(x/8)+(x>2)*(x<=4)*(1/4)+(x>4)*((6-x)/8)}{1}{5}{0,1,2,4,5,6}{0.3}
\secInicio[sec:c2-introduction][sec:c2-intro-piecewise-linear]

A Bogotá fintech releases approved working-capital loans after fraud and
bank-account checks.  The elapsed time can be measured to any fraction of an
hour.  Automatic cases never finish before 2 hours or after 10 hours, and
operational records give no reason to favor one time over another anywhere
between those limits.  A bakery owner needs the money within 7 hours to meet a
supplier's dispatch deadline.  The fintech separately studies the 3--7 hour
band when assigning automated-processing capacity.

\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
\[
 f_T(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac18,&2\le t\le10,\\[2mm]0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-uniform-service-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
The right tail is a rectangle of width $10-7=3$ and height $1/8$:
\[
 P(T>7)=A_{\mathrm{rectangle}}=(10-7)\frac18=\frac38=37.5\%.
\]
Thus the bakery has a $37.5\%$ modeled risk of missing its deadline.

\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-uniform-service-c}
\CtwoSolutionStep{6}{Interval probability}
The central band is a rectangle of width $7-3=4$:
\[
 P(3\le T\le7)=(7-3)\frac18=\frac12.
\]
Hence half of comparable disbursements occupy this capacity-planning band.

\subsection{Coffee auction premium}\label{sec:c2-linear-coffee}
A Bogotá specialty-coffee roaster buys small lots from Colombian producers.
The premium above the reference price can be measured to any fraction of a
thousand pesos per kilogram.  Auction rules keep it between 0 and 8 thousand
pesos.  As the premium grows, outcomes become steadily more common.  Among two
equally narrow price bands, one placed beside the upper limit occurs five times
as often as one placed beside the lower limit.  Premiums above 6 thousand pesos
threaten margins, while the 2--6 thousand peso band can be absorbed without
changing menu prices.

\COneGraph[x]{0}{8}{(x+2)/48}{2}{6}{0,2,6,8}{0.24}
\endgroup

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-a}{2.1}{Construct the complete probability density function of $X$ from the narrative.  Show how total area $1$ determines every unknown value, give the required formula, and include $0$ outside the possible range.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-b}{2.2}{Calculate the margin-risk tail probability $P(X>6)$ geometrically.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-c}{2.3}{Calculate the central-band probability $P(2\le X\le6)$ geometrically.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C2.1 - Construct the pdf from the situation.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-a}
\CtwoSolutionStep{1}{Support and shape}
The auction bounds give support $[0,8]$.  ``Increases at a constant rate''
gives one straight line, and the endpoint relationship gives heights $h$ and
$5h$, both positive.

\CtwoSolutionStep{2}{Total area \(=1\)}
The full trapezoid must have area $1$:
\[
 1=\frac12(8)(h+5h)=24h,\qquad h=\frac1{24},\quad5h=\frac5{24}.
\]
\CtwoSolutionStep{3}{Straight-line rule}
The line through $(0,1/24)$ and $(8,5/24)$ has slope
$(5/24-1/24)/8=1/48$ and intercept $1/24=2/48$.  Hence
\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
\[
 f_X(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x+2}{48},&0\le x\le8,\\[2mm]0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
Both endpoint heights are positive, so the model is linear rather than
triangular, and its trapezoidal area is $1$.

\subsubsection*{C2.2 - Calculate the tail probability $P(X>6)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
The heights are $f_X(6)=1/6$ and $f_X(8)=5/24$.  The right tail is a
width-$2$ trapezoid:
\[
 P(X>6)=\frac12(8-6)\left(\frac16+\frac5{24}\right)=\frac38.
\]
Thus $37.5\%$ of modeled auctions exceed the manager's margin threshold.

\subsubsection*{C2.3 - Calculate the interval probability $P(2\le X\le6)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-linear-coffee-c}
\CtwoSolutionStep{6}{Interval probability}
Since $f_X(2)=1/12$ and $f_X(6)=1/6$, the interval is a nonconstant
trapezoid of width $4$:
\[
 P(2\le X\le6)=\frac12(6-2)\left(\frac1{12}+\frac16\right)=\frac12.
\]
Thus the absorbable premium band occurs in half of the modeled auctions.

\subsection{Solar microgrid surplus}\label{sec:c2-linear-microgrid}
A rural solar microgrid records the energy left after households and workshops

 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-uniform-refund-b}{2.2}{Calculate the service-credit tail probability $P(R>12)$ geometrically.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-uniform-refund-c}{2.3}{Calculate the central-band probability $P(3\le R\le11)$ geometrically.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C2.1 - Construct the pdf from the situation.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-uniform-refund-a}
\CtwoSolutionStep{1}{Support and shape}
Let $R$ denote refund time in days.  The breakpoints are $5$ and $9$.  Taking
the reviewed-case height as $k$, the quick- and disputed-case heights are $2k$
and $3k$.

\CtwoSolutionStep{2}{Total area \(=1\)}
Normalisation of the three rectangles gives
\[
 1=5(2k)+(9-5)k+(15-9)(3k)=32k,\qquad k=\frac1{32}.
\]
\CtwoSolutionStep{3}{Straight-line rule}
The density is constant on each relevant section, so no line equation is required.

Therefore
\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
\[
 f_R(r)=\begin{cases}
 \dfrac1{16},&0\le r<5,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac1{32},&5\le r<9,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac3{32},&9\le r\le15,\\[1mm]
 0,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
\]

\subsubsection*{C2.2 - Calculate the tail probability $P(R>12)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-uniform-refund-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
The service-credit tail is a width-$3$, height-$3/32$ rectangle:
\[
 P(R>12)=(15-12)\frac3{32}=\frac9{32}=28.125\%.
\]

\subsubsection*{C2.3 - Calculate the central-band probability
$P(3\le R\le11)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-uniform-refund-c}
\CtwoSolutionStep{6}{Interval probability}
Splitting at both changes gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P(3\le R\le11)
 &=(5-3)\frac1{16}+(9-5)\frac1{32}+(11-9)\frac3{32}\\
 &=\frac4{32}+\frac4{32}+\frac6{32}=\frac7{16}=43.75\%.
 \end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Weekly payout liquidity}\label{sec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity}
A Bogotá marketplace holds cash for Friday seller payouts, refunds, and
chargebacks.  Required liquidity may be any measured amount from 0 to 10
billion pesos.  Occurrence begins at none when no cash is required,
becomes steadily more common up to its highest level at 2 billion pesos, and
remains equally common through 5 billion pesos.  It then becomes steadily less
common until requirements at 7 billion pesos occur half as often as at the
highest level; from there it again becomes steadily less common until there is
no occurrence at 10 billion pesos.  Requirements above 6 billion
pesos trigger an expensive backup credit line.  The 1--8 billion peso band is
used to assess how often the standard treasury plan covers the week.

\begingroup\pgfplotsset{every axis/.append style={yticklabels=\empty}}
\COneGraph[d]{0}{10}{(x<=2)*(2*x/25)+(x>2)*(x<=5)*(4/25)+(x>5)*(x<=7)*((9-x)/25)+(x>7)*(2*(10-x)/75)}{1}{8}{0,1,2,5,6,7,8,10}{0.20}
\endgroup

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-a}{2.1}{Construct the complete probability density function of $D$ from the narrative.  Show how total area $1$ determines every unknown value, give all required formulas, and include $0$ outside the possible range.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-b}{2.2}{Calculate the backup-credit tail probability $P(D>6)$ geometrically.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-c}{2.3}{Calculate the central-band probability $P(1\le D\le8)$ geometrically.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C2.1 - Construct the pdf from the situation.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-a}
\CtwoSolutionStep{1}{Support and shape}
The story gives support $[0,10]$ and breakpoints $2,5,7$.  If the maximum is
$H$, the corresponding heights are $0,H,H,H/2,0$.  Thus the geometry is a
base-$2$ triangle, width-$3$ rectangle, width-$2$ trapezoid, and base-$3$
triangle.

\CtwoSolutionStep{2}{Total area \(=1\)}
Normalisation gives
\[
 1=\frac12(2)H+3H+\frac12(2)\left(H+\frac H2\right)
   +\frac12(3)\frac H2=\frac{25}{4}H,\qquad H=\frac4{25}.
\]
\CtwoSolutionStep{3}{Straight-line rule}
Deriving each line from its two endpoint values gives slopes $2/25$, $0$,
$-1/25$, and $-2/75$, respectively.  Therefore
\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
\[
 f_D(d)=\begin{cases}
 \dfrac{2d}{25},&0\le d\le2,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac4{25},&2<d\le5,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{9-d}{25},&5<d\le7,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{2(10-d)}{75},&7<d\le10,\\[1mm]
 0,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
\]
Adjacent equations agree at every breakpoint, and the geometric areas total
$1$.

\subsubsection*{C2.2 - Calculate the tail probability $P(D>6)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
The tail begins within the declining $5$--$7$ segment.  Its heights at $6$ and
$7$ are $3/25$ and $2/25$.  From $6$ to $7$ is a trapezoid; from $7$ to $10$
is a triangle:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P(D>6)
 &=\underbrace{\frac12(7-6)\left(\frac3{25}+\frac2{25}\right)}_{\text{trapezoid}}
 +\underbrace{\frac12(10-7)\frac2{25}}_{\text{triangle}}\\
 &=\frac1{10}+\frac3{25}=\frac{11}{50}=22\%.
 \end{aligned}
\]
This is the modeled frequency with which backup credit is triggered.

\subsubsection*{C2.3 - Calculate the interval probability $P(1\le D\le8)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-liquidity-c}
\CtwoSolutionStep{6}{Interval probability}
Although the interval spans every structural piece, its complement consists
of two simple tail triangles.  Since $f_D(1)=2/25$ and $f_D(8)=4/75$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P(1\le D\le8)
 &=1-\underbrace{\frac12(1)\frac2{25}}_{[0,1]}
     -\underbrace{\frac12(10-8)\frac4{75}}_{[8,10]}\\
 &=1-\frac1{25}-\frac4{75}=\frac{68}{75}\approx90.7\%.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus the standard treasury range covers about $90.7\%$ of modeled weeks.

\subsection{Seafood export inspection time}\label{sec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection}
A coastal inspection office records how long chilled seafood shipments wait
for export clearance.  A shipment may wait any amount from 0 to 8 hours.
Occurrence begins at none for immediate clearance, becomes steadily
more common up to its highest level at 2 hours, and then becomes steadily less
common until a 5-hour wait occurs half as often as a 2-hour wait.  That level
remains unchanged through 6 hours, after which occurrence becomes steadily less
common until there is none at 8 hours.  A wait above 6 hours requires
an overnight cold-storage booking, while the 1--7 hour band is covered by the
ordinary inspection shift.

\COneGraph[i]{0}{8}{(x<=2)*(2*x/17)+(x>2)*(x<=5)*((16-2*x)/51)+(x>5)*(x<=6)*(2/17)+(x>6)*((8-x)/17)}{1}{7}{0,1,2,5,6,7,8}{0.28}

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-a}{2.1}{Construct the complete probability density function of $I$ from the narrative.  Show how total area $1$ determines every unknown value, give all required formulas, and include $0$ outside the possible range.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-b}{2.2}{Calculate the overnight-storage tail probability $P(I>6)$ geometrically.}
 \CriterionTask{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-c}{2.3}{Calculate the central-band probability $P(1\le I\le7)$ geometrically.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{C2.1 - Construct the pdf from the situation.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-a}
\CtwoSolutionStep{1}{Support and shape}
Let $I$ denote inspection time in hours.  The support is $[0,8]$, with heights
$0,H,H/2,H/2,0$ at $0,2,5,6,8$.

\CtwoSolutionStep{2}{Total area \(=1\)}
The triangle, trapezoid, rectangle, and final
triangle must have total area $1$:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 1&=\frac12(2)H
 +\frac12(5-2)\left(H+\frac H2\right)
 +(6-5)\frac H2+\frac12(8-6)\frac H2\\
 &=\frac{17}{4}H,
 \qquad H=\frac4{17}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
\CtwoSolutionStep{3}{Straight-line rule}
The changing segments have slopes $2/17$, $-2/51$, and $-1/17$;
the constant segment needs no line equation.  Using the appropriate endpoints
in point-slope form gives the formulas below.
\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
\[
 f_I(i)=\begin{cases}
 \dfrac{2i}{17},&0\le i\le2,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{16-2i}{51},&2<i\le5,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac2{17},&5<i\le6,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{8-i}{17},&6<i\le8,\\[1mm]
 0,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
\]
Adjacent formulas agree at $2$, $5$, and $6$, and their areas sum to $1$.

\subsubsection*{C2.2 - Calculate the tail probability $P(I>6)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
The overnight-storage tail is the final triangle, with base $8-6=2$ and height
$2/17$:
\[
 P(I>6)=\frac12(2)\frac2{17}=\frac2{17}\approx11.8\%.
\]

\subsubsection*{C2.3 - Calculate the central-band probability
$P(1\le I\le7)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-inspection-c}
\CtwoSolutionStep{6}{Interval probability}
The complement contains two triangles.  Their boundary heights are
$f_I(1)=2/17$ and $f_I(7)=1/17$, so
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P(1\le I\le7)
 &=1-\frac12(1)\frac2{17}-\frac12(8-7)\frac1{17}\\
 &=1-\frac1{17}-\frac1{34}=\frac{31}{34}\approx91.2\%.
 \end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Port truck-gate waiting time}\label{sec:c2-piecewise-linear-gate}
A port terminal records how long trucks wait between joining the entry queue
and reaching the inspection gate.  A wait may be any amount from 0 to 12
hours.  Occurrence begins at none for no wait, becomes steadily more
common up to its highest level at 3 hours, and then becomes steadily less common
until a 6-hour wait occurs half as often as a 3-hour wait.  That lower level
remains unchanged through 9 hours, after which occurrence becomes steadily less
common until there is none at 12 hours.  A wait above 10 hours causes
a missed loading slot, while the 2--10 hour band is used for routine gate
staffing.

\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-gate-a}
\CtwoSolutionStep{1}{Support and shape}
Let $W$ denote gate waiting time in hours.  The support is $[0,12]$, and the
heights at $0,3,6,9,12$ are $0,H,H/2,H/2,0$.

\CtwoSolutionStep{2}{Total area \(=1\)}
Normalisation gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 1&=\frac12(3)H

 +(9-6)\frac H2+\frac12(12-9)\frac H2\\
 &=6H,\qquad H=\frac16.
 \end{aligned}
\]
\CtwoSolutionStep{3}{Straight-line rule}
The successive slopes are $1/18$, $-1/36$, $0$, and $-1/36$.
Using the endpoint values gives the formulas shown below.

\CtwoSolutionStep{4}{Complete pdf}
The complete pdf is therefore
\[
 f_W(w)=\begin{cases}
 \dfrac{w}{18},&0\le w\le3,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{9-w}{36},&3<w\le6,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac1{12},&6<w\le9,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{12-w}{36},&9<w\le12,\\[1mm]
 0,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
\]
The formulas meet at the stated heights and the geometric areas total $1$.

\subsubsection*{C2.2 - Calculate the tail probability $P(W>10)$.}
\phantomsection\label{subsec:c2-piecewise-linear-gate-b}
\CtwoSolutionStep{5}{Tail probability}
At $w=10$, the final side has height $f_W(10)=2/36=1/18$.  The right tail is
a triangle of base $2$:
\[
 P(W>10)=\frac12(12-10)\frac1{18}=\frac1{18}\approx5.6\%.

\subsubsection*{C2.3 - Calculate the central-band probability
$P(2\le W\le10)$.}

The left outside triangle has base $2$ and height $f_W(2)=1/9$; the right
outside triangle is the tail just calculated.  Thus
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 P(2\le W\le10)
 &=1-\frac12(2)\frac19-\frac1{18}
 =1-\frac19-\frac1{18}=\frac56.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus routine staffing covers about $83.3\%$ of modeled waits.

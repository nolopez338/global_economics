	\section*{Evaluated criteria}
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}]
		\item Characterises continuous random variables by distinguishing them from discrete variables and interpreting their probability density function, cumulative distribution function, and support in economic contexts.
		\item Applies uniform and triangular continuous distributions with constant or piecewise-linear probability density functions to calculate interval probabilities using geometric areas.
		\item Applies circular-arc probability density functions to calculate interval probabilities using the geometry of circles and circular segments.
		\item Applies the exponential distribution to constant-rate waiting-time situations and assesses its suitability.
	\end{enumerate}
	

	\sectionScored
	{Identifying Probability Distributions from Contexts}
	{C1-\sml{5}}
	{sec:identifying-probability-distributions}
	{
		A variety of Colombian organizations use probability models to represent uncertain quantities and events. In each situation below, a random variable is defined from the information provided. The appropriate probability distribution must be identified from the characteristics of the situation.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:identifying-probability-distributions-c1-characterization}{1}
			{For each context, characterize the random variable and identify the appropriate probability distribution. Briefly justify your choice based on the features described. \score{5}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\textbf{Context 1.} A payment platform monitors one scheduled electronic transfer. By the end of processing, it is either completed successfully or not completed. The variable \(X_1\) records the outcome to help estimate verification and support needs.
		
		\textbf{Context 2.} An agricultural technology company monitors the daily temperature variation inside a refrigerated storage unit. The variation \(X_2\) ranges from \(-2^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(2^\circ\mathrm{C}\), with all values in this interval considered equally likely. The company uses this model to set an operating margin.
		
		\textbf{Context 3.} A streaming platform sends the same offer to exactly 80 randomly selected eligible users. Each user either subscribes or does not. The variable \(X_3\) counts the number of subscribers to estimate the server capacity needed for the promotion.
		
		\textbf{Context 4.} A Bogotá parking company records the number of vehicle breakdowns during a fixed one-week period. Comparable records show that breakdowns occur independently and without a predictable timetable. The variable \(X_4\) counts the breakdowns to help schedule maintenance staff.
		
		\textbf{Context 5.} An online marketplace studies the delivery time \(X_5\) for a particular type of order. Delivery times range from 20 to 80 minutes, with deliveries around 45 minutes occurring most frequently. They become progressively less common toward either extreme. The company uses this model to estimate delivery times.
		
		\textbf{Context 6.} A quality-control laboratory tests electronic components until the first defective component is found. Components are tested independently, and testing stops after the first defect. The variable \(X_6\) records the number of components tested, including the defective one, to estimate inspection costs.
		
		\textbf{Context 7.} A cloud-computing provider studies storage usage \(X_7\) among small business customers. Usage ranges from 0 to 50 gigabytes and becomes steadily more common toward the upper limit, without reaching a peak before 50 GB. The provider uses this model to plan capacity.
		
		\textbf{Context 8.} A retailer sends a promotional message to exactly 120 customers selected using the same procedure. Each customer either makes a purchase or does not. The variable \(X_8\) counts the number of purchases to estimate the inventory needed for the campaign.
		
		\textbf{Context 9.} A coffee exporter measures the moisture content \(X_9\) of coffee bags under usual storage conditions. Measurements cluster around 11%, with approximately symmetric behaviour above and below this value. Values become less common farther from 11%. The exporter uses this model to establish quality-control thresholds.
		
		\textbf{Context 10.} A food-delivery company records the number of customer cancellations during a fixed three-hour dinner period in one delivery zone. Comparable evenings show cancellations occurring independently without a recurring timetable. The variable \(X_{10}\) counts the cancellations to estimate customer-service needs.
		
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:identifying-probability-distributions-c1-solution}
		\subsection*{C1 - Distribution identification: Solutions \score{10}}
		
		The appropriate distribution for each context is determined by whether the variable is discrete or continuous and by the defining features of the situation:
		
	\begin{enumerate}
		
		\item \textbf{Context 1 — Discrete, Bernoulli.}
		The transfer is either completed or not completed.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 2 — Continuous, Uniform.}
		All values within a fixed interval are equally likely.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 3 — Discrete, Binomial.}
		The variable counts subscriptions among 80 comparable users.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 4 — Discrete, Poisson.}
		The variable counts independent incidents occurring during a fixed period.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 5 — Continuous, Triangular.}
		The variable has a minimum, maximum, and most likely value.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 6 — Discrete, Geometric.}
		The variable counts trials until the first defective component is found.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 7 — Continuous, Linear.}
		The density increases steadily toward the upper limit.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 8 — Discrete, Binomial.}
		The variable counts purchases among 120 comparable customers.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 9 — Continuous, Normal.}
		Values cluster symmetrically around a central mean.
		
		\item \textbf{Context 10 — Discrete, Poisson.}
		The variable counts independent cancellations during a fixed period.
		
	\end{enumerate}

	}
	
	\sectionScored
	{Delivery Time for a Local Grocery Order}
	{C2-\sml{4}}
	{sec:linear-delivery-time-distribution}
	{
		A Colombian grocery-delivery company studies the additional time required to deliver orders during a busy evening period. For a particular delivery zone, the added delivery time \(X\) can range from 0 to 10 minutes. A delay of any length within this interval is possible, and shorter delays become steadily more common. Historical data indicate that no additional delay is 4 times as common as a 10-minute delay.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:linear-delivery-time-probabilities}{2}
			{Determine the complete probability density function for the added delivery time. Then determine the probability that the added delivery time exceeds 7 minutes, and the probability that it is between 3 and 7 minutes. \score{4}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:linear-delivery-time-probabilities}
		\subsection*{C2 - Linear probability distribution \score{4}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Support and Shape}
		The random variable \(X\) has support \([0,10]\). The context describes a steadily decreasing straight-line density whose height at \(0\) is four times its height at \(10\). Write the endpoint heights as \(4h\) and \(h\). The density is zero outside the support.
		
		\SolvedGraph{Shape from the context}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{11}{0.30}{0,10}{0,10}
			{0,0.20,0.05}{x}{$0$,$4h$,$h$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.20) (10,0.05) (10,0) (11,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Total Area = 1}
		The total area under a probability density function must equal \(1\). Therefore, the trapezoid formed by the density has area \(1\):
		\[
		1=\frac12(10)(4h+h)=25h,
		\qquad
		h=\frac1{25},
		\qquad
		4h=\frac4{25}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Completed density graph}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{11}{0.18}{0,3,7,10}{0,3,7,10}
			{0,0.16,0.04}{x}{$0$,$\frac4{25}$,$\frac1{25}$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.16) (10,0.04) (10,0) (11,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{3}{Straight Line Rule}
		The density is a straight line passing through the two endpoint points
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m=
		\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
		\]
		\[
		\left(0,\frac4{25}\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(10,\frac1{25}\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m=
		\frac{\frac1{25}-\frac4{25}}{10 - 0}
		=
		-\frac3{250},
		\]
		and the \(y\)-intercept is
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b=
		y_1-mx_1
		=
		y_2-mx_2.
		\]
		\[
		\left(0,\frac4{25}\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(10,\frac1{25}\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b=
		\frac4{25}
		-\left(-\frac3{250}\right)(0)
		=
		\frac1{25}
		-\left(-\frac3{250}\right)(10)
		=
		\frac4{25}.
		\]
		
		Therefore, the probability density function is
		\[
		f_X(x)=\frac{40-3x}{250}
		\]
		on the stated interval.
		
		\SolutionStage{4}{Complete pdf}
		Including the values outside the possible range of added delivery times, the complete probability density function is
		\[
		f_X(x)=
		\begin{cases}
			\dfrac{40-3x}{250},&0\le x\le10,\\[1mm]
			0,&\text{otherwise.}
		\end{cases}
		\]
		
		\SolutionStage{5}{Probability of More Than 7 Minutes}
		The company wants to know the chance that an order takes more than 7 additional minutes to deliver. This corresponds to the area under the density from 7 to the maximum possible delay of 10 minutes:
		\[
		P(X>7)
		=
		\frac12(10-7)
		\left(
		f_X(7)+f_X(10)
		\right).
		\]
		
		Since
		\[
		f_X(7)=\frac{19}{250}
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		f_X(10)=\frac1{25},
		\]
		\[
		P(X>7)
		=
		\frac12(3)
		\left(
		\frac{19}{250}+\frac1{25}
		\right)
		=
		\frac{9}{100}.
		\]
		
		Thus, the probability of more than 7 minutes of additional delivery time is
		\[
		\boxed{P(X>7)=0.09}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Tail graph: $P(X>7)$}{%
			\COneGraph[x]{0}{10}{(40-3*x)/250}{7}{10}{0,3,7,10}{0.18}
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{6}{Probability Between 3 and 7 Minutes}
		The company also wants to know the chance that the added delivery time falls between 3 and 7 minutes. This corresponds to the area under the density between these two times:
		\[
		P(3\le X\le7)
		=
		\frac12(7-3)
		\left(
		f_X(3)+f_X(7)
		\right).
		\]
		
		Since
		\[
		f_X(3)=\frac{31}{250}
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		f_X(7)=\frac{19}{250},
		\]
		\[
		P(3\le X\le7)
		=
		\frac12(4)
		\left(
		\frac{31}{250}+\frac{19}{250}
		\right)
		=
		\frac25.
		\]
		
		Thus, the probability of between 3 and 7 minutes of additional delivery time is
		\[
		\boxed{P(3\le X\le7)=0.40}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Band graph: $P(3\le X\le7)$}{%
			\COneGraph[x]{0}{10}{(40-3*x)/250}{3}{7}{0,3,7,10}{0.18}
		}
		
		\vspace{-5mm}
		\secInicio[sec:linear-delivery-time-distribution][subsec:linear-delivery-time-probabilities]
		
	}

	\sectionScored
	{Daily Commute Time for a Bogotá Apprentice}
	{C2-\sml{4}}
	{sec:triangular-commute-time-distribution}
	{
		An apprentice records the time spent travelling to a training programme
		each day. Commute times range from 1 to 9 hours. Times become steadily
		more common from 1 hour up to a most common time of 4 hours, then
		steadily less common through 9 hours; there are no commute times right
		at either limit.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities}{2}
			{Determine the complete probability density function for the commute
				time. Then determine the probability that the commute takes more
				than 6 hours, and the probability that it takes between 3 and 7
				hours. \score{4}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities}
		\subsection*{C2 - Triangular probability distribution \score{4}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Support and Shape}
		The random variable \(T\) has support \([1,9]\). The context supplies
		zero height at both endpoints, a steady rise to the most common value
		at \(t=4\), and a steady fall thereafter. Denote the unknown peak height
		by \(H\); the density is zero outside the support.
		
		\SolvedGraph{Shape from the context}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{0}{10}{0.30}{1,4,9}{1,4,9}
			{0,0.25}{t}{$0$,$H$}
			{(0,0) (1,0) (4,0.25) (9,0) (10,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Total Area = 1}
		The triangle must have area \(1\):
		\[
		1=\frac12(9-1)H=4H,
		\qquad
		H=\frac14.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Completed density graph}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{0}{10}{0.30}{1,3,4,6,7,9}{1,3,4,6,7,9}
			{0,0.25}{t}{$0$,$\frac14$}
			{(0,0) (1,0) (4,0.25) (9,0) (10,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{3}{Straight Line Rule}
		For the rising segment, the density is a straight line passing
		through the two endpoint points
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m=
		\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
		\]
		\[
		(1,0)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(4,\frac14\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m_1=
		\frac{\frac14-0}{4-1}
		=
		\frac1{12}.
		\]
		The \(y\)-intercept is
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b=
		y_1-mx_1
		=
		y_2-mx_2.
		\]
		\[
		(1,0)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(4,\frac14\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b_1=
		0-\frac1{12}(1)
		=
		\frac14-\frac1{12}(4)
		=
		-\frac1{12}.
		\]
		Therefore, the equation of the rising segment is
		\[
		y=m_1t+b_1
		=
		\frac{t-1}{12}.
		\]
		
		For the falling segment, the density is a straight line passing
		through the two endpoint points
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m=
		\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
		\]
		\[
		\left(4,\frac14\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		(9,0).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		m_2=
		\frac{0-\frac14}{9-4}
		=
		-\frac1{20}.
		\]
		The \(y\)-intercept is
		\[
		\left(x_1,y_1\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		\left(x_2,y_2\right).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b=
		y_1-mx_1
		=
		y_2-mx_2.
		\]
		\[
		\left(4,\frac14\right)
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		(9,0).
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		b_2=
		\frac14-\left(-\frac1{20}\right)(4)
		=
		0-\left(-\frac1{20}\right)(9)
		=
		\frac9{20}.
		\]
		Therefore, the equation of the falling segment is
		\[
		y=m_2t+b_2
		=
		\frac{9-t}{20}.
		\]
		
		The adjacent rules agree at \(t=4\): both give \(\frac14\).
		
		\SolutionStage{4}{Complete pdf}
		Including the values outside the possible range of commute times, the
		complete probability density function is
		\[
		f_T(t)=
		\begin{cases}
			\dfrac{t-1}{12},&1\le t\le4,\\[1mm]
			\dfrac{9-t}{20},&4<t\le9,\\[1mm]
			0,&\text{otherwise.}
		\end{cases}
		\]
		
		\SolutionStage{5}{Probability of More Than 6 Hours}
		The apprentice wants to know the chance that the commute takes more
		than 6 hours. This corresponds to the area under the density from
		6 to the maximum possible commute time of 9 hours:
		\[
		P(T>6)
		=
		\frac12(9-6)
		\left(
		f_T(6)+f_T(9)
		\right).
		\]
		
		First, evaluate the density at the two endpoints:
		\[
		f_T(6)
		=
		\frac{9-6}{20}
		=
		\frac3{20},
		\]
		\[
		f_T(9)
		=
		\frac{9-9}{20}
		=
		0.
		\]
		
		Substituting these values gives
		\[
		\begin{aligned}
			P(T>6)
			&=
			\frac12(9-6)
			\left(
			\frac3{20}+0
			\right)\\
			&=
			\frac12(3)\left(\frac3{20}\right)\\
			&=
			\frac9{40}.
		\end{aligned}
		\]
		
		Thus, the probability of a commute lasting more than 6 hours is
		\[
		\boxed{P(T>6)=0.225}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Tail graph: $P(T>6)$}{%
			\COneGraph[t]{1}{9}
			{(x<=4)*((x-1)/12)+(x>4)*((9-x)/20)}
			{6}{9}{1,3,4,6,7,9}{0.30}
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{6}{Probability Between 3 and 7 Hours}
		The apprentice also wants to know the chance that the commute takes
		between 3 and 7 hours. We use the complement rule: the event
		\(3\le T\le7\) is the complement of the two tail events
		\(T<3\) and \(T>7\). Therefore,
		\[
		P(3\le T\le7)
		=
		1-P(T<3)-P(T>7).
		\]
		
		The two complementary regions are triangular, so their probabilities
		are given by
		\[
		P(T<3)
		=
		\frac12(3-1)f_T(3),
		\qquad
		P(T>7)
		=
		\frac12(9-7)f_T(7).
		\]
		Hence,
		\[
		P(3\le T\le7)
		=
		1
		-\frac12(3-1)f_T(3)
		-\frac12(9-7)f_T(7).
		\]
		
		First, evaluate the density at the two relevant points:
		\[
		f_T(3)
		=
		\frac{3-1}{12}
		=
		\frac16,
		\]
		\[
		f_T(7)
		=
		\frac{9-7}{20}
		=
		\frac1{10}.
		\]
		
		Substituting these values gives
		\[
		\begin{aligned}
			P(3\le T\le7)
			&=
			1
			-\frac12(3-1)\left(\frac16\right)
			-\frac12(9-7)\left(\frac1{10}\right)\\
			&=
			1-\frac16-\frac1{10}\\
			&=
			\frac{11}{15}.
		\end{aligned}
		\]
		
		Thus, the probability of a commute lasting between 3 and 7 hours is
		\[
		\boxed{P(3\le T\le7)=\frac{11}{15}\approx73.3\%}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Band graph: $P(3\le T\le7)$}{%
			\COneGraph[t]{1}{9}
			{(x<=4)*((x-1)/12)+(x>4)*((9-x)/20)}
			{3}{7}{1,3,4,6,7,9}{0.30}
		}
		
		\vspace{-5mm}
		\secInicio[sec:triangular-commute-time-distribution]
		[subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities]
		
	}
	
	
	\sectionScored
	{Timing of an Unexpected Market Movement}
	{C3-\sml{4}}
	{sec:uniform-market-event-time}
	{
		A financial monitoring system records the time of day at which an unexpected movement in an international currency market occurs. The movement is equally likely to occur at any time during a 24-hour period, so no part of the day is more likely than another.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:uniform-market-event-time-probability}{3}
			{Determine the complete probability density function for the time at which the market movement occurs. Then determine the probability that the movement occurs between 9 p.m. and 5 a.m. \score{4}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:uniform-market-event-time-probability}
		\subsection*{C3 - Uniform probability distribution \score{4}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Support and Shape}
		Let \(T\) represent the time of day, measured in hours after midnight. The random variable \(T\) has support \([0,24)\). The context says that the market movement is equally likely to occur at any time during the day, so the density is a rectangle of unknown height \(h\) and is zero outside the support.
		
		\SolvedGraph{Shape from the context}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{25}{0.30}{0,24}{0,24}
			{0,0.05}{t}{$0$,$h$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.05) (24,0.05) (24,0) (25,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Total Area = 1}
		The rectangle must have area \(1\):
		\[
		1=(24-0)h=24h,
		\qquad
		h=\frac1{24}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Completed density graph}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{25}{0.15}{0,5,21,24}{0,5,21,24}
			{0,0.05}{t}{$0$,$\frac1{24}$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.04167) (24,0.04167) (24,0) (25,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{3}{Straight Line Rule}
		The only segment is horizontal. Thus \(m=0\), and its constant rule on the support is
		\[
		f_T(t)=\frac1{24};
		\]
		no nontrivial line calculation is needed.
		
		\SolutionStage{4}{Complete pdf}
		Including the values outside the possible range of times, the complete probability density function is
		\[
		f_T(t)=
		\begin{cases}
			\dfrac1{24},&0\le t<24,\\[1mm]
			0,&\text{otherwise}.
		\end{cases}
		\]

		from \(0\) is at most \(\pi/12\).

		occurs on both sides of the reference marker:

		\]

		to \(360^\circ\). The comparison is given in degrees: being within
		\(10^\circ\) of the reference marker means being within \(10^\circ\)
		on either side of \(0^\circ\).

		\[
		10^\circ
		=
		10\left(\frac{\pi}{180}\right)
		=
		\frac{\pi}{18}
		\text{ radians}.
		\]

		total angular width
		\[
		\begin{aligned}
			\text{Degrees:}\qquad
			2(10^\circ)

			20^\circ,\\[2mm]
			\text{Radians:}\qquad

			&=
			\frac{\pi}{9}.

		\]

		\]

		\]

	\sectionScored
	{Comparing Investment Transaction Rates}
	{C4-\sml{10}}
	{sec:exponential-comparing-investment-rates}
	{
		Two investment platforms process buy and sell transactions throughout
		the trading day. The transactions processed by each platform are
		modeled by independent Poisson processes.
		
		For Platform A, there is a \(75\%\) probability that the next
		transaction is processed within 8 minutes. For Platform B, there is a
		\(60\%\) probability that the next transaction is processed within
		5 minutes.
		
		Assume both platforms begin processing transactions at the same time.
		On average, how many minutes does it take for one platform to be ahead
		of the other by 1 transaction?
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:c4-compare-rates-investment}{10}
			{Determine the rate parameter \(\lambda\) for each platform and
				the expected time until one platform is ahead of the other by
				1 transaction. \score{10}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\subsection*{C4.10 - Comparing exponential rates}\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:c4-compare-rates-investment}
		
		Determine the rate parameters for the two platforms.
		
		For Platform A,
		\[
		P(T<8)=0.75.
		\]
		Using
		\[
		P(T<t)=1-e^{-\lambda t},
		\]
		we obtain
		\[
		0.75=1-e^{-8\lambda_A}
		\quad\longrightarrow\quad
		e^{-8\lambda_A}=0.25.
		\]
		Therefore,
		\[
		\lambda_A
		=-\frac{\ln(0.25)}{8}
		\approx0.1733
		\text{ transactions/minute}.
		\]
		
		For Platform B,
		\[
		P(T<5)=0.60.
		\]
		Thus,
		\[
		0.60=1-e^{-5\lambda_B}
		\quad\longrightarrow\quad
		e^{-5\lambda_B}=0.40.
		\]
		Therefore,
		\[
		\lambda_B
		=-\frac{\ln(0.40)}{5}
		\approx0.1833
		\text{ transactions/minute}.
		\]
		
		Platform B has the higher rate, with a rate difference of
		\[
		\lambda_B-\lambda_A
		\approx0.1833-0.1733
		=0.0100
		\text{ transactions/minute}.
		\]
		
		The expected difference in the number of transactions after \(t\)
		minutes is
		\[
		E[N_B(t)-N_A(t)]
		=(\lambda_B-\lambda_A)t.
		\]
		
		To find the time at which Platform B is expected to be ahead by
		1 transaction, set
		\[
		(\lambda_B-\lambda_A)t=1.
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		t=\frac{1}{\lambda_B-\lambda_A}
		=\frac{1}{0.0100}
		=100\text{ minutes}.
		\]
		
		\[
		\boxed{
			\text{Platform B is expected to be ahead by 1 transaction
				after approximately 100 minutes.}
		}
		\]
	}

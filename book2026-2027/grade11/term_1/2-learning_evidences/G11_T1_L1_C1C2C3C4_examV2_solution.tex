	\section*{Evaluated criteria}
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}]
		\item Characterises continuous random variables by distinguishing them from discrete variables and interpreting their probability density function, cumulative distribution function, and support in economic contexts.
		\item Applies uniform and triangular continuous distributions with constant or piecewise-linear probability density functions to calculate interval probabilities using geometric areas.
		\item Applies circular-arc probability density functions to calculate interval probabilities using the geometry of circles and circular segments.
		\item Applies the exponential distribution to constant-rate waiting-time situations and assesses its suitability.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	
	\sectionScored
	{Identifying Probability Distributions from Contexts}
	{C1-\sml{5}}
	{sec:identifying-probability-distributions}
	{
		A variety of Colombian organizations use probability models to represent
		uncertain quantities and events. In each situation below, a random
		variable is defined from the information provided. The appropriate
		probability distribution must be identified from the characteristics
		of the situation.
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:identifying-probability-distributions-c1-characterization}{1}
			{For each context, characterize the random variable and identify the appropriate probability distribution. \score{5}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\textbf{Context 1.} A university sends an invitation to exactly
		60 randomly selected alumni to participate in a fundraising campaign.
		Each alumnus either makes a donation or does not. The university records
		the number of alumni who donate to estimate the expected response to
		the campaign.
		
		\textbf{Context 2.} A courier company models the travel time for a
		delivery route. Travel times range from 25 to 55 minutes, with times
		around 40 minutes occurring most frequently. Values become progressively
		less common as they move away from 40 minutes toward either endpoint.
		The company uses this model to estimate delivery durations.
		
		\textbf{Context 3.} A telecommunications company counts the number of
		incoming technical-support requests received by a service center during
		a fixed 30-minute period. Historical records indicate that requests
		occur independently and without a predictable pattern. The company uses
		this model to plan the number of available support agents.
		
		\textbf{Context 4.} A water-utility company studies the daily water
		consumption of a small residential building. Consumption ranges from
		100 to 500 litres, and the probability density increases steadily as
		consumption approaches 500 litres. The company uses this model to plan
		daily supply capacity.
		
		\textbf{Context 5.} A manufacturer examines batches of circuit boards
		until the first board that fails a functional test is found. Boards are
		tested independently, and testing stops immediately after the first
		failure. The manufacturer records the number of boards tested, including
		the failed board, to estimate inspection requirements.
		
		\textbf{Context 6.} A logistics company studies the delay, in minutes,
		of trucks arriving at a distribution center. Delays can take any value
		between 0 and 30 minutes, with all values in this interval considered
		equally likely. The company uses this model to estimate scheduling
		margins.
		
		\textbf{Context 7.} A hospital records the number of patients arriving
		at an emergency department during each fixed four-hour shift.
		Comparable shifts show that arrivals occur independently and without
		a predictable timetable. The hospital uses this model to plan staffing
		levels.
		
		\textbf{Context 8.} A cinema chain sends a discount code to exactly
		150 randomly selected customers. Each customer either uses the code
		or does not. The chain records the number of customers who use the
		discount to estimate the additional attendance generated by the campaign.
		
		\textbf{Context 9.} A dairy producer measures the fat content of milk
		samples from its regular production process. Measurements cluster around
		3.5\%.
		Measurements farther from 3.5\% occur progressively less often. The
		producer uses this model to establish quality-control limits.
		
		\textbf{Context 10.} A city's electric utility studies the daily
		electricity consumption of a small commercial building. Consumption
		ranges from 200 to 800 kWh, and the probability density decreases
		steadily as consumption moves from 200 kWh toward 800 kWh. The utility
		uses this model to estimate the likelihood of different daily loads.
		
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:identifying-probability-distributions-c1-solution}
		\subsection*{C1 - Distribution identification: Solutions \score{5}}
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			
			\item \textbf{Context 1 — Discrete, Binomial.}
			\(X_1\) counts donations among 60 selected alumni, with each alumnus
			having two possible outcomes.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 2 — Continuous, Triangular.}
			\(X_2\) has a minimum of 25 minutes, maximum of 55 minutes, and
			mode of 40 minutes.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 3 — Discrete, Poisson.}
			\(X_3\) counts independent technical-support requests occurring
			during a fixed time interval.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 4 — Continuous, Linear.}
			\(X_4\) has a density that increases steadily toward 500 litres.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 5 — Discrete, Geometric.}
			\(X_5\) counts the number of boards tested until the first failure.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 6 — Continuous, Uniform.}
			\(X_6\) can take any value in \([0,30]\), with all values equally likely.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 7 — Discrete, Poisson.}
			\(X_7\) counts independent patient arrivals during a fixed period.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 8 — Discrete, Binomial.}
			\(X_8\) counts discount-code users among 150 customers, with each
			customer having two possible outcomes.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 9 — Continuous, Normal.}
			\(X_9\) is approximately symmetric and concentrated around 3.5\%.
			
			\item \textbf{Context 10 — Continuous, Linear.}
			\(X_{10}\) has a density that decreases steadily from 200 kWh
			toward 800 kWh.
			
		\end{enumerate}

	\sectionScored
	{Charging Time for an Electric Vehicle}
	{C2-\sml{3}}
	{sec:linear-charging-time-distribution}
	{
		A Colombian transportation company studies the additional charging time
		required for electric vehicles at one of its charging stations. For a
		particular type of vehicle, the additional charging time can range
		from 0 to 4 minutes. A charging time of any length within this interval
		is possible, and shorter additional times become steadily more common.
		Historical data indicate that an additional charging time of 0 minutes
		is twice as common as an additional charging time of 4 minutes.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:linear-charging-time-probabilities}{2}
			{Determine the probability that the additional charging time is between 1 and 3 minutes. \score{3}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:linear-charging-time-probabilities}
		\subsection*{C2 - Linear probability distribution \score{3}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Support and Shape}
		The random variable \(X\) has support \([0,4]\). The density decreases
		linearly, and its height at \(0\) is twice its height at \(4\).
		Let the endpoint heights be \(2h\) and \(h\).
		
		\SolvedGraph{Shape from the context}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{5}{0.45}{0,4}{0,4}
			{0,0.30,0.15}{x}{$0$,$2h$,$h$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.30) (4,0.15) (4,0) (5,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Total Area = 1}
		The total area under a probability density function is \(1\).
		For a trapezoid with parallel sides of heights \(h_1\) and \(h_2\) and base \(b\),
		\[
		A=\frac{h_1+h_2}{2}(b)
		\]
		Therefore, in this case,
		\[
		1=\frac{2h+h}{2}(4)
		\qquad \rightarrow \qquad
		1=3h(2)
		\qquad \rightarrow \qquad
		h=\frac16.
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		f(0)=2h=\frac13,
		\qquad
		f(4)=h=\frac16.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Completed density graph}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{-1}{5}{0.30}{0,1,3,4}{0,1,3,4}
			{0,0.30,0.15}{x}{$0$,$\frac13$,$\frac16$}
			{(-1,0) (0,0) (0,0.30) (4,0.15) (4,0) (5,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{3}{Complete pdf}
		The density is the straight line through
		\[
		\begin{array}{c@{\qquad}c}
			(x_1,y_1) & (x_2,y_2) \\[2mm]
			\left(0,\dfrac13\right)
			&
			\left(4,\dfrac16\right)
		\end{array}
		\]
		For a general straight line,
		\[
		f(x)=mx+b
		\qquad
		\text{where}
		\qquad
		m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		b=y_1-mx_1
		\]
		Substituting the given points,
		\[
		m=
		\frac{\frac16-\frac13}{4-0}
		=
		-\frac1{24}
		\qquad
		b=\frac13 - \frac{-1}{24}(0) =\frac13
		\]
		
		\[
			f(x)
			=-\frac{x}{24}+\frac13 = -\frac{x}{24}+\frac{8}{24} =\frac{8-x}{24}
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		f(x)=
		\begin{cases}
			\dfrac{8-x}{24},&0\le x\le4\\[1mm]
			0,&\text{otherwise}
		\end{cases}
		\]
		
		\SolutionStage{4}{Probability Between 1 and 3 Minutes}
		The required probability is the area under the density from \(1\) to \(3\):
		\[
		P(1\le X\le3)
		=
		\frac{f(1)+f(3)}{2}(3-1)
		\]
		
		Since
		\[
		f(1)=\frac{8-1}{24}=\frac{7}{24},
		\qquad
		f(3)=\frac{8-3}{24}=\frac{5}{24}.
		\]
		\[
		P(1\le X\le3)
		=
		\frac12
		\left(
		\frac14+\frac18
		\right)(2)
		=
		\frac38.
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		\boxed{P(1\le X\le3)=\frac38=0.375}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Band graph: $P(1\le X\le3)$}{%
			\COneGraph[x]{0}{4}{1/3-x/24}{1}{3}{0,1,3,4}{0.30}
		}
		
		\vspace{-5mm}
		\secInicio[sec:linear-charging-time-distribution][subsec:linear-charging-time-probabilities]
		
	}
	
	\sectionScored
	{Daily Sales Revenue of a Local Business}
	{C2-\sml{4}}
	{sec:triangular-commute-time-distribution}
	{
		A local business records its daily sales revenue over several months.
		Daily revenue ranges from \$1 million to \$7 million. Revenue becomes
		steadily more common from \$1 million up to a most common level of
		\$6 million, then steadily less common through \$7 million; there are
		no observations exactly at either limit.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities}{2}
			{Determine the probability that the daily sales revenue is between
				\$2 million and \$6 million. \score{4}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities}
		\subsection*{C2 - Triangular probability distribution \score{4}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Support and Shape}
		Let \(R\) represent the daily sales revenue, measured in millions
		of dollars. The random variable \(R\) has support \([1,7]\).
		Its density rises linearly to a peak at \(r=6\), then falls
		linearly to zero at \(r=7\). Let the peak height be \(H\).
		
		\SolvedGraph{Shape from the context}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{0}{8}{0.30}{1,2,6,7}{1,2,6,7}
			{0,0.35}{r}{$0$,$H$}
			{(0,0) (1,0) (6,0.3333) (7,0) (8,0)}%
		}
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Total Area = 1}
		The total area under a probability density function is \(1\).
		For a triangle with base \(b\) and height \(H\),
		\[
		A=\frac12 bH.
		\]
		
		Therefore, in this case,
		\[
		1
		=
		\frac12(7-1)H
		\qquad \rightarrow \qquad
		1
		=
		\frac12(6)H
		\qquad \rightarrow \qquad
		1=3H
		\qquad \rightarrow \qquad
		H=\frac13.
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		f(6)=\frac13.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Completed density graph}{%
			\CompactDensityCoordinatesGraph{0}{8}{0.30}{1,2,6,7}{1,2,6,7}
			{0,0.35}{r}{$0$,$\frac13$}
			{(0,0) (1,0) (6,0.3333) (7,0) (8,0)}%
		}
		
			\SolutionStage{3}{Complete pdf}
			The density consists of two straight-line segments. For a general
			straight line,
			\[
			f(r)=mr+b,
			\qquad
			\text{where}
			\qquad
			m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}
			\qquad\text{and}\qquad
			b=y_1-mx_1.
			\]
			
			For the rising segment, the line passes through
			\[
			\begin{array}{c@{\qquad}c}
				(x_1,y_1) & (x_2,y_2) \\[2mm]
				(1,0)
				&
				\left(6,\dfrac13\right)
			\end{array}
			\]
			Thus
			\[
			m_1
			=
			\frac{\frac13-0}{6-1}
			=
			\frac1{15}
			\qquad
			b_1
			=
			0-\frac1{15}(1)
			=
			-\frac1{15}.
			\]
			
			Therefore,
			\[
			f(r)
			=
			\frac{r}{15}-\frac1{15}
			=
			\frac{r-1}{15}.
			\]
			
			For the falling segment, the line passes through
			\[
			\left(6,\frac13\right)
			\qquad\text{and}\qquad
			(7,0).
			\]
			However, these calculations are not needed for the requested
			probability, because the interval \(2\le R\le6\) ends at the mode.
			Therefore, the equation of the falling segment is stated directly
			without showing its derivation.
			
			The complete probability density function is
			\[
			f(r)=
			\begin{cases}
				\dfrac{r-1}{15},&1\le r\le6,\\[1mm]
				\dfrac{7-r}{3},&6<r\le7,\\[1mm]
				0,&\text{otherwise.}
			\end{cases}
			\]
		
		\SolutionStage{4}{Probability Between \$2 and \$6 Million}
		The complete triangular density has a base extending from
		\(r=1\) to \(r=7\). The required interval extends from
		\(r=2\) to the mode at \(r=6\). Therefore, the area outside
		the required interval consists of two tails:
		the left tail from \(r=1\) to \(r=2\), and the right tail
		from \(r=6\) to \(r=7\):
		\[
		P(2\le R\le6)
		=
		1-P(R<2)-P(R>6).
		\]
		
		For the left tail, the density is
		\[
		f(r)=\frac{r-1}{15}.
		\]
		Thus,
		\[
		f(2)
		=
		\frac{2-1}{15}
		=
		\frac1{15}.
		\]
		
		The left-tail area is therefore
		\[
		P(R<2)
		=
		\frac12(2-1)\left(\frac1{15}\right)
		=
		\frac1{30}.
		\]
		
		At the mode the height is the height of the triangle as seen in part 1.
		\[
		f(6)
		=
		\frac{1}{3}		\]
		
		The right-tail area is therefore
		\[
		P(R>6)
		=
		\frac12(7-6)\left(\frac13\right)
		=
		\frac16.
		\]
		
		Consequently,
		\[
		\begin{aligned}
			P(2\le R\le6)
			&=
			1-\frac1{30}-\frac16
			\\
			&=
			1-\frac1{30}-\frac5{30}
			\\
			&=
			1-\frac6{30}
			\\
			&=
			\frac{24}{30}
			\\
			&=
			\frac45.
		\end{aligned}
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		\boxed{P(2\le R\le6)=\frac45=80\%}.
		\]
		
		\SolvedGraph{Band graph: $P(2\le R\le6)$}{%
			\COneGraph[r]{1}{7}
			{(x<=6)*((x-1)/15)+(x>6)*((7-x)/3)}
			{2}{6}{1,2,6,7}{0.35}
		}
		
		
		\vspace{-5mm}
		\secInicio[sec:triangular-commute-time-distribution]
		[subsec:triangular-commute-time-probabilities]
		
	}
	
	\sectionScored
	{Timing of an Overnight Price Adjustment}
	{C3-\sml{2}}
	{sec:uniform-price-adjustment-time}
	{
		A retail company monitors the time of day at which an unexpected
		price adjustment occurs in one of its online markets. The adjustment
		is equally likely to occur at any time during a 24-hour period,
		so no part of the day is more likely than another.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:uniform-price-adjustment-time-probability}{3}
			{Determine the probability that the price adjustment occurs between
				8 p.m. and 6 a.m. \score{2}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:uniform-price-adjustment-time-probability}
		\subsection*{C3 - Uniform probability distribution \score{2}}
		
		\SolutionStage{1}{Uniform Distribution}
		Let \(T\) represent the time of the price adjustment, measured in hours
		after midnight. Since every time during the 24-hour period is equally
		likely,
		\[
		T\sim U(0,24)
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		f(t)=\frac1{24-0}=\frac1{24}.
		\]
		
		\SolutionStage{2}{Probability Between 8 p.m. and 6 a.m.}
		The interval crosses midnight, so it consists of
		\[
		20\le T<24
		\qquad\text{and}\qquad
		0\le T\le6.
		\]
		
		The probability is found by calculating the area of each rectangle
		individually and then adding them:
		\[
		A_1=(6-0)\left(\frac1{24}\right)
		=\frac6{24},
		\]
		\[
		A_2=(24-20)\left(\frac1{24}\right)
		=\frac4{24}.
		\]
		
		Therefore,
		\[
		P(\text{between 8 p.m. and 6 a.m.})
		=
		A_1+A_2
		=
		\frac6{24}+\frac4{24}
		=
		\frac{10}{24}
		=
		\boxed{\frac5{12}\approx41.67\%}.
		\]

	\sectionScored
	{Direction of an Unexpected Sensor Reading}
	{C3-\sml{3}}
	{sec:uniform-sensor-direction}

		\approx \boxed{2.2\text{ orders}}

	\sectionScored
	{Comparing Economic Forecasting Rates}
	{C4-\sml{2}}
	{sec:exponential-comparing-economic-forecasting-rates}
	{
		Two economic forecasting systems monitor significant changes in market
		indicators throughout the day. The detected changes by each system are
		modeled by independent Poisson processes.
		
		For System A, there is a \(70\%\) probability that the next significant
		change is detected within 10 minutes. For System B, there is a \(50\%\)
		probability that the next significant change is detected within
		6 minutes.
		
		\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
		\begin{enumerate}
			\CriterionTask
			{subsec:c4-compare-rates-economic}{2}
			{Determine after how many minutes the expected numbers of significant
				changes detected by the two systems differ by 1 change.
				\score{2}}
		\end{enumerate}
		
		\subsection*{C4.10 - Comparing detection rates}\phantomsection
		\label{subsec:c4-compare-rates-economic}
		
		For an exponential waiting time \(T\), if
		$$
		P(T<t)=p
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		p=1-e^{-\lambda t}
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		{
			\lambda=-\frac{\ln(1-p)}{t}
		}
		$$
		
		For System A, \(P(T<10)=0.70\), so
		$$
		\lambda_A
		=-\frac{\ln(1-0.70)}{10}
		\approx0.1204
		\>\frac{\text{changes}}{\text{minute}}
		$$
		
		For System B, \(P(T<6)=0.50\), so
		$$
		\lambda_B
		=-\frac{\ln(1-0.50)}{6}
		\approx0.1155
		\>\frac{\text{changes}}{\text{minute}}
		$$
		
		Therefore, System A has the higher detection rate. The difference
		between the rates is
		$$
		\lambda_A-\lambda_B
		\approx0.1204-0.1155
		=0.0049
		\>\frac{\text{changes}}{\text{minute}}
		$$
		
		After \(t\) minutes, this rate difference accumulates to
		$
		(\lambda_A-\lambda_B)t
		$
		changes. We want this difference to reach 1 change:
		$$
		(\lambda_A-\lambda_B)t=1.
		\qquad \longrightarrow \qquad
		t
		=\frac{1}{\lambda_A-\lambda_B}
		=\frac{1}{0.0049}
		\approx \boxed{204\text{ minutes}}
		$$
	}

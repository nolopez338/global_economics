\section*{Learning objective}
Structure decision-making problems under uncertainty, calculate and organise payoffs, and apply the Maximax and Maximin criteria to compare alternatives and justify decisions.

\section*{Criteria assessment}
This task sheet assesses criteria C1 through C4. Each problem assesses the criteria indicated in its title. The number of points assigned to each task is shown at the end of the item. Partial credit may be awarded when the correct procedure is shown but arithmetic errors are made. Answers that do not show the required procedure may not receive credit. A minimum of 10 points is required to pass each criterion.

\section*{Evaluated criteria}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}]
    \item Characterises decision-making problems under uncertainty by identifying decision alternatives, states of nature, consequences, and the economic or financial context of the decision.
    \item Organises decision problems using payoff tables, placing alternatives, states of nature, and payoffs in a clear structure for comparison.
    \item Calculates profits by combining revenues with fixed and variable costs across different states of nature, determining the corresponding payoffs for each alternative, and organising the results in payoff tables for comparison.
    \item Applies the Maximin and Maximax criteria to make optimistic or conservative decisions without probabilities, selecting the alternative with the highest possible payoff or the best worst-case, interpreting risks or limitations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{[6] Weekend Market Stall (C1--C2)}
\label{sec:weekend-market-stall-c1-c2}
A craft cooperative must decide where to operate its stall for the weekend before knowing how strong customer demand will be. The cooperative can choose either a central stall or a neighbourhood stall, while weekend demand may turn out to be strong or weak.

If the cooperative chooses the central stall, it expects to earn a profit of fourteen thousand pounds when demand is strong and five thousand pounds when demand is weak. If it chooses the neighbourhood stall, it expects to earn a profit of ten thousand pounds when demand is strong and eight thousand pounds when demand is weak.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:weekend-market-stall-c1-c2-c1-characterization}{\textbf{(3)} \textbf{(C1)} Characterize the key elements of the decision-making situation.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:weekend-market-stall-c1-c2-c2-payoff-table}{\textbf{(3)} \textbf{(C2)} Construct the payoff table.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [3] C1 characterization.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:weekend-market-stall-c1-c2-c1-characterization}
The decision maker is the craft cooperative, and the business context is choosing a weekend selling location. Let
\(A_1=\) central stall and \(A_2=\) neighbourhood stall. The states of nature are \(S_1=\) strong demand and \(S_2=\) weak demand. The consequences are the profits earned by the cooperative, measured in thousand pounds. The cooperative chooses its location before it knows which demand state will occur, so the decision is made under uncertainty.

\subsection{[15] Urban Delivery Service (C1--C2)}
\label{sec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2}
An urban logistics company provides transportation and delivery services across Bogotá. Before beginning daily operations, the company must decide how to organize the vehicles used to move packages between different parts of the city. It can either hire and operate its own dedicated van fleet or contract a third-party logistics provider to carry out the deliveries. Traffic conditions throughout the city may turn out to be relatively clear or heavily congested.

The payoff from the service depends on the number of deliveries successfully completed during the day, together with contributions and costs associated with both the chosen transportation arrangement and the traffic conditions. All monetary values are measured in thousand USD.

Hiring and operating its own van fleet provides a contribution of \(0.7\) thousand USD for each completed delivery and involves a fixed cost of \(8\) thousand USD for the day's operation. Contracting a third-party delivery service instead provides a contribution of \(0.5\) thousand USD for each completed delivery and involves a fixed cost of \(13\) thousand USD.

Traffic conditions also affect the financial result of providing the transportation service. When traffic across the city is relatively clear, each completed delivery contributes an additional \(0.2\) thousand USD, while the operation incurs an additional fixed cost of \(4\) thousand USD. When the city experiences heavy traffic, each completed delivery contributes an additional \(0.1\) thousand USD, while the operation incurs a larger additional fixed cost of \(6\) thousand USD.

The number of deliveries that can be completed during the day also depends on both the transportation arrangement and the traffic conditions. With its own van fleet, the company expects to complete \(80\) deliveries when traffic is relatively clear and \(55\) when traffic is heavy. When using a third-party delivery service, it expects to complete \(70\) deliveries when traffic is relatively clear and \(60\) when traffic is heavy.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2-c1-characterization}{\textbf{(5)} \textbf{(C1)} Characterize the key elements of the decision-making situation.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2-c2-payoff-table}{\textbf{(10)} \textbf{(C2)} Construct the payoff table.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [5] C1 characterization.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2-c1-characterization}
The decision maker is the urban logistics company, in the context of organising its vehicles for one day of deliveries in Bogotá. The alternatives are \(A_1=\) own van fleet and \(A_2=\) third-party provider. The states are \(S_1=\) relatively clear traffic and \(S_2=\) heavy traffic. The completed-delivery quantities are
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
q(A_i,S_j)&S_1&S_2\\ \hline
A_1&80&55\\ A_2&70&60
\end{array}
\]
The alternative-based contributions per delivery are \(v^A(A_1)=0.7\) and \(v^A(A_2)=0.5\); the state-based contributions are \(v^S(S_1)=0.2\) and \(v^S(S_2)=0.1\). The alternative fixed costs are \(F^A(A_1)=8\) and \(F^A(A_2)=13\), while the state fixed costs are \(F^S(S_1)=4\) and \(F^S(S_2)=6\). Monetary values are in thousand USD. The final financial consequence is the payoff after subtracting both fixed costs from the variable contribution. The arrangement is selected before traffic is known.

\secInicio[sec:decision-making-under-uncertainty][sec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2]

\subsubsection*{b) [10] C2 payoff calculations and table.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:urban-delivery-service-c1-c2-c2-payoff-table}
For every cell,
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(A_i,S_j)&=q(A_i,S_j)[v^A(A_i)+v^S(S_j)]\\
&\quad-F^A(A_i)-F^S(S_j).
\end{aligned}
\]
\textit{Own fleet, clear traffic:} combined contribution \(=0.7+0.2=0.9\) per delivery; variable contribution \(=80(0.9)=72\); combined fixed cost \(=8+4=12\). Therefore
\[
P(A_1,S_1)=72-12=60\text{ thousand USD}.
\]
\textit{Own fleet, heavy traffic:} combined contribution \(=0.7+0.1=0.8\); variable contribution \(=55(0.8)=44\); combined fixed cost \(=8+6=14\). Thus
\[
P(A_1,S_2)=44-14=30\text{ thousand USD}.
\]
\textit{Third party, clear traffic:} combined contribution \(=0.5+0.2=0.7\); variable contribution \(=70(0.7)=49\); combined fixed cost \(=13+4=17\). Thus
\[
P(A_2,S_1)=49-17=32\text{ thousand USD}.
\]
\textit{Third party, heavy traffic:} combined contribution \(=0.5+0.1=0.6\); variable contribution \(=60(0.6)=36\); combined fixed cost \(=13+6=19\). Thus
\[
P(A_2,S_2)=36-19=17\text{ thousand USD}.
\]
The completed payoff table, in thousand USD, is
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Alternative & Clear \(S_1\) & Heavy \(S_2\)\\
\midrule
Own fleet \(A_1\)&60&30\\
Third party \(A_2\)&32&17\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{[6] School Lunch Service (C1--C3)}
\label{sec:school-lunch-service-c1-c3}
A school enterprise must decide which lunch menu to offer before it knows how strong student demand will be. The enterprise can prepare either a hot-lunch menu or a sandwich menu, while demand may turn out to be high or low.

If the enterprise chooses the hot-lunch menu and demand is high, it expects total revenue of \(24\) thousand USD and total costs of \(10\) thousand USD. If demand is low, the same menu is expected to generate total revenue of \(15\) thousand USD and total costs of \(9\) thousand USD.

If the enterprise chooses the sandwich menu and demand is high, it expects total revenue of \(21\) thousand USD and total costs of \(12\) thousand USD. If demand is low, the sandwich menu is expected to generate total revenue of \(18\) thousand USD and total costs of \(11\) thousand USD.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:school-lunch-service-c1-c3-c1-characterization}{\textbf{(3)} \textbf{(C1)} Characterize the key elements of the decision-making situation.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:school-lunch-service-c1-c3-c3-payoff-calculations-table}{\textbf{(3)} \textbf{(C3)} Calculate the profit, then construct the payoff table.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [3] C1 characterization.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:school-lunch-service-c1-c3-c1-characterization}
The decision maker is the school enterprise, in the economic context of selecting the lunch service it will prepare. The alternatives are \(A_1=\) hot-lunch menu and \(A_2=\) sandwich menu; the states are \(S_1=\) high demand and \(S_2=\) low demand. For each alternative-state pair, the given total revenue \(R(A_i,S_j)\) and total cost \(C(A_i,S_j)\) determine the profit payoff \(P(A_i,S_j)\), all in thousand USD. The menu is selected before student demand is known, so demand is uncertain at the time of decision.

Under low demand, \(R(A_2,S_2)=18\) and \(C(A_2,S_2)=11\), so

\subsection{[15] Cold-Chain Distribution (C1--C3)}
\label{sec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3}
A food distributor must decide which refrigerated fleet to operate before it knows what demand and fuel conditions will be. The company can choose either a compact refrigerated fleet or a large refrigerated fleet. After this decision is made, market and fuel conditions may turn out to be favourable, standard, or adverse.

The number of delivery batches that can be handled depends on both the fleet selected and the conditions that occur. With the compact refrigerated fleet, the distributor expects to handle eight hundred delivery batches under favourable conditions, six hundred under standard conditions, and three hundred under adverse conditions. With the large refrigerated fleet, it expects to handle eleven hundred delivery batches under favourable conditions, seven hundred under standard conditions, and four hundred under adverse conditions.

Revenue from each hundred delivery batches depends partly on the fleet selected. The compact refrigerated fleet generates twelve thousand USD per hundred batches, while the large refrigerated fleet generates sixteen thousand USD per hundred batches. Revenue is also affected by the conditions that occur. Favourable conditions add three thousand USD of revenue per hundred batches, standard conditions add two thousand USD, and adverse conditions add one thousand USD.

The fleet choice also creates variable costs for each hundred delivery batches. The compact refrigerated fleet has a variable cost of five thousand USD per hundred batches, while the large refrigerated fleet has a variable cost of seven thousand USD per hundred batches. Conditions create an additional variable cost as well. Favourable conditions add one thousand USD of cost per hundred batches, standard conditions add two thousand USD, and adverse conditions add four thousand USD.

In addition to these variable amounts, the fleet selected generates fixed revenues and fixed costs. Choosing the compact refrigerated fleet provides sixteen thousand USD in fixed revenue and creates twenty thousand USD in fixed costs. Choosing the large refrigerated fleet provides twenty-five thousand USD in fixed revenue and creates thirty-five thousand USD in fixed costs.

The conditions that occur also generate fixed financial effects. Favourable conditions provide twelve thousand USD in additional fixed revenue and create six thousand USD in additional fixed costs. Standard conditions provide eight thousand USD in additional fixed revenue and create ten thousand USD in additional fixed costs. Adverse conditions provide four thousand USD in additional fixed revenue and create fourteen thousand USD in additional fixed costs.

For each possible combination of fleet choice and conditions, total revenue is obtained by combining the revenue earned from the corresponding number of delivery batches with the fixed revenues associated with both the fleet and the conditions. Total cost is obtained in the same way by combining the variable costs generated by the corresponding number of delivery batches with the fixed costs associated with both the fleet and the conditions.
\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3-c1-characterization}{\textbf{(5)} \textbf{(C1)} Characterize the key elements of the decision-making situation.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3-c3-payoff-calculations-table}{\textbf{(10)} \textbf{(C3)} Calculate the profit, then construct the payoff table.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [5] C1 characterization.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3-c1-characterization}
The decision maker is the food distributor, in the context of choosing refrigerated capacity for cold-chain distribution. Let \(A_1=\) compact refrigerated fleet and \(A_2=\) large refrigerated fleet. The states are \(S_1=\) favourable, \(S_2=\) standard, and \(S_3=\) adverse market and fuel conditions. The handled quantities depend on both the fleet and the state.

Because every variable rate is per hundred batches, the quantities used in the formulas are hundreds of batches:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
q(A_i,S_j)&S_1&S_2&S_3\\ \hline
A_1&800/100=8&600/100=6&300/100=3\\
A_2&1100/100=11&700/100=7&400/100=4
\end{array}
\]
In thousand USD per hundred batches, alternative revenue rates are \(r^A(A_1)=12\), \(r^A(A_2)=16\), and state revenue rates are \(r^S(S_1)=3\), \(r^S(S_2)=2\), \(r^S(S_3)=1\). Alternative cost rates are \(c^A(A_1)=5\), \(c^A(A_2)=7\), and state cost rates are \(c^S(S_1)=1\), \(c^S(S_2)=2\), \(c^S(S_3)=4\).

Alternative fixed revenues are \(F^{R,A}(A_1)=16\), \(F^{R,A}(A_2)=25\), and state fixed revenues are \(F^{R,S}(S_1)=12\), \(F^{R,S}(S_2)=8\), \(F^{R,S}(S_3)=4\). Alternative fixed costs are \(F^{C,A}(A_1)=20\), \(F^{C,A}(A_2)=35\), and state fixed costs are \(F^{C,S}(S_1)=6\), \(F^{C,S}(S_2)=10\), \(F^{C,S}(S_3)=14\). Fixed amounts are in thousand USD. The final consequence is profit \(P(A_i,S_j)\). The fleet is chosen before future demand and fuel conditions are known.

\secInicio[sec:decision-making-under-uncertainty][sec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3]

\subsubsection*{b) [10] C3 payoff calculations and table.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:cold-chain-distribution-c1-c3-c3-payoff-calculations-table}
For each pair,
\[
\begin{aligned}
R&=q[r^A(A_i)+r^S(S_j)]\\
 &\quad+F^{R,A}(A_i)+F^{R,S}(S_j),
\end{aligned}
\]
\[
\begin{aligned}
C&=q[c^A(A_i)+c^S(S_j)]\\
 &\quad+F^{C,A}(A_i)+F^{C,S}(S_j),\\
P&=R-C.
\end{aligned}
\]
All following monetary results are in thousand USD.

\textit{Compact / favourable \((A_1,S_1)\).} Here \(q=800/100=8\). The combined revenue rate is \(12+3=15\), so variable revenue is \(8(15)=120\), and
\[R(A_1,S_1)=120+16+12=148.\]
The combined cost rate is \(5+1=6\), so variable cost is \(8(6)=48\), and
\[C(A_1,S_1)=48+20+6=74.\]
Therefore \(P(A_1,S_1)=148-74=74\) thousand USD.

\textit{Compact / standard \((A_1,S_2)\).} Here \(q=600/100=6\). The combined revenue rate is \(12+2=14\), variable revenue is \(6(14)=84\), and
\[R(A_1,S_2)=84+16+8=108.\]
The combined cost rate is \(5+2=7\), variable cost is \(6(7)=42\), and
\[C(A_1,S_2)=42+20+10=72.\]
Therefore \(P(A_1,S_2)=108-72=36\) thousand USD.

\textit{Compact / adverse \((A_1,S_3)\).} Here \(q=300/100=3\). The combined revenue rate is \(12+1=13\), variable revenue is \(3(13)=39\), and
\[R(A_1,S_3)=39+16+4=59.\]
The combined cost rate is \(5+4=9\), variable cost is \(3(9)=27\), and
\[C(A_1,S_3)=27+20+14=61.\]
Therefore \(P(A_1,S_3)=59-61=-2\) thousand USD, a loss of \(2\) thousand USD.

\textit{Large / favourable \((A_2,S_1)\).} Here \(q=1100/100=11\). The combined revenue rate is \(16+3=19\), variable revenue is \(11(19)=209\), and
\[R(A_2,S_1)=209+25+12=246.\]
The combined cost rate is \(7+1=8\), variable cost is \(11(8)=88\), and
\[C(A_2,S_1)=88+35+6=129.\]
Therefore \(P(A_2,S_1)=246-129=117\) thousand USD.

\textit{Large / standard \((A_2,S_2)\).} Here \(q=700/100=7\). The combined revenue rate is \(16+2=18\), variable revenue is \(7(18)=126\), and
\[R(A_2,S_2)=126+25+8=159.\]
The combined cost rate is \(7+2=9\), variable cost is \(7(9)=63\), and
\[C(A_2,S_2)=63+35+10=108.\]
Therefore \(P(A_2,S_2)=159-108=51\) thousand USD.

\textit{Large / adverse \((A_2,S_3)\).} Here \(q=400/100=4\). The combined revenue rate is \(16+1=17\), variable revenue is \(4(17)=68\), and
\[R(A_2,S_3)=68+25+4=97.\]
The combined cost rate is \(7+4=11\), variable cost is \(4(11)=44\), and
\[C(A_2,S_3)=44+35+14=93.\]
Therefore \(P(A_2,S_3)=97-93=4\) thousand USD.

The completed payoff table, in thousand USD, is
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Alternative & Favourable & Standard & Adverse\\
\midrule
Compact \(A_1\)&74&36&-2\\
Large \(A_2\)&117&51&4\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{[2] Mobile Produce Shop (C4)}
\label{sec:mobile-produce-shop-c4}
A produce seller must locate a mobile shop at the business district ($A_1$) or the residential district ($A_2$). Demand may be high ($S_1$) or low ($S_2$). Profits, in thousand USD, are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ \\
\midrule
Business district & 48 & 12 \\
Residential district & 35 & 24 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:mobile-produce-shop-c4-maximax}{\textbf{(1)} Apply Maximax and state the selected location.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:mobile-produce-shop-c4-maximin}{\textbf{(1)} Apply Maximin and state the selected location.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [1] Maximax.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:mobile-produce-shop-c4-maximax}
This optimistic criterion selects
\(\max_i[\max_j P(A_i,S_j)]\). The row maxima are
\[
\max_jP(A_1,S_j)=\max\{48,12\}=48,
\]
\[
\max_jP(A_2,S_j)=\max\{35,24\}=35.
\]
Comparing them gives \(\max\{48,35\}=48\). Therefore Maximax selects the \textbf{business district}, because an optimistic decision maker chooses the alternative with the greatest possible profit: \(48\) thousand USD.

\secInicio[sec:decision-making-under-uncertainty][sec:mobile-produce-shop-c4]

\subsubsection*{b) [1] Maximin.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:mobile-produce-shop-c4-maximin}
This conservative criterion selects
\(\max_i[\min_j P(A_i,S_j)]\). The row minima are
\[
\min_jP(A_1,S_j)=\min\{48,12\}=12,
\]
\[
\min_jP(A_2,S_j)=\min\{35,24\}=24.
\]
Comparing them gives \(\max\{12,24\}=24\). Therefore Maximin selects the \textbf{residential district}, because a conservative decision maker chooses the best worst-case profit: \(24\) thousand USD.

\subsection{[8] Warehouse Technology (C4)}
\label{sec:warehouse-technology-c4}
A wholesaler must choose one of four warehouse systems before market and supply conditions are known. The possible states are strong demand ($S_1$), steady demand ($S_2$), and weak demand with delays ($S_3$). The profits, in thousand USD, are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ & $S_3$ \\
\midrule
Automated system & 96 & 52 & 18 \\
Modular system & 78 & 61 & 42 \\
Robotic system & 88 & 48 & 30 \\
Flexible system & 72 & 64 & 50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Questions/Tasks.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*)}]
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:warehouse-technology-c4-maximax}{\textbf{(4)} Apply Maximax and state the selected system.}
    \item \TaskHyperref{subsec:warehouse-technology-c4-maximin}{\textbf{(4)} Apply Maximin and state the selected system.}
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection*{a) [4] Maximax.}
\phantomsection
\label{subsec:warehouse-technology-c4-maximax}
Determine the best payoff for each system:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\max_jP(A_1,S_j)=\max\{96,52,18\}=96,\\
\max_jP(A_2,S_j)=\max\{78,61,42\}=78,\\
\max_jP(A_3,S_j)=\max\{88,48,30\}=88,\\
\max_jP(A_4,S_j)=\max\{72,64,50\}=72.
\end{array}
\]
Thus
\[
\max_i[\max_jP(A_i,S_j)]=\max\{96,78,88,72\}=96.
\]
Maximax selects the \textbf{automated system}. This is the optimistic decision because it pursues the greatest possible profit, \(96\) thousand USD.

	\subsection{A Surprising Connection}
	\label{sec:surprising-connection}
	
	On Monday, Maya helps a café decide whether to install a small or large
	production capacity before customer demand is known. Across town, Leo advises
	a farmer who must plant crop A or crop B before the season turns dry or rainy.
	Meanwhile, Sara compares two investment projects before the market proves weak
	or strong.
	
	At first, the problems seem unrelated: coffee cups, crops, and investments have
	different prices, risks, units, and consequences. Those are the story's
	\emph{clothing}. Look beneath it, however, and the same mathematical skeleton
	appears:
	\[
	\boxed{\text{choice before uncertainty}}
	\; + \;
	\boxed{\text{possible states}}
	\; + \;
	\boxed{\text{payoff for each combination}}.
	\]
	This pattern also appears when a company selects a production plan, a family
	evaluates a financial decision, a school enterprise plans output, or a
	government selects a policy. Recognising it is the super tool called
	\textbf{abstraction}.

	\subsection{From Different Stories to One Notation}
	\label{sec:one-notation}
	
	The nouns change, but their mathematical \emph{roles} stay the same. A
	decision-maker chooses an alternative \(A_i\), an uncertain state \(S_j\)
	occurs, and the combination determines the payoff \(P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)\).
	
	For example, Maya's ``large capacity'', Leo's ``crop B'', and Sara's
	``project B'' may all be labelled \(A_2\). They are not the same real-world
	choice; they simply occupy the same role in their respective decision models.
	
	The same abstraction works even when the number of alternatives or states
	changes:

	to represent any number of alternatives and states.

	\subsection{C1: Identify the Decision Structure}
	\label{sec:c1-decision-structure}
	
	Start by asking: Who makes the decision? What is the economic or financial
	context? What can that person or organisation choose? What uncertain event
	occurs \emph{after} the choice? Which states can it produce? What consequence
	belongs to each alternative--state pair? In what units will it be measured?
	
	Write the \(m\) alternatives and \(n\) states as
	\[
	A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_m
	\qquad\text{and}\qquad
	S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_n.
	\]
	
	The order is important:
	\[
	\text{Choose }A_i
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	\text{uncertainty is resolved as }S_j
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	\text{consequence and payoff }P_{ay}(A_i,S_j).
	\]
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The decision-maker controls the choice of \(A_i\).
		\item The decision-maker does not control which \(S_j\) occurs.
		\item The pair \((A_i,S_j)\) determines a consequence.
		\item The payoff assigns an economic or financial value to that consequence.
	\end{itemize}
	
	Once these elements have been identified, record them in a
	\verb|\DecisionElementsTable|. The table separates the four pieces needed
	before constructing a payoff table:
	\[
	\boxed{\text{alternatives}}
	\qquad
	\boxed{\text{states}}
	\qquad
	\boxed{\text{uncertain event}}
	\qquad
	\boxed{\text{consequence and units}}.
	\]
	
	For example, suppose Maya's café must choose between a small-capacity plan
	and a large-capacity plan before next month's customer demand is known.
	Demand may be low or high. Each capacity--demand pair produces a monthly
	profit measured in thousand USD.
	
	\DecisionElementsTable
	{Small capacity $A_1$; Large capacity $A_2$}
	{Low demand $S_1$; High demand $S_2$}
	{Customer demand after the capacity plan is selected}
	{Monthly profit in thousand USD for each capacity--demand pair}
	
	The table makes the abstraction explicit:
	\[
	A_i=\text{capacity choice},
	\qquad
	S_j=\text{realised demand},
	\qquad
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=\text{monthly profit}.
	\]
	
	A \emph{consequence} may first be expressed in words. For example,
	``the large café has unused equipment when demand is low.'' Its numerical
	\emph{payoff} might then be a profit of \(-2\) thousand USD. The words
	describe what happens; the payoff gives that consequence a value that can
	later be placed in the payoff table. The units are part of that value.

	\subsection{C2: Give the Structure a Table}
	\label{sec:c2-payoff-table}
	
	The payoff table organizes the decision structure identified in C1.
	\textbf{Alternatives go in rows}, \textbf{states go in columns}, and each
	cell contains the payoff associated with one alternative--state pair
	\((A_i,S_j)\).
	
	The general structure is:
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
			\toprule
			Alternative
			& $S_1$
			& $S_2$
			& $\cdots$
			& $S_n$ \\
			\midrule
			$A_1$
			& $P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)$
			& $P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)$
			& $\cdots$
			& $P_{ay}(A_1,S_n)$ \\
			
			$A_2$
			& $P_{ay}(A_2,S_1)$
			& $P_{ay}(A_2,S_2)$
			& $\cdots$
			& $P_{ay}(A_2,S_n)$ \\
			
			$\vdots$
			& $\vdots$
			& $\vdots$
			& $\ddots$
			& $\vdots$ \\
			
			$A_m$
			& $P_{ay}(A_m,S_1)$
			& $P_{ay}(A_m,S_2)$
			& $\cdots$
			& $P_{ay}(A_m,S_n)$ \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	Thus,
	\[
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)
	\]
	is the payoff obtained when alternative \(A_i\) is chosen and state
	\(S_j\) occurs.
	
	The table has \(m\) alternative rows and \(n\) state columns. Therefore,
	there are
	\[
	mn
	\]
	alternative--state pairs, and each pair must have exactly one payoff.
	
	For a problem with two alternatives and two states, the payoff-table
	skeleton is:
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{lcc}
			\toprule
			Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ \\
			\midrule
			$A_1$ & $P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)$ & $P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)$ \\
			$A_2$ & $P_{ay}(A_2,S_1)$ & $P_{ay}(A_2,S_2)$ \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	Once the payoffs have been calculated or supplied, they replace the
	symbolic entries. For example:
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{lcc}
			\toprule
			Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ \\
			\midrule
			$A_1$ & 10 & 4 \\
			$A_2$ & 7 & 8 \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	Every numerical entry must use the same payoff meaning and compatible
	units. The table is complete only when every pair \((A_i,S_j)\) has exactly
	one corresponding value.

	\subsection{C3: Build the Value in Each Cell}
	\label{sec:c3-cell-value}
	
	In C2, each payoff-table cell was identified as
	\[
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j).
	\]
	C3 determines the numerical value that goes into that cell.
	
	For each pair \((A_i,S_j)\), ask first:
	\[
	\text{Is the payoff given directly, or must it be calculated?}
	\]
	
	If the payoff is supplied directly, place that value in the corresponding
	cell. If it must be constructed from payoff components, use
	\[
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)
	=
	q(A_i,S_j)
	\left[
	v(A_i)+v(S_j)
	\right]
	+
	f(A_i)
	+
	f(S_j).
	\]
	Here,
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \(q(A_i,S_j)\) is the quantity associated with the cell;
		\item \(v(A_i)\) is a variable per-unit payoff component determined by the alternative;
		\item \(v(S_j)\) is a variable per-unit payoff component determined by the state;
		\item \(f(A_i)\) is a fixed payoff component determined by the alternative;
		\item \(f(S_j)\) is a fixed payoff component determined by the state.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The result is one complete value for one payoff-table cell:
	\[
	(A_i,S_j)
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	\text{insert in row }A_i\text{, column }S_j.
	\]
	
	When the payoff represents \emph{profit}, construct revenue and cost
	separately:
	\[
	P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=R(A_i,S_j)-C(A_i,S_j).
	\]
	
	A general revenue model is
	\[
	R(A_i,S_j)
	=
	q(A_i,S_j)
	\left[
	r(A_i)+r(S_j)
	\right]
	+
	f_r(A_i)
	+
	f_r(S_j),
	\]
	while a general cost model is
	\[
	C(A_i,S_j)
	=
	q(A_i,S_j)
	\left[
	c(A_i)+c(S_j)
	\right]
	+
	f_c(A_i)
	+
	f_c(S_j).
	\]
	
	Therefore, the workflow for a profit cell is
	\[
	\boxed{\text{calculate }R(A_i,S_j)}
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	\boxed{\text{calculate }C(A_i,S_j)}
	\quad\longrightarrow\quad
	\boxed{P(A_i,S_j)=R(A_i,S_j)-C(A_i,S_j)}.
	\]
	
	For example, the cell in row \(A_1\) and column \(S_2\) is
	\[
	P(A_1,S_2)=R(A_1,S_2)-C(A_1,S_2).
	\]
	Once this value is calculated, it is placed directly in the corresponding
	position of the payoff table:
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{lcc}
			\toprule
			Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ \\
			\midrule
			$A_1$ & $P(A_1,S_1)$ & $\boxed{P(A_1,S_2)}$ \\
			$A_2$ & $P(A_2,S_1)$ & $P(A_2,S_2)$ \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	Repeat the same process until every cell contains one payoff. When using these formulas:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item use only the components given in the context;
		\item multiply only per-unit values by quantity;
		\item keep fixed values separate from quantity;
		\item keep all values in compatible units;
		\item calculate revenue and cost separately before subtracting.
	\end{itemize}

	
	
	The objective of C3 is therefore not to choose an alternative. It is to
	complete the payoff table constructed in C2 by assigning exactly one
	correctly calculated payoff to every pair \((A_i,S_j)\).

	\subsection{C4: View One Table Through Two Decision Lenses}
	\label{sec:c4-decision-lenses}
	
	C1--C3 construct a valid model and completed table. C4 asks how to compare its
	rows when no probabilities are supplied. The two criteria below answer
	different questions; neither is universally correct.
	
	\subsubsection*{The Optimistic Lens: Maximax}
	\phantomsection
	\label{subsec:maximax-lens}
	Maximax selects the largest possible payoff. First define the row maximum for
	alternative \(A_i\) by
	\[
	M_i=\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}P(A_i,S_j).
	\]
	Then select the alternative (or alternatives) with the largest row maximum:
	\[
	A_{\text{Maximax}}=
	\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}
	\left\{\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}P(A_i,S_j)\right\}.
	\]
	
	\subsubsection*{The Protective Lens: Maximin}
	\phantomsection
	\label{subsec:maximin-lens}
	Maximin selects the largest worst-case payoff. First define the row minimum for
	alternative \(A_i\) by
	\[
	m_i=\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}P(A_i,S_j).
	\]
	Then select the alternative (or alternatives) with the largest row minimum:
	\[
	A_{\text{Maximin}}=
	\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}
	\left\{\min_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}P(A_i,S_j)\right\}.
	\]
	
	One small table makes the two lenses visible. Suppose Maya's café profits are
	in \textbf{thousand USD}:
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{lrr}
			\toprule
			Alternative & Low demand \(S_1\) & High demand \(S_2\)\\
			\midrule
			Small capacity \(A_1\) & 6 & 14\\
			Large capacity \(A_2\) & -2 & 22\\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=8mm and 10mm]
			\node[motnode,minimum width=3.4cm,minimum height=1.2cm] (table)
			{one completed\\payoff table};
			\node[motnode,above right=5mm and 12mm of table,fill=motgold!12] (max)
			{optimistic lens\\row maxima: \((14,22)\)};
			\node[motnode,below right=5mm and 12mm of table,fill=motgreen!12] (min)
			{protective lens\\row minima: \((6,-2)\)};
			\node[motnode,right=of max] (mx) {Maximax\\selects \(A_2\)};
			\node[motnode,right=of min] (mn) {Maximin\\selects \(A_1\)};
			\draw[motarrow] (table)--(max); \draw[motarrow] (max)--(mx);
			\draw[motarrow] (table)--(min); \draw[motarrow] (min)--(mn);
		\end{tikzpicture}
	\end{center}
	
	Thus, the optimistic lens favours large capacity's upside, while the protective
	lens favours small capacity's stronger worst case. If two alternatives share
	the relevant row maximum under Maximax or row minimum under Maximin, report a
	\textbf{tie}; do not choose one arbitrarily.

	\subsection{Zoom Back into the Real World}
	\label{sec:real-world-interpretation}
	
	A calculation is not yet a complete recommendation. Maximax focuses on upside
	and may overlook severe downside. Maximin protects against the worst payoff
	represented in the model but may reject attractive opportunities. Neither rule
	uses probabilities. Maya should therefore say more than ``\(A_2\)'': she
	should name ``large café capacity'', state the optimistic attitude, note the
	\(-2\) thousand USD downside, and explain the assumptions behind the payoffs.
	
	Leo and Sara must do the same zoom back in. What does the selected crop imply
	for the farm? What risk does the project expose the investor to? Are the states
	complete and the monetary estimates reasonable? A model is only as useful as
	its alternatives, states, payoffs, units, and assumptions. A criterion is a
	transparent decision lens, not an automatic declaration of the one universally
	correct choice. Other criteria exist, but they are outside our C1--C4 journey.

	\subsection{A Reusable Checklist}
	\label{sec:reusable-checklist}
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=2em]
		\item What decision is being made?
		\item What are the alternatives \(A_i\)?
		\item What uncertain event occurs after the decision?
		\item What are the states \(S_j\)?
		\item What consequence belongs to each \((A_i,S_j)\)?
		\item What are the units?
		\item Are payoffs supplied, or must revenue and cost be calculated?
		\item Is every table cell complete?
		\item Are Maximax or Maximin appropriate for the stated attitude?
		\item What does the result mean in the original context?
		\item What risks, assumptions, ties, or limitations must be reported?
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Final Takeaway}
	\label{sec:final-takeaway}
	\begin{bigidea}[Keep the skeleton; restore the meaning]
		\centering\large
		The context gives the decision meaning. Abstraction gives it structure. The
		payoff table gives it visibility. A decision criterion gives it a transparent
		rule for comparison. Interpretation brings the mathematics back to the real
		world.
	\end{bigidea}
	
	The stories will keep changing. Your super tool can travel with you.

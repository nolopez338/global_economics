\section{Introduction}\label{sec:c8-introduction}
All problems use \textbf{profit payoffs}: larger values are better, and negative values represent losses. Alternatives $A_i$ occupy rows; mutually exclusive states of nature $S_j$ occupy columns. The payoff $P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)$ is the profit if $A_i$ is chosen and $S_j$ occurs. A decision is made before the state is known.

\subsection{Four ways to compare the same alternatives}
\textbf{Maximax} selects the alternative with the highest row maximum. It asks, ``Which alternative offers the greatest possible profit?'' \textbf{Maximin} selects the alternative with the highest row minimum. It asks, ``Which alternative has the best worst-case profit?'' Neither needs probabilities, even when probabilities are available.

\textbf{Minimax Regret} first compares alternatives within each state:
\[
b(S_j)=\max_i P_{ay}(A_i,S_j),\qquad
r(A_i,S_j)=b(S_j)-P_{ay}(A_i,S_j).
\]
Regret is the profit forgone compared with the best alternative for the state that actually occurs; it is not necessarily a cash loss. Select the alternative with the \emph{smallest maximum regret}. This criterion needs the full payoff table, but no probabilities.

\textbf{EMV/Bayes} selects the highest Expected Monetary Value:
\[
EMV(A_i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(S_j)P_{ay}(A_i,S_j),\qquad
P(S_j)\geq0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(S_j)=1.
\]
It requires a probability for every state as well as the payoffs. In these problems the state probabilities are the same for every alternative. Maximizing expected money expresses a risk-neutral orientation: it does not directly penalize uncertainty or protect against a large loss. An EMV is a weighted average, not a guaranteed profit or necessarily an attainable single outcome.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.15\textwidth}*{3}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}@{}}
\toprule
Criterion & Information and selection & Risk orientation & Main limitation\\
\midrule
Maximax & Payoffs; highest row maximum & Optimistic; pursues upside & Ignores other outcomes and their likelihoods.\\
Maximin & Payoffs; highest row minimum & Conservative; protects the worst payoff & Ignores upside and how unlikely the worst state may be.\\
Minimax Regret & Full payoff table; smallest row maximum regret & Cautious about missed opportunities & Ignores likelihoods; depends on the alternatives included.\\
EMV/Bayes & Payoffs and valid probabilities; highest EMV & Risk-neutral about money & Sensitive to probability estimates; may recommend an unaffordable downside.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Criteria can disagree because they compare different summaries of the \emph{same} information. Agreement does not mean that their reasoning is identical. Report all tied alternatives; do not invent a tie-breaking rule. Without probability information, EMV cannot be calculated unless an additional probability assumption is explicitly justified. Equal probabilities are not implied by uncertainty.

\textbf{How to complete the numerical problems.} The summary tables are already completed; do not recalculate row maxima, row minima, regrets, or EMVs. Identify the recommended alternative(s) by comparing the appropriate summary column. In the answer table, record the winning summary value and name the quantity it represents (for example, ``Row minimum: 35''). Then justify and compare the recommendations using the supplied summaries and the original payoffs.

	\[
	A_1:-10,\qquad A_2:20+s,\qquad A_3:35.

	\[
	20+s>35
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	s>15.
	\]

	\[
	\boxed{s=16}.
	\]

	\[
	A_2:36,\qquad A_3:35.
	\]

At $s=16$, eligible payoffs are $(60,55,36)$ and $(40,45,35)$. \subsection{Choosing a Criterion}\label{sec:c8-match}
Which criterion fits each \emph{stated priority}? Give its required information and one limitation, and briefly contrast another feasible criterion.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A start-up knows its payoffs but lacks reliable probabilities. Which criterion suits a founder seeking maximum upside and accepting the downside?
\item A family business has reliable probabilities. Which criterion suits its priority of the highest worst-case profit?
\item A delivery manager lacks reliable probabilities. Which criterion minimizes the greatest profit forgone relative to the best plan in each state?
\item A retailer trusts its probabilities and makes repeated seasonal decisions. Which criterion suits its priority of average monetary profit?
\item A museum knows its payoffs but lacks attendance probabilities. What information is missing for an EMV choice, and why would equal probabilities be an assumption?
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Solution}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Maximax fits the optimistic priority and needs only payoffs, but ignores downside outcomes. Maximin is also feasible and instead protects the worst payoff.
\item Maximin fits the conservative priority and needs only payoffs, but ignores upside and likelihoods. EMV/Bayes is feasible but prioritizes expected profit.
\item Minimax Regret needs the full payoff table, without probabilities, but may sacrifice worst-case protection. Maximin is feasible and instead protects the minimum profit.
\item EMV/Bayes fits the risk-neutral priority and needs payoffs and reliable probabilities, but cannot guarantee a season's profit. Maximin is feasible and instead protects the worst outcome.
\item A nonnegative probability for each attendance state, summing to one, is missing. Equal probabilities would assume equally likely states without evidence. The three payoff criteria remain feasible but answer different priorities.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluating Decision Claims}\label{sec:c8-critique}
Is each claim valid? Correct it if needed, using a numerical example where possible.
\begin{enumerate}
\item ``Maximin and Minimax Regret always agree because both are cautious.''
\item ``An EMV of 55 guarantees a seasonal profit of 55.''
\item ``Regret of 25 means an actual monetary loss of 25.''
\item ``Agreement with Maximax makes Bayes optimistic.''
\item ``Valid probabilities make EMV best for every decision-maker.''
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Solution}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Invalid. In delivery expansion, Maximin selects $A_3$ (minimum 35); Minimax Regret selects $A_2$ (maximum regret 25, minimum 10). Worst-case protection and limited missed opportunities are different priorities.
\item Invalid. Delivery plan $A_4$ has EMV 55 but can earn only 60, 65, or 20. Expected profit is not guaranteed profit.
\item Invalid. With low festival attendance, $A_1$ earns 10 but has regret $35-10=25$. Regret measures forgone profit, not an actual loss.
\item Invalid. Both choose investment $A_1$, but Maximax uses its maximum of 90 and Bayes its EMV of 55.75. Bayes remains risk-neutral.
\item Invalid. The farm's EMV choice $A_2$ has minimum 20 and fails the required 30. Only $A_3$, with minimum 35 and EMV 40.5, meets the obligation despite valid probabilities.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Decision Committee Report}\label{sec:c8-report}
Return to the delivery expansion problem in Section~\ref{sec:c8-worked}. Four committee members prioritize, respectively, the largest possible profit, the best worst-case profit, the smallest maximum missed opportunity, and the highest expected profit.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Which criterion and plan fit each member's priority? Give the deciding value and information used or ignored.
\item Which plan would you recommend? Compare two plans numerically, state your priority and one limitation, and explain a reasonable disagreement.
\item If the forecast is withdrawn, which summaries remain usable and which recommendation loses support? Does one remaining criterion become universally best?
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Solution}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the stated order: Maximax chooses $A_1$ (row maximum 100), ignoring downside and probabilities; Maximin chooses $A_3$ (row minimum 35), ignoring upside and probabilities. Minimax Regret chooses $A_2$ (maximum regret 25), comparing all payoffs but ignoring likelihoods; EMV/Bayes chooses $A_4$ (EMV 55), using payoffs and probabilities without protecting the worst outcome.
\item One valid report is: ``We prioritize expected profit and recommend $A_4$: its EMV is 55 versus 47 for $A_2$. This depends on reliable probabilities and does not guarantee 55. A member prioritizing missed opportunities may prefer $A_2$, with maximum regret 25 versus 40 for $A_4$.'' Other choices are valid with a stated priority, numerical support, and a limitation.
\item Row maxima, row minima, and maximum regrets remain usable, retaining choices $A_1$, $A_3$, and $A_2$, respectively. The EMVs and Bayes choice $A_4$ lose their forecast basis. No remaining criterion becomes universally best: each serves a different priority.
\end{enumerate}

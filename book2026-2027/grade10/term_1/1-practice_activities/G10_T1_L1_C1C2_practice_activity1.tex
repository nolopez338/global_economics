	\section*{Evaluated criteria}
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}]
		\item Characterises decision-making problems under uncertainty by identifying decision alternatives, states of nature, consequences, and the economic or financial context of the decision.
		\item Organises decision problems using payoff tables, placing alternatives, states of nature, and payoffs in a clear structure for comparison.
	\end{enumerate}

	\section{Introduction}
	\label{sec:introduction}

	\SectionProblemsTableOfContents{
		\SectionProblemLink{sec:problem1-introductory-general-problem-c1-c2}{Introductory General Problem (C1--C2)} \\
		\SectionProblemLink{sec:problem2-advanced-general-problem-c1-c2}{Advanced General Problem (C1--C2)}
	}\vspace{5mm}
	
	A decision problem under uncertainty describes a choice that must be made before an uncertain event is known. The decision-maker chooses one \emph{alternative}; later, the uncertain event produces one \emph{state of nature}. A \emph{consequence} describes what happens for that alternative--state combination, and a \emph{payoff} is the numerical value assigned to the consequence. Thus, consequences describe outcomes in words, while payoffs express their economic or financial values in numbers.

	Let
	\[
		A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_m
	\]
	be the available decision alternatives, where $i$ identifies a particular alternative, and let
	\[
		S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_n
	\]
	be the possible states of nature, where $j$ identifies a particular state. The decision-maker chooses one $A_i$, and the uncertain event subsequently produces one $S_j$. The pair $(A_i,S_j)$ produces one consequence, whose numerical payoff is $P_{ij}$. Alternatives form the rows of a payoff table and states form its columns, so
	\[
		\mathbf{P}=
		\begin{pmatrix}
			P_{11} & P_{12} & \cdots & P_{1n}\\
			P_{21} & P_{22} & \cdots & P_{2n}\\
			\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
			P_{m1} & P_{m2} & \cdots & P_{mn}
		\end{pmatrix}.
	\]
	In particular, $P_{ij}$ means the payoff obtained when alternative $A_i$ is selected and state $S_j$ occurs.

	More advanced problems use the following payoff components.
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{cl}
			\toprule
			Symbol & Meaning \\
			\midrule
			$q_{ij}$ & Number of items for $A_i$ under $S_j$ \\
			$v_i^A$ & Variable payoff per item determined by $A_i$ \\
			$v_j^S$ & Variable payoff per item determined by $S_j$ \\
			$f_i^A$ & Fixed payoff determined by $A_i$ \\
			$f_j^S$ & Fixed payoff determined by $S_j$ \\
			$P_{ij}$ & Total payoff for the pair $(A_i,S_j)$ \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	The complete four-component structure is
	\[
		P_{ij}=q_{ij}\left(v_i^A+v_j^S\right)+f_i^A+f_j^S.
	\]
	Here, $q_{ij}v_i^A$ is the variable payoff generated by the alternative-based rate; $q_{ij}v_j^S$ is the variable payoff generated by the state-based rate; $f_i^A$ is the alternative-based fixed component; and $f_j^S$ is the state-based fixed component. Adding all applicable components gives $P_{ij}$. A component with $i$ changes by alternative (and therefore by row), one with $j$ changes by state (and therefore by column), and one with both $i$ and $j$, such as $q_{ij}$, can change for every cell.

	Simpler structures occur when components are absent; an absent component can be treated as zero. For example, $P_{ij}=q_{ij}v_i^A$ uses only an alternative-based variable component, while $P_{ij}=f_i^A+f_j^S$ uses only the two fixed components. Directly supplied payoffs need no decomposition: when $P_{ij}$ is already given, it is placed directly in its payoff-table cell. The direct-payoff form and the complete four-component form therefore describe the same table structure; they differ only in whether the cell value is supplied or calculated.

	A reusable procedure is:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
		\item Identify the decision and its economic or financial context.
		\item List the available alternatives $A_i$.
		\item Identify the uncertain event.
		\item List its possible states $S_j$.
		\item Determine the consequence for every pair $(A_i,S_j)$.
		\item Identify which payoff values depend on the alternative and which depend on the state.
		\item Calculate each $P_{ij}$ using the appropriate formula, unless it is supplied directly.
		\item Place alternatives in rows and states in columns.
		\item Check that every alternative--state pair has exactly one payoff.
		\item Check that all quantities and payoff components use compatible units.
	\end{enumerate}
	The purpose is to convert a verbal decision situation into a complete, comparable payoff table. At this stage, the purpose is not to select the best alternative.

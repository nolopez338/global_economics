	\subsection{Main Ideas and Concepts}
\label{sec:main-ideas-and-concepts}

\subsubsection*{1. Start with the choice and its timing}
A school enterprise offers a printing service for a school fair. Its student team must decide whether to offer a standard packet service or a colour packet service. The team must advertise one service before it knows how much demand there will be. First it chooses a service; later it learns whether demand is high or low.

The team is the \emph{decision-maker}, and the two services are its \emph{alternatives}. The uncertain process is how demand develops after advertising. High demand and low demand are the two possible \emph{states of nature}: the possible outcomes of that process. In this simplified model, exactly one of those states occurs. The team controls its service choice, but it does not control the demand state.

A \emph{consequence} tells us what a choice and a demand state mean for the enterprise. For example, offering standard packets under high demand could leave the team with money to buy supplies for its next project. A \emph{payoff} measures the financial result in money. A consequence therefore explains what happens and why it matters; a payoff gives its numerical financial value.

\textbf{Check.} Is ``high demand'' the team's choice or an outcome of the uncertain process? \emph{Suggested answer:} It is a possible outcome, or state. The team chooses the service before knowing the state.

\subsubsection*{2. Give the choices short names}
Short names help us label tables without repeatedly writing long service descriptions. Call the standard packet service $A_1$, read ``alternative one'', and the colour packet service $A_2$, read ``alternative two''.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule Symbol & Choice in the school enterprise \\ \midrule
$A_1$ & Offer the standard packet service \\
$A_2$ & Offer the colour packet service \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The small number below the letter is a \emph{subscript}. It identifies a choice; it does not give a score or rank. For example, writing $A_2$ on a row means that the row is about the colour service.

\textbf{Check.} Does the 2 in $A_2$ mean that this service earns twice as much? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. It is only the service's identifying number.

\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{3. Give the possible demand outcomes short names}
We also need short column labels for the possible demand outcomes. Call high demand $S_1$, read ``state one'', and low demand $S_2$, read ``state two''.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule Symbol & Demand outcome \\ \midrule
$S_1$ & High demand \\
$S_2$ & Low demand \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Thus, if demand turns out to be low, we record $S_2$, whichever service the team chose.

\textbf{Check.} Can choosing $A_1$ guarantee $S_1$? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. Choosing the standard service does not choose the demand state.

\subsubsection*{4. Match one choice with one state}
To describe a possible consequence, we must say both which service is offered and which demand state occurs. We write $(A_1,S_1)$ and read it as ``standard packet service with high demand''. This ordered pair names one possible combination; the alternative comes first and the state second.

There are four combinations to record:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule Combination & Contextual reading \\ \midrule
$(A_1,S_1)$ & Standard service with high demand \\
$(A_1,S_2)$ & Standard service with low demand \\
$(A_2,S_1)$ & Colour service with high demand \\
$(A_2,S_2)$ & Colour service with low demand \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Before the choice and the demand outcome are known, our description must cover all four possibilities. After one service is chosen and one state occurs, only its particular combination is realised.

\textbf{Check.} If the team chooses colour service, should we erase the standard-service combinations from the original problem table? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. The complete table records the consequences of both available choices under both states.

\subsubsection*{5. Attach a supplied monetary payoff}
Suppose the enterprise is given a projected financial surplus of 20 USD for standard service with high demand. This means 20 USD remains available for its next project. We need a way to attach that number to the right combination. Write
\[P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)=20\text{ USD}.\]
Read the full statement as ``the payoff from choosing standard service when high demand occurs is 20 US dollars''. The name $P_{ay}$ stands for payoff. Its two \emph{arguments}, $A_1$ and $S_1$, identify the service and demand state. These parentheses identify inputs; they do not mean multiplication.

For this surplus measure, a positive payoff means money remains, zero means no surplus or loss, and a negative payoff means a loss that must be covered. For example, $+20$ USD can fund supplies, 0 USD leaves no money for the next project, and $-5$ USD means the team must find 5 USD to cover a shortfall. These are examples of possible financial results, not extra components of the supplied 20 USD.

\textbf{Check.} Does $P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)=20$ tell us how many packets were printed? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. It supplies a monetary payoff, not an item count or a breakdown of that payoff.

\Needspace{23\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{6. Put each payoff in its place}
Begin a payoff table with service names as rows and demand outcomes as columns. The supplied 20 USD belongs where the standard-service row meets the high-demand column. If the other supplied payoffs are 8 USD for standard service with low demand, 15 USD for colour service with high demand, and 6 USD for colour service with low demand, the completed table is:
\begin{center}
\textbf{Supplied payoffs (USD).}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule Service & High demand ($S_1$) & Low demand ($S_2$) \\ \midrule
Standard ($A_1$) & 20 & 8 \\
Colour ($A_2$) & 15 & 6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, the 6 in the second row and second column says $P_{ay}(A_2,S_2)=6$ USD. Every cell links a consequence to a choice and a state. No calculation is needed when the payoff is supplied directly.

\textbf{Check.} Where would you look for the colour-service payoff under high demand? \emph{Suggested answer:} Row $A_2$, column $S_1$: 15 USD.

The next general problem consolidates this same placement skill with generic choices and a different monetary scale. Its values are supplied independently; they are not the school enterprise's figures.

\subsection{Introductory General Problem (C1--C2)} 
	\label{sec:problem1-introductory-general-problem-c1-c2} 
	
	A decision-maker chooses between generic alternatives $A_1$ and $A_2$. After the choice, one uncertain event produces either state $S_1$ or state $S_2$. The payoffs, in thousand USD, are supplied directly:
	\[
	P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)=10,\quad
	P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)=4,\quad
	P_{ay}(A_2,S_1)=7,\quad
	P_{ay}(A_2,S_2)=8.
	\]
	
	Use the first argument to locate the alternative row and the second to locate the state column. The four combinations are
	\[
	(A_1,S_1),\quad(A_1,S_2),\quad(A_2,S_1),\quad(A_2,S_2).
	\]

\subsection{From Supplied Payoffs to Calculated Models}
\label{sec:from-supplied-to-calculated}

\subsubsection*{7. Extend the familiar table}
The introductory problem has two alternatives and two states. To discuss any selected alternative without naming a specific row, write $A_i$, read ``alternative with index $i$''. To discuss any particular state, write $S_j$, read ``state with index $j$''. The letters $i$ and $j$ are identifying indices. For example, setting $i=2$ gives $A_i=A_2$, and setting $j=1$ gives $S_j=S_1$. In the introductory table, these substitutions locate $P_{ay}(A_2,S_1)=7$ thousand USD.

We also need to say how many choices and states a larger problem contains. Let $m$ be the \emph{number of alternatives} and $n$ the \emph{number of states}. In the familiar two-by-two table, $m=2$ and $n=2$. A problem with three services and two demand outcomes would have $m=3$ and $n=2$:
\[A_1,A_2,A_3\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_1,S_2.\]
In general, the lists are $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ and $S_1,\ldots,S_n$. The dots mean ``continue the list''. The index $i$ runs from 1 to $m$, and $j$ runs from 1 to $n$. An index identifies a row or column; a count tells us how many there are; a payoff is a financial value with units. These are different jobs.

\textbf{Check.} In the introductory problem, are $i=2$, $m=2$, and a payoff of 7 interchangeable? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. They mean the selected row number, the total number of alternatives, and a payoff of 7 thousand USD.

Now we can write a general template. Each $P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)$, read ``the payoff for alternative $i$ under state $j$'', still belongs at the intersection of that row and column.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule Alternative & $S_1$ & $S_2$ & $\cdots$ & $S_n$ \\ \midrule
$A_1$ & $P_{ay}(A_1,S_1)$ & $P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)$ & $\cdots$ & $P_{ay}(A_1,S_n)$ \\
$A_2$ & $P_{ay}(A_2,S_1)$ & $P_{ay}(A_2,S_2)$ & $\cdots$ & $P_{ay}(A_2,S_n)$ \\
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\ddots$ & $\vdots$ \\
$A_m$ & $P_{ay}(A_m,S_1)$ & $P_{ay}(A_m,S_2)$ & $\cdots$ & $P_{ay}(A_m,S_n)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Check.} If there are three alternatives and two states, how many payoff cells are needed? \emph{Suggested answer:} Six: one for each of the $3\times2$ combinations.

\subsubsection*{8. Keep the table; change how its entries are obtained}
Sometimes a problem supplies a financial result directly, as in the first general problem. Sometimes it supplies item counts, amounts earned per item, or fixed amounts instead. For example, ``three packets, earning 2 USD each'' requires a calculation before we can enter a monetary payoff. The rows and columns stay the same; the method for obtaining each entry changes.

We return to the school enterprise for the following \emph{calculated-payoff variations}. Each variation states its financial arrangement explicitly. Its calculated payoffs replace, rather than reproduce, the earlier supplied figures. Unless stated otherwise, money is in USD and an item is one printed packet.

\textbf{Check.} May we put ``3 packets'' in a payoff table labelled USD? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. We must first use the stated financial arrangement to find a monetary amount.

\subsubsection*{9. Record the quantity for each combination}
A service may sell different numbers of packets under high and low demand. Colour packets may also attract different numbers of buyers from standard packets under the same demand conditions. We therefore record a quantity for each combination.

Suppose standard service sells 6 packets under high demand and 3 under low demand; colour service sells 4 and 2 packets, respectively. Write $q(A_1,S_2)=3$ packets, read ``the quantity for standard service under low demand is three packets''. More generally, $q(A_i,S_j)$ means the quantity for the selected alternative and particular state.
\begin{center}
\textbf{Quantities (packets), not monetary payoffs.}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule Service & High demand ($S_1$) & Low demand ($S_2$) \\ \midrule
Standard ($A_1$) & 6 & 3 \\
Colour ($A_2$) & 4 & 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, $q(A_2,S_1)=4$ packets. The quantity can differ both across a row and down a column because it depends on the service and demand together. Use this quantity table throughout the school variations below.

\textbf{Check.} Does $q(A_1,S_2)=3$ mean a payoff of 3 USD? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. It means three packets; we still need the monetary terms.

\Needspace{30\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{10. Variation A: earnings per packet set by the service}
Suppose each standard packet contributes 2 USD to the enterprise, and each colour packet contributes 3 USD. These are the stated earnings per packet after the relevant operating expenses; this variation includes no other payments. We need a rate determined by the service. Write $v(A_1)=2$ USD per packet and $v(A_2)=3$ USD per packet. Read $v(A_1)$ as ``the variable payoff per item for standard service''. In general, $v(A_i)$ is the alternative-based per-item payoff.

For a given service, this rate is the same in both demand states. Multiply its rate by the number of packets to obtain the monetary payoff:
\[P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i).\]
Read this as ``payoff equals quantity times the rate for the chosen alternative''. For standard service under low demand, the compact worked example follows the input--calculation--final payoff sequence.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$q(A_1,S_2)$ & 3 packets & Standard-service sales under low demand \\
$v(A_1)$ & 2 USD per packet & Standard-service earnings per packet \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule Symbolic calculation & Numerical calculation (USD) \\ \midrule
\SymbolicCell{q(A_1,S_2)\times v(A_1)}{P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)} & \PayoffNumber{3\times 2}{6} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\par\medskip\textbf{Final payoff: 6 USD for standard service under low demand.}
\end{center}
The units explain the multiplication: 3 packets times 2 USD per packet gives 6 USD. Under high demand the same service earns $6\times2=12$ USD. A constant rate across a row does not make the total payoff constant when quantities differ.

\textbf{Check.} Why is the standard-service rate 2 in both cells, while the payoffs are 12 and 6? \emph{Suggested answer:} The rate is per packet; the quantities are six and three packets.

\Needspace{27\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{11. Variation B: earnings per packet set by demand}
Consider a \emph{separate one-component arrangement}: a sponsor pays for each packet, and these payments are the only payoff. The sponsor pays 1 USD per packet under high demand and 0.5 USD per packet under low demand, whichever service is chosen. These rates replace the service-based earnings in Variation A.

Write $v(S_1)=1$ and $v(S_2)=0.5$, both in USD per packet. Read $v(S_2)$ as ``the variable payoff per item under low demand''. In general, $v(S_j)$ is the state-based per-item payoff. Here the model is
\[P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=q(A_i,S_j)v(S_j).\]
\Needspace{23\baselineskip}
For standard service under low demand, collect the inputs and calculate this one cell:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$q(A_1,S_2)$ & 3 packets & Standard-service sales under low demand \\
$v(S_2)$ & 0.5 USD per packet & Low-demand sponsor payment per packet \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\textbf{Standard service under low demand: one cell.}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule Symbolic calculation & Numerical calculation (USD) \\ \midrule
\SymbolicCell{q(A_1,S_2)\times v(S_2)}{P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)} & \PayoffNumber{3\times 0.5}{1.5} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\par\medskip
\textbf{Final payoff table (USD).}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule Alternative & $S_2$ \\ \midrule
$A_1$ & 1.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Within a state column, the rate is the same for both services. The total need not be: colour service under low demand earns $2\times0.5=1$ USD because it sells only two packets.

\textbf{Check.} Does a shared column rate guarantee equal column payoffs? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. Different services can sell different quantities in that state.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\textbf{Extension AB: both per-packet earnings apply.}
Now suppose the enterprise receives both its service earnings from Variation A and the sponsor payments from Variation B for every packet. This is a new, two-component arrangement. Each packet generates two monetary contributions, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)&=q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i)+q(A_i,S_j)v(S_j)\\
&=q(A_i,S_j)\bigl[v(A_i)+v(S_j)\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]
The first line counts the earnings from each source separately. The second adds the two rates first, then multiplies by their common quantity. Read the bracket as ``service earnings per packet plus demand-based earnings per packet''.

\Needspace{23\baselineskip}
For standard service under low demand, both rates apply to the same three packets:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$q(A_1,S_2)$ & 3 packets & Standard-service sales under low demand \\
$v(A_1)$ & 2 USD per packet & Service earnings for each packet \\
$v(S_2)$ & 0.5 USD per packet & Low-demand sponsor payment per packet \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\textbf{Standard service under low demand: one cell.}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule Symbolic calculation & Numerical calculation (USD) \\ \midrule
\SymbolicCell{q(A_1,S_2)[v(A_1)+v(S_2)]}{P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)} & \PayoffNumber{3[2+0.5]}{7.5} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\par\medskip
\textbf{Final payoff table (USD).}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule Alternative & $S_2$ \\ \midrule
$A_1$ & 7.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This is also $3\times2+3\times0.5=7.5$ USD. Only compatible rates can be added: both here are USD per packet and apply to the same packets. A rate in USD per packet cannot be added directly to an amount in USD.

\textbf{Check.} Is $3(2+0.5)$ the same as $3\times2+0.5$? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. Both rates apply to all three packets, so both are multiplied by 3.

\Needspace{22\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{12. Variation C: a fixed amount set by the service}
Not every payment is earned once per packet. Suppose a school agreement provides a single payment of 5 USD for offering standard service or 4 USD for offering colour service at the fair, independent of how many packets are printed. These payments are \emph{fixed}: within this model, they do not depend on quantity.

Write $f(A_1)=5$ USD and $f(A_2)=4$ USD. Read $f(A_1)$ as ``the fixed payoff for standard service''. In general, $f(A_i)$ is the alternative-based fixed payoff. In a \emph{fixed-payment-only variation}, with no per-packet earnings, the model is
\[P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=f(A_i).\]
For $A_1$ under either state, substituting $f(A_1)=5$ USD gives a final payoff of 5 USD. The entire standard-service row is 5; the entire colour-service row is 4. No quantity multiplication is needed.

\textbf{Extension AC: add the fixed agreement payment to Variation A.}
If the agreement payment is received in addition to the service's per-packet earnings, the model becomes
\[P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i)+f(A_i).\]
\Needspace{23\baselineskip}
For standard service under low demand, use the packet count, service rate, and single agreement payment:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$q(A_1,S_2)$ & 3 packets & Standard-service sales under low demand \\
$v(A_1)$ & 2 USD per packet & Service earnings for each packet \\
$f(A_1)$ & 5 USD & Single standard-service agreement payment \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\textbf{Standard service under low demand: one cell.}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule Symbolic calculation & Numerical calculation (USD) \\ \midrule
\SymbolicCell{q(A_1,S_2)\times v(A_1)+f(A_1)}{P_{ay}(A_1,S_2)} & \PayoffNumber{3\times 2+5}{11} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\par\medskip
\textbf{Final payoff table (USD).}\par\medskip
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule Alternative & $S_2$ \\ \midrule
$A_1$ & 11 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Multiplying the fixed 5 USD by three would wrongly count a single agreement payment three times.

\textbf{Check.} If the number of packets doubles, does the agreement payment double? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. The per-packet contribution doubles, but the fixed payment stays 5 USD for standard service.

\Needspace{25\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{13. Variation D: a fixed amount set by the state}
A state can also trigger a single payment or cost. Suppose high demand brings a one-off school grant of 2 USD for the fair, while low demand brings a one-off unused-space charge of 1 USD. These amounts apply to either service and do not depend on the packet count.

Write $f(S_1)=2$ USD and $f(S_2)=-1$ USD. Read $f(S_2)$ as ``the fixed payoff under low demand''. The negative sign records a cost that reduces the financial result. In general, $f(S_j)$ is the state-based fixed payoff. In a \emph{state-payment-only variation}, with no other financial components,
\[P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=f(S_j).\]
For either service under low demand, the input $f(S_2)=-1$ USD gives a final payoff of $-1$ USD. Under high demand the final payoff is 2 USD. Thus each column is constant in this one-component case. ``Fixed'' means independent of quantity, not identical across states.

Compare the two low-demand terms: $v(S_2)=0.5$ USD \emph{per packet} in Variation B, but $f(S_2)=-1$ USD \emph{once for the fair} in Variation D. Three packets would generate $3\times0.5=1.5$ USD from the first term, while the second term would still be $-1$ USD.

\textbf{Check.} Should three packets make the unused-space charge $-3$ USD? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. It is a single 1 USD charge, so its contribution is $-1$ USD.

\Needspace{25\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{14. Assemble the complete model}
\textbf{Complete extension ABCD.} Suppose all four arrangements now apply together: service earnings and sponsor payments for each packet, plus the service agreement payment and the state-dependent grant or charge. These are the same four sources already explained, now combined in one financial result:
\[
P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)
=q(A_i,S_j)\bigl[v(A_i)+v(S_j)\bigr]+f(A_i)+f(S_j).
\]
Read this as ``quantity times the sum of the two per-item rates, plus the alternative's fixed amount, plus the state's fixed amount''.

\Needspace{23\baselineskip}
For standard service under low demand, collect the familiar inputs first:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$q(A_1,S_2)$ & 3 packets & Standard-service sales under low demand \\
$v(A_1)$ & 2 USD per packet & Service earnings for each packet \\
$v(S_2)$ & 0.5 USD per packet & Sponsor payment for each packet \\
$f(A_1)$ & 5 USD & Single standard-service agreement payment \\
$f(S_2)$ & $-1$ USD & Single low-demand unused-space charge \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The final payoff is 11.5 USD: money available for the next project after these contributions and the charge are included. This differs from Variation A's 6 USD because the financial arrangement now includes three additional components.

The four additive payoff components are $q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i)$, $q(A_i,S_j)v(S_j)$, $f(A_i)$, and $f(S_j)$. The quantity $q(A_i,S_j)$ multiplies the two variable components; it is not a fifth additive payoff component. Each of the four components has monetary units after any multiplication.

An absent component is set to zero. For example, setting $v(S_j)=0$, $f(A_i)=0$, and $f(S_j)=0$ leaves the one-component formula $P_{ay}(A_i,S_j)=q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i)$. This does not assume that every real arrangement includes four payments; it is a way to describe simpler models using the same structure.

\textbf{Check.} Why do five input types produce only four payoff components? \emph{Suggested answer:} Quantity is a multiplier used in two components, not a separate amount of money to add.

The practice sections follow this progression. A direct-payoff model supplies each cell. One-component models use just one of the four monetary components. Two-component models add two, for example $q(A_i,S_j)v(A_i)+f(A_i)$. Three-component models add three, for example $q(A_i,S_j)[v(A_i)+v(S_j)]+f(A_i)$. Complete four-component models include all four. The number of components counts monetary contributions, not symbols or table cells.

An argument also tells us which input determines a component. A rate or fixed amount with argument $A_i$ is determined by the alternative and is reused across its row. One with argument $S_j$ is determined by the state and is reused down its column. A quantity with both arguments is specified for each combination. Values need not actually differ: two services may happen to have equal rates, even though each rate is specified by service.

\textbf{Check.} If both services earn 2 USD per packet, can the rates still be alternative-based? \emph{Suggested answer:} Yes. The arrangement specifies the rate for each service; the two values happen to match.

\textbf{Procedure for the practice problems.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Identify who decides, the available choices, and the uncertain process. State what is chosen first and which state is learned later. Describe what the consequences mean economically (C1).
\item Name the alternatives and states, and set up rows and columns for every combination (C2).
\item If payoffs are supplied, place them directly in their cells. If calculation is needed, first build a \emph{Symbol | Value | Meaning in this problem} input table with units and signs. Identify which financial components actually apply.

\item Collect only the evaluated monetary values in a final payoff table. Check row and column placement, signs, units, and completeness. The task ends with an organised description of the possible financial results.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A cell lists a quantity of 4 items. Is 4 ready to enter in a payoff table measured in thousand USD? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. Quantity counts items; use the applicable monetary rates and fixed amounts to calculate the payoff.
\item A payment is 3 USD per item and a grant is 5 USD once. For two items, should both be multiplied by two? \emph{Suggested answer:} No. The total is $2\times3+5=11$ USD; the grant is counted once.

\end{enumerate}

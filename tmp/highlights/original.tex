\section*{Evaluated criterion}
\textbf{C1:} Characterises discrete random variables in economic and financial contexts by identifying their support, probability mass function and possible outcomes.
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:t2-c1-introduction}
All data in this activity are hypothetical teaching models. Probabilities have no monetary units. Keep exact expressions where useful and round final probabilities to four decimal places.

\subsection{From a school sale to a number}

A school enterprise offers a notebook to each of three visitors. Each visitor either buys (B) or does not buy (N). An \emph{outcome} records the whole story, such as BNN: the first visitor buys, while the second and third do not. A \emph{random variable} assigns a number to every outcome.

The experiment has eight possible outcomes:
\[
\Omega=\{\mathrm{NNN},\mathrm{BNN},\mathrm{NBN},\mathrm{NNB},
\mathrm{BBN},\mathrm{BNB},\mathrm{NBB},\mathrm{BBB}\}.
\]

Consider three random variables defined on this experiment:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Example 1 (Number of buyers).}
	Let $X$ be the number of visitors who buy a notebook.
	Then $X(\mathrm{BNN})=1$, $X(\mathrm{BBN})=2$,
	$X(\mathrm{BBB})=3$, and $X(\mathrm{NNN})=0$.
	
	\item \textbf{Example 2 (Sales revenue).}
	If each notebook costs \$5, let $Y$ be the total revenue.
	Then $Y(\mathrm{BNN})=5$, $Y(\mathrm{BBN})=10$,
	$Y(\mathrm{BBB})=15$, and $Y(\mathrm{NNN})=0$.
	
	\item \textbf{Example 3 (First visitor's purchase).}
	Let $Z$ equal 1 if the first visitor buys a notebook
	and 0 otherwise. Then $Z(\mathrm{BNN})=1$,
	$Z(\mathrm{BBN})=1$, $Z(\mathrm{BBB})=1$,
	and $Z(\mathrm{NBN})=0$.
\end{itemize}

The notation $X(\mathrm{BNN})=1$ is read ``X of BNN equals one''. The capital letter names the random variable (the rule), while a lowercase letter such as $x$ represents a possible or observed numerical value.

\textbf{Check.} Are BNN and NBN the same outcome? Do they produce the same values of $X$, $Y$, and $Z$?

\emph{Suggested answer:} No; they describe different visitors buying. Both give $X=1$ and $Y=5$, but $Z(\mathrm{BNN})=1$ whereas $Z(\mathrm{NBN})=0$.


\subsection{Support, measurements and units}

The \emph{support} of a random variable is the set of values with positive probability. Different random variables describing the same experiment may have different supports and measurement units.

Consider the three random variables introduced previously:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Example 1 (Number of buyers).}
	The support of $X$ is $\{0,1,2,3\}$, measured in buyers.
	The values represent zero, one, two, or three visitors
	purchasing a notebook.
	
	\item \textbf{Example 2 (Sales revenue).}
	The support of $Y$ is $\{0,5,10,15\}$, measured in dollars.
	Revenue increases in increments of \$5 because each
	notebook has a fixed price.
	
	\item \textbf{Example 3 (First visitor's purchase).}
	The support of $Z$ is $\{0,1\}$, with no physical unit.
	The values indicate whether the first visitor buys (1)
	or does not buy (0).
\end{itemize}

Braces denote a set of possible values. All three examples have \emph{discrete} supports because their values can be listed individually. A discrete support may be finite, such as $\{0,1,2,3\}$, or countably infinite, such as $\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$, where the dots indicate that the pattern continues indefinitely.

A \emph{continuous} random variable, by contrast, can take values throughout an interval. For example, the exact time required to complete a purchase may be modelled as continuous and measured in seconds. If times are rounded to whole seconds, the recorded values become discrete.

\textbf{Check.} Can $X=1.6$, $Y=7$, or $Z=2$? Explain.

\emph{Suggested answer:} No. The buyer count $X$ must be an integer between 0 and 3; revenue $Y$ can only be 0, 5, 10, or 15 dollars; and the indicator $Z$ can only be 0 or 1.


\subsection{Assign probability to each value}

The \emph{probability mass function} (PMF) assigns a probability to each value of a discrete random variable. For $X$, it is written $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$, read ``the probability that X equals x''. The subscript identifies the random variable: $p_Y(y)=P(Y=y)$ describes revenue, while $p_Z(z)=P(Z=z)$ describes the first visitor's purchase indicator.

Every probability must be non-negative, and the probabilities over the support must add to 1. A value outside the support has probability zero. To find the probability of a value, add the probabilities of all outcomes that produce it; different outcomes need not be equally likely.

For each visitor in the school sale, assign
\[
P(\mathrm{B})=0.4,\qquad P(\mathrm{N})=0.6,
\]
where B means buying a notebook and N means not buying. These probabilities add to 1. Assume that the three visitors decide independently and that each has these same probabilities. Independence means that one visitor's decision does not change the probabilities for another visitor.

\textbf{Step 1: Find the probability of each complete outcome.} Multiply the probabilities of the three decisions in their recorded order. For example,
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(\mathrm{BNN})&=0.4\times0.6\times0.6=0.144,\\
P(\mathrm{BBN})&=0.4\times0.4\times0.6=0.096.
\end{aligned}
\]
Applying the same rule to all eight outcomes gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
	P(\mathrm{NNN})&=0.6^3=0.216, & P(\mathrm{BNN})&=0.4\times0.6^2=0.144,\\
	P(\mathrm{NBN})&=0.6\times0.4\times0.6=0.144, & P(\mathrm{NNB})&=0.6^2\times0.4=0.144,\\
	P(\mathrm{BBN})&=0.4^2\times0.6=0.096, & P(\mathrm{BNB})&=0.4\times0.6\times0.4=0.096,\\
	P(\mathrm{NBB})&=0.6\times0.4^2=0.096, & P(\mathrm{BBB})&=0.4^3=0.064.
\end{aligned}
\]
The outcomes are not all equally likely: their probabilities come from the independent decisions, not from dividing 1 by the number of outcomes. Check the total:
\[
0.216+3(0.144)+3(0.096)+0.064=1.
\]

\textbf{Step 2: Group outcomes by the value of each variable.} Add the probabilities of the outcomes that give the same value.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Example 1 (Number of buyers).}
	The outcomes BNN, NBN, and NNB produce $X=1$, so
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		p_X(1)
		&=P(\mathrm{BNN})+P(\mathrm{NBN})+P(\mathrm{NNB})\\
		&=0.144+0.144+0.144=0.432.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Similarly, BBN, BNB, and NBB produce $X=2$, so
	\[
	p_X(2)=0.096+0.096+0.096=0.288.
	\]
	Only NNN gives $X=0$, and only BBB gives $X=3$.
	The complete PMF is
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		p_X(0)&=0.216, & p_X(1)&=0.432,\\
		p_X(2)&=0.288, & p_X(3)&=0.064.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Example 2 (Sales revenue).}
	Each notebook sells for 5 USD, so $Y=5X$ when revenue
	is recorded in USD. The same outcomes that produce one
	buyer produce revenue of 5 USD. Thus the complete PMF is
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		p_Y(0)&=0.216, & p_Y(5)&=0.432,\\
		p_Y(10)&=0.288, & p_Y(15)&=0.064.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	The revenue values have monetary units; their
	probabilities do not.
	
	\item \textbf{Example 3 (First visitor's purchase).}
	The first visitor buys in BNN, BBN, BNB, and BBB, so
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		p_Z(1)
		&=0.144+0.096+0.096+0.064\\
		&=0.400.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	The first visitor does not buy in NNN, NBN, NNB,
	and NBB, so
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		p_Z(0)
		&=0.216+0.144+0.144+0.096\\
		&=0.600.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 3: Check and interpret.} For $X$ and $Y$, the probabilities total $0.216+0.432+0.288+0.064=1$; for $Z$, they total $0.600+0.400=1$. All entries are non-negative. The probabilities for $Z$ agree with the original chances of the first visitor not buying or buying. Although $X$ and $Y$ have different supports and units, corresponding values have the same probabilities because $Y=5X$. The variable $Z$ groups the outcomes differently.

\subsection{Introductory general problem: grouping outcomes}
\subsubsection*{Mathematical wording}
An experiment has outcomes $a,b,c,d$ with probabilities $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4$. Define $X(a)=0$, $X(b)=X(c)=1$, $X(d)=2$.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Construct the mapping and PMF; state the support and find $P(X=1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{Financial narrative wording}
A school shop receives one payment. There are exactly four possible payment methods: cash, debit card, credit card, or a mobile wallet. The chance of cash is 10\%, of debit card is 20\%, of credit card is 30\%, and of a mobile wallet is 40\%. The shop pays no processing fee for cash, a fee of \$1 for either type of card, and a fee of \$2 for a mobile wallet. These are the only processing charges.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Show which processing fee goes with each payment method. List all possible fee amounts and give the chance of each amount. What is the chance that the shop pays exactly \$1 in processing fees?
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Complete worked solution}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{0.48\linewidth}}
\toprule
Symbol & Value & Meaning in this problem \\ \midrule
$X$ & $0,1,2$ & Possible numerical values; fees in USD in the story \\
$p_X(1)$ & $0.2+0.3$ & Mass grouped from two outcomes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 1: Define the variable.} $X$ is the numerical score assigned to an outcome; its units are score units in the abstract version. In the financial story, define $X$ as the processing fee in USD. Label cash, debit card, credit card, and mobile wallet by $a,b,c,d$, respectively; their probabilities are $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4$. The fees give exactly the same numerical mapping.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 2: Map outcomes.} \begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\toprule
Outcome & $a$ & $b$ & $c$ & $d$ \\ \midrule
Probability & 0.1 & 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.4 \\
$X$ value & 0 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The support is $\{0,1,2\}$. The outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive; equal likelihood is not assumed.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\textbf{Step 3: Translate and calculate.} The rule for grouping outcomes is
\[
p_X(x)=\sum_{\omega:X(\omega)=x}P(\omega).
\]
Read this as: ``The probability that $X$ takes the value $x$ is the sum of the probabilities of all outcomes that give that value.'' Apply it in the following order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Choose the value $x$.} The capital letter $X$ names the rule that assigns a number to each outcome; the lowercase letter $x$ is the particular number we want. For example, choose $x=1$. In the story, this means a processing fee of \$1.
\item \textbf{Consider each outcome $\omega$.} The Greek letter $\omega$ (omega) stands for one complete outcome, such as $a$, $b$, $c$, or $d$. The expression $X(\omega)$ is the number assigned to that outcome; for example, $X(b)=1$.
\item \textbf{Keep only outcomes with $X(\omega)=x$.} The colon in $\omega:X(\omega)=x$ means ``such that''. This condition tells us which outcomes to include in the sum. When $x=1$, keep $b$ and $c$ because both give 1; leave out $a$ and $d$ because they give 0 and 2.
\item \textbf{Find the probability of each matching outcome.} The term $P(\omega)$ means the probability of the individual outcome, not its assigned numerical value. Here the matching probabilities are $P(b)=0.2$ and $P(c)=0.3$.
\item \textbf{Add those probabilities.} The symbol $\sum$ (capital sigma) means ``add''. Different complete outcomes are mutually exclusive, so add their probabilities, counting each matching outcome once:
\[
p_X(1)=P(X=1)=P(b)+P(c)=0.2+0.3=0.5000.
\]
The left side names the probability assigned to the value 1; the right side shows how it is built from the outcomes. Financially, add the chances of debit and credit card payments because both produce the same \$1 fee.
\end{enumerate}
Repeat for the other values: $p_X(0)=P(a)=0.1$ and $p_X(2)=P(d)=0.4$. If no outcome gives a chosen value, the sum has no terms and its value is zero; for example, $p_X(3)=0$. The complete PMF on the support is:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
$x$ & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ \midrule
$p_X(x)$ & 0.1 & 0.5 & 0.4 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 4: Check and interpret.} $0.1+0.5+0.4=1$ and all entries are non-negative. The score equals 1 in half of repetitions under this model, even though two distinct outcomes produce it. Financially, debit and credit card payments both cost the shop \$1, so the chance of a \$1 processing fee is 50\%. Fee amounts are measured in USD; probabilities have no monetary units.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\subsection{Bridge to the general model}
For any discrete variable,
\[p_X(x)=P(X=x)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=1.\]
These statements express two requirements: each assigned probability is non-negative, and the probabilities of all possible values add to 1. Read them step by step:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identify the variable and a value.} The capital letter $X$ names the rule that assigns a number to each outcome; the lowercase letter $x$ represents one possible numerical value. In the preceding grouping problem, $X$ is the score, or the processing fee in USD in the financial story, and its possible values are 0, 1, and 2.
\item \textbf{Read $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$.} The expression $X=x$ describes the event that the variable takes the chosen value. Both $p_X(x)$ and $P(X=x)$ name the probability of that event. For example, $p_X(1)=P(X=1)=0.5$: the chance of a \$1 fee is 50\%. The value has monetary units in the story; its probability has no units.
\item \textbf{Read $\geq0$.} This symbol means ``greater than or equal to zero''. No assigned probability can be negative. In the example, $0.1$, $0.5$, and $0.4$ are all non-negative. Values outside the support have probability zero; for example, $p_X(3)=0$.
\item \textbf{Identify the support $S$.} The support is the set of values with positive probability. Here $S=\{0,1,2\}$. The notation $x\in S$ means ``x belongs to S'' and tells us which values to use in the sum.
\item \textbf{Expand $\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)$.} The symbol $\sum$ means ``add''. Let $x$ take each value in $S$ once, find its probability, and add those probabilities. For this support,
\[
\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=p_X(0)+p_X(1)+p_X(2).
\]
This sum runs over numerical values. The earlier sum $\sum_{\omega:X(\omega)=x}P(\omega)$ runs over the outcomes that produce one chosen value. Group those outcomes first, then include each value's probability once. In particular, include $p_X(1)=0.5$ once, even though both $b$ and $c$ produce 1.
\item \textbf{Read the final $=1$ and check it.} The supported values are mutually exclusive and together cover all possible values of $X$, so their probabilities must add to 1:
\[
p_X(0)+p_X(1)+p_X(2)=0.1+0.5+0.4=1.
\]
The total 1 means a 100\% chance of obtaining some supported value. Financially, the fee must be \$0, \$1, or \$2; the total probability is 1, not a payment of \$1. Checking this total is called \emph{normalization}.
\end{enumerate}

For a finite support, the sum has a last term. For a countably infinite support, such as $S=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$, the same notation means adding an infinite list:
\[
\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=p_X(1)+p_X(2)+p_X(3)+\cdots=1.
\]
More precisely, the totals of the first finitely many terms must approach 1 as more terms are included. Every term must still be non-negative. An infinite list of possible values can therefore have a finite total probability of 1; the next problems show how to check both finite and infinite models.

\subsection{Advanced general problem: a finite model}
\subsubsection*{Mathematical wording}
A model assigns masses $c,2c,3c$ to scores $0,2,5$, respectively; let $X$ be its score.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Determine $c$, verify the PMF, and calculate $P(X\geq2)$.
\end{enumerate}
\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{Financial narrative wording}
A shop offers a promotional refund on one purchase. The refund can only be \$0, \$2, or \$5. A \$2 refund is twice as likely as no refund, and a \$5 refund is three times as likely as no refund. These are the only possible refund amounts.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find the chance of no refund and use it to find the chances of the other refund amounts. Check that all refund chances are non-negative and add to a total chance of 1. What is the chance of a refund of at least \$2?
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Complete worked solution}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 1: Define and identify support.} $X$ is the score with support $\{0,2,5\}$. In the financial story, $X$ is the refund in USD. If $c$ is the chance of no refund, the relative likelihoods give masses $c,2c,3c$ for refunds of 0, 2, and 5 USD. This is the same finite model; normalization must be checked.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 2: Normalize.} $\sum_xp_X(x)=c+2c+3c=6c=1$, so $c=1/6$. The masses are $1/6,1/3,1/2$, all positive and with total 1, so they define a PMF. In the story, these are the chances of refunds of \$0, \$2, and \$5, respectively.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 3: Translate and calculate.} $P(X\geq2)=p_X(2)+p_X(5)=1/3+1/2=5/6\approx0.8333$.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 4: Check and interpret.} The probability lies between 0 and 1. The model has only three possible values despite the gap from 2 to 5. Financially, the chance of a refund of at least \$2 is approximately 83.33\%. The refund is measured in USD; the probability has no units.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\subsection{Advanced general problem: an infinite model}
\subsubsection*{Mathematical wording}
A model assigns $p_T(t)=2^{-t}$ for $t=1,2,\ldots$, where $T$ is a positive-integer trial count.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Verify the PMF and calculate $P(T>3)$.
\end{enumerate}
\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsubsection*{Financial narrative wording}
A student running a school enterprise calls potential customers until the first sale. Each call is an independent attempt, with a constant one-half chance of making a sale. Count every call, including the call that makes the first sale. There is no limit on the number of calls allowed, and calling stops at the first sale.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Check that the chances for every possible number of calls up to and including the first sale are non-negative and add to 1, allowing for arbitrarily many calls. What is the chance that more than 3 calls are needed to make the first sale?
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Complete worked solution}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 1: Define and identify support.} $T$ is a positive-integer trial count with support $\{1,2,\ldots\}$. In the financial story, $T$ counts calls, including the successful call. A first sale on call $t$ requires $t-1$ failures followed by a success. Independence gives $p_T(t)=(1/2)^{t-1}(1/2)=2^{-t}$. This is the same infinite model; normalization must be checked.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 2: Normalize.} The first $m$ terms total $1-2^{-m}$; as $m$ grows without bound this approaches 1. Every $2^{-t}$ is positive, so the infinite list defines a PMF. In the story, the total chance of a first sale on some finite call is 1.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\subsection{Calls until a sale}
A sales team models $T$, the call number of its first sale, by $p_T(t)=(1/2)^t$ for $t=1,2,\ldots$. It also records exact call duration $D$ in minutes.

\textbf{Questions/Tasks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Classify both variables; verify the waiting-count PMF and find $P(T\leq3)$.
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Complete worked solution}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 1: Define and distinguish.} $T$ counts calls and has countably infinite support. $D$ measures time and is treated as continuous; its exact support contains intervals, rather than a list of isolated counts.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 2: Check the supplied model.} All masses $2^{-t}$ are positive. Their first $m$ terms sum to $1-2^{-m}\to1$, so the infinite PMF is valid. It is compatible with independent calls with constant sale chance $1/2$.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 3: Calculate.} $P(T\leq3)=\sum_{t=1}^{3}p_T(t)=1/2+1/4+1/8=7/8=0.8750$.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Step 4: Interpret.} The first sale occurs within three calls with probability 87.5\%. There is no fixed maximum call number in the model; this does not make the count continuous.
\end{minipage}\par\medskip

\section{Closing reference: key ideas and common errors}
An outcome is a whole story; a random variable maps it to a number. Group outcomes before assigning masses. Support contains only positive-mass values. Discrete does not mean equally likely, and a monetary variable need not be continuous. Check every mass and the total; never confuse a number of outcomes with a probability.

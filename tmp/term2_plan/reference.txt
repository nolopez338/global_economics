| **PEDAGOGICAL ACTION:** **N1.** | **What can happen when…?****Criteria:** C1, C2, C3, C4. **Sessions:** 3 to 6 (4 × 50 min). **PPDAC phase**: Problem and Plan.  **IGCSE**: E8.1, E8.2, E8.3, E8.4, E8.6.**What this action is and what it is for.****SESSION 3: The experiment and the origin of the number.****Criteria C1 and C2 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students discover, by ordering six events, that some probabilities are deduced from how the object is made, and others are only estimated by looking at what has already happened. From this distinction, they build the sample space of economic situations and audit the figures they brought.**[CONTEXT]:** **Which one would you bet on?**Once students are organized and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the question to be answered in the session: *How many people don't pay?*  Students will be organized in randomly selected pairs and given a set of six cards. (Follow the link to see the [cards](file:///C:\Users\razonar\OneDrive%20-%20COLEGIO%20SAN%20BARTOLOME%20LA%20MERCED\Documentos%20y%20Formatos%202026%20-%202027\OCTAVO%20Y%20NOVENO\NOVENO\PA1\Global_Economics_Probability_Cards_6_on_Letter.docx)).The teacher will draw or project a horizontal line on the board; on the left, the word unlikely, and on the right, likely. Students will read the cards and analyse where to place them; they will be given three minutes to do so. Once the time has finished, they must write on the cards the reason for their selection. If there is no reason why the card is not going to be pasted. The teacher gives the instruction so a member from the group pastes the 6 cards. Once it is done, the teacher leads the discussion, asking about the chosen criteria, never about the order. What did you use to decide? How is the object built, or something that you already know happened? Considering the answers, the teacher will lead the students to a conclusion; those questions can be organized by **structure** and **experience**. The right order doesn't matter. No one knows the real probability of C and D, and that's the point: they are precisely the two that the group cannot locate without data. Misplacing them is the productive result.**[EXPERIENCE]** **· Part One · 15 minutes · Constructing the sample space.**Students will be regrouped in teams of three students, and will be given back a set of cards, the same ones that were pasted on the whiteboard. The teacher introduces the distinction between random experiment and event.  ******Random Experiment:**A **random experiment** is a process or action that produces an outcome which cannot be predicted with certainty beforehand.**Example:** *Rolling a six-sided die is a random experiment because the result is unknown until the die is rolled.***Event:**An **event** is a specific outcome or a set of outcomes of a random experiment that we are interested in.**Example:** *Obtaining an even number when rolling a six-sided die is an event. The possible outcomes of this event are {2, 4, 6}.***In short:** A **random experiment** is the action performed, while an **event** is the outcome (or group of outcomes) of interest resulting from that action.“Until this step we have analised events, now we will analise the entire experiment, not only if the loan is paid, but everything that happens when the cooperative lends.”Considering the information on the cards, students will start reading, analysing and writing what repeats, all possible outcomes, and if two events can happen at the same time. This will lead them to establish the sample space of each card. This will be the students’ production for this session. While students work in teams, the teacher walks through, asking if any case is left out. Or if two of those events can happen at the same time.   Once the six sample spaces are written, each group must decide whether the event probability would come from experience or from structure. A few minutes before the session finishes, the teacher will ask the groups to exchange notebooks so the other group can verify whether they left any data behind. The teacher will sign the notebooks to check each student's production.  **SESSION 4: Where the number comes from.****Criteria C1 and C2 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students audit the figures they brought and discover that a statistic without its denominator cannot be used. They then verify experimentally that an estimate becomes trustworthy as the number of cases grows, express probabilities in three notations, and use the complement rule to read a risk claim in its opposite form.**[CONTEXT]:** **The question no one in this room can answer.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the question of the session on the board: *Where does the number come from?*The teacher will return to the sample spaces produced in session 3 and point at the outcomes labelled **experience**. Those outcomes have no number yet, and no one in the room can supply one from memory. That is why the figures were brought."Last session we found out that for the loan and the insurance we cannot deduce anything. We must go and look. You all went and looked. Today we find out what you found."The teacher will label three columns on the board: **with denominator · without denominator · without source**.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part One · 12 minutes · Auditing the figures.**Each student will state, aloud and briefly, their figure, its source, its date, and the number of cases it was based on. The teacher will place each figure in one of the three columns without commenting on the student who brought it.The teacher will then ask the group what can be done with each column. The discussion must reach the following conclusion, in the students' own words: a figure without its denominator does not tell us how much it is worth.**To consider:** if most students have no denominator, the teacher will use that fact rather than treat it as a failure to complete the task: *"this is exactly what happens when somebody sells us a loan."* The column *without denominator* is not a list of students who did not work; it is the most instructive material of the session.Only the figures from the first column will be pegged onto the probability line.**Relative frequency:**The relative frequency of an outcome is the number of times it occurred divided by the total number of cases observed. It is written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage.Example: if 40 out of 200 clients fell into arrears, the relative frequency of arrears is 40/200 = 0.2 = 20 %.**In short:** relative frequency is what we count; probability is what we conclude from it. The two are only close when the number of cases is large.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part Two · 16 minutes · The estimate that settles down.**Students will be organised in pairs. Each pair will receive an opaque bag containing 100 counters in a proportion the teacher will not disclose, and a cumulative record sheet.The instruction is to draw one counter, record its colour, return it to the bag, and repeat. Every ten draws the pair must calculate the relative frequency accumulated so far and write it down.At fifty draws the teacher will stop the activity and ask each pair to state its estimate of the proportion of red counters, and to state how much confidence it places in that estimate.Estimates will be compared across the room and plotted. The group must observe that at ten draws the estimates differ widely and by fifty they converge.**To consider:** what is being learnt here is the convergence, not the final value. If the teacher reveals the true proportion before the pairs compare their estimates, the activity loses its purpose. The proportion may be revealed at the very end, and only to confirm what the group already concluded.This will be the students' production for this part: the cumulative record and the plotted curve.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part Three · 14 minutes · The one-step shortcut.**Each audited figure will now be written in the three notations — fraction, decimal and percentage — and pegged on the probability line in its correct position.The teacher will write on the board 0.05 and 0.05 % one below the other and will ask the group whether they are the same number. Part of the group will say yes; that disagreement is the point of the moment.**Complement rule:**The complement of an event A is the event that A does not occur. It is written A′ or "not A".Since one of the two must happen, P(A) + P(not A) = 1, and therefore P(not A) = 1 − P(A).Example: if the probability that a loan is not repaid is 0.08, the probability that it is repaid is 1 − 0.08 = 0.92.Students will take each audited claim and rewrite it in its opposite form. The teacher will then pose the economic question:"If it is exactly the same figure, why does the lender advertise 92 % repayment while the rating agency reports 8 % arrears?"**[REFLECTION]:** **5 minutes.**The teacher will ask which of the figures brought can be used and what would be needed to use the ones that were discarded. The teacher will present two fictitious profiles with the same estimated probability of default and different histories and will ask whether the decision is the same. **No answer will be taken.** Students will write their response individually and keep it; the matter returns in session 12.**[EVALUATION]:**Exit ticket of three lines: one risk claim rewritten using the complement, what changes in its persuasive effect, and how many cases the figure rests on. The teacher will sign the notebooks to verify the cumulative record of Part Two.**SESSION 5: When two things happen at the same time.****Criterion C3 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students organise the outcomes of two simultaneous events using three different representations and decide which of them makes the structure of the problem visible. The cooperative does not lend once: it lends to several students and runs an insurance fund at the same time.**[CONTEXT]:** **The cooperative lends more than once.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the two questions of the session on the board:What is the probability that **two** loans are not repaid in the same month? What is the probability that **at least one** insured device is damaged?The teacher will make explicit that these are not the same question and that neither can be answered with what the group built in sessions 3 and 4, because both involve two events at once.**Combined events:**A combined event occurs when two or more events are considered together in the same experiment.Example: rolling two dice and asking for the probability that both show a six is a combined event.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part One · 15 minutes · Predict, then simulate.**Students will be organised in pairs. Before any material is handed out, each pair must write down which of the four possible outcomes they believe will occur most often when two loans are processed: both repaid, only the first repaid, only the second repaid, neither repaid.Each pair will then receive two dice or a random generator and will simulate fifty pairs of loans, recording how many times each of the four outcomes occurred.**To consider:** the written prediction is what turns the simulation into learning rather than into a manual activity. Pairs that simulate first and predict afterwards are not doing the same task.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part Two · 15 minutes · From the experiment to the tree.**Students will build the tree diagram that corresponds to the situation they simulated and will compare the theoretical branches with the frequencies they observed.**Tree diagram:**A tree diagram represents a sequence of events. Each branch shows one possible outcome and carries its probability. The probabilities on the branches leaving any point must add up to 1.The teacher will ask why the simulated frequencies do not match the tree exactly, and what would happen if instead of fifty the group ran five thousand trials. The answer connects directly with what was verified in session 4 with the bag.Every pair must check that the branches of their tree add up to 1. Any pair that does not reach 1 must find the missing or duplicated case before moving on.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part Three · 12 minutes · The same problem in three representations.**Each pair will write the same situation as a possibility diagram, a tree diagram, and a two-way table.**Possibility diagram and two-way table:**A possibility diagram lists all the outcomes of a combined event in a grid, so that the total number of cases can be seen at once.A two-way table classifies the outcomes according to two characteristics and shows the totals of each row and each column.The teacher will then ask which of the three representations makes it easier to see "at least one", and which makes it easier to see "exactly one". Students must justify their answer rather than state a preference.This will be the students' production for this session: the three representations of the same case, with the tree verified.**[REFLECTION]:** **** **3 minutes.**The teacher will ask which representation the group would use to explain this problem to someone who was not in the lesson. That representation is the one that goes into the risk protocol.The two-way table produced by the group will be fixed to the Risk Underwriting Desk. It is the raw material of Evidence 1, and it will be needed again in session 11.**[EVALUATION]:**Pairs will exchange notebooks and verify two things in the other pair's work: that no case is missing from the possibility diagram, and that the branches of the tree add up to 1. The teacher will sign the notebooks.**SESSION 6: The rule is chosen before the calculation.****Criterion C4 · Refraction and Evidence 1 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students decide and justify which rule combines two events **before** calculating anything. The session closes the pedagogical action with its refraction, addressed to the pupils of the school, and with the delivery of the first piece of formal evidence.**[CONTEXT]:** **Today the trap is not in the calculation.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write on the board the two questions that govern the session:Can they happen at the same time? Does knowing one change the other?The teacher will announce that the justifications will be collected, not the answers."If the justification is right and the number is wrong, we have done the work. The other way round, we have not."**Mutually exclusive events:**Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur at the same time.Example: a loan being repaid in full and not being repaid at all are mutually exclusive.**Addition rule:** for mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).**Independent events:**Two events are independent when knowing that one has occurred does not change the probability of the other.Example: two loans made to students from different families in different circumstances may be treated as independent.**Multiplication rule:** for independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part One · 18 minutes · Justify before calculating.**Each student will receive three situations taken from the cooperative. The work is individual and silent.The rule is explicit and must be enforced: **the justification is written first, and only then the calculation.** For each situation the student must state whether the events are mutually exclusive, independent, or neither, and why, before applying any rule.**To consider:** it is visible on the page who wrote the justification first and who wrote it after calculating. The order on the sheet is itself evidence, and it is worth pointing this out to the group before they begin.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **Part Two · 12 minutes · Comparing assumptions.**The teacher will conduct a whole-class comparison, ordered from the most intuitive justification to the most precise. What is compared is the assumption, not the answer: two students who reached the same number may have assumed different things.**To consider:** if the entire group applies the same rule, the teacher will present a fourth situation in which that rule fails. Comparison requires divergence.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 6 minutes · The case that cannot be settled yet.**The teacher will present two loans made to pupils in the same class during an economic downturn and will ask whether they are still independent."If the first one does not pay because there was a downturn, how comfortable am I about the second?"The teacher will **not** resolve this. The doubt will be written on the Risk Underwriting Desk and labelled: *settled in Pedagogical Action N3*. This is the first explicit link between the three actions of the term.**[ACTION]:** **· 10 minutes · Refraction of Pedagogical Action N1.****"A probability describes many; it never promises about one."**The teacher will return to the recipient the group named in session 2 and will remind them that this is the moment when the work leaves the classroom.The group will draft a short warning, addressed to the pupils of the school, on how to read the risk figures that appear in credit and insurance advertising. It will be displayed outside the classroom, where pupils who do not take this subject can see it.Quality conditions, which must be stated before the group begins to write:- Every claim analysed states its source, its date and its denominator, or expressly flags that one of them is missing.- The complement is used correctly, and the text explains what changes when a figure is read in its opposite form.- The text is comprehensible to a Year 7 reader.- No judgement is passed on identifiable people, in keeping with the working rules agreed in session 2."If a Year 7 pupil reads this and afterwards looks at a credit advert differently, we have done the work. If only we understand it, we wrote it for ourselves, and that is not service."**[EVALUATION]:** **Evidence 1:**The evidence for this learning process will be a formal assessment (test) designed to evaluate both conceptual and analytical skills related to probability in economic and financial contexts. Students will interpret random experiments and identify their corresponding sample spaces; calculate the probability of single events on the 0 to 1 scale, expressing results as fractions, decimals or percentages; estimate probabilities from relative frequencies; and apply the complement rule to determine the probability of an event occurring or not occurring. They will also represent combined events using possibility diagrams, tree diagrams and tables to organise possible outcomes in situations involving investment, credit and insurance, and apply the addition rule for mutually exclusive events and the multiplication rule for independent events. Through these tasks, students are expected to demonstrate their ability to organise and interpret probabilistic information, select appropriate representations and probability rules, perform accurate calculations, and use probability reasoning to analyse economic and financial situations and support informed decision-making. |

| **PEDAGOGICAL ACTION:  N2**  | **PEDAGOGICAL ACTION N2****In how many ways can it happen?****Criteria:** C5, C6, C7. **Sessions:** 7 to 10 (4 × 50 min). **PPDAC phase:** Data. **IGCSE:** E8.2 · Institutional pre-university content [SBM]: counting techniques.**What this action is and what it is for.**Pedagogical Action N1 left the group able to say what can happen and how likely each outcome is. This action answers the question that follows in the work plan: **in how many ways can it happen, and which rule does the structure of the situation require?**Counting is what makes probability computable when listing the outcomes one by one stops being viable. The difficulty is not in the formulae but in deciding which one to use, and that decision rests on two questions students habitually skip: does order matter, and can an element be repeated? In finance, the answer is rarely obvious. A portfolio of three investments is a combination; an order of priority for repayment is a permutation; and the same set of options admits both readings depending on what is being decided.For that reason, the action devotes a whole session to structure before any counting technique appears and derives the combination formula from the exhaustive listing of a small case rather than presenting it.**Recipient of this action's refraction:** the student council, as agreed in session 2.**SESSION 7: Choosing the right rule.****Criterion C5 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students classify economic situations according to their probabilistic structure, before calculating anything, and build a decision chart that will remain on the Risk Underwriting Desk for the rest of the term. The session settles the doubt left open at the end of session 6.**[CONTEXT]:** **The doubt we left on the Desk.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the question of the session on the board: *Which rule does this situation need?*The teacher will point at the note left on the Desk in session 6 — two loans made to students in the same class during an economic downturn — and will state that today the group acquires the criterion to decide cases like that one, although the full answer will only arrive in Pedagogical Action N3."Last session I asked you whether those two loans were still independent, and we did not settle it. Today we are not going to settle it either, but we are going to learn to ask the question properly. Because the mistake is not calculating badly. The mistake is calculating before deciding."**Non-mutually exclusive events:**Two events are not mutually exclusive when they can occur at the same time.Example: a calculator being damaged and a calculator being lost during the same school year are not mutually exclusive, because both can happen to the same device.**Addition rule for non-mutually exclusive events:** P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The subtraction avoids counting the cases in which both occur twice.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **·Part One · 20 minutes · Classify before calculating.**Students will be organised in visibly random groups of three, and each group will be assigned a vertical surface. Each group will receive eight cards, each one describing an economic situation involving two events.The instruction is explicit and must be enforced: **no number is written on the surface during this part.** For each card the group must write only the classification — mutually exclusive, independent, or neither — and the reason for it.The teacher will circulate asking two questions and no others: *"can both of these happen at the same time?"* and *"if I tell you the first one happened, does that change what you think about the second?"***To consider:** the two questions the teacher asks are the same two written on the board in session 6. They are not new: they are being applied. If a group classifies every card as independent, the teacher will send it back to the note on the Desk about the two loans in a downturn.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 18 minutes · Building the decision chart.**With the whole class, the teacher will build a chart on a large sheet: which question is asked first, and which rule each answer leads to.The chart is built from the group's own classifications, not dictated. The teacher writes what the students propose and asks the class to check whether it works for each of the eight cards.The chart must end with three exits: the addition rule for mutually exclusive events, the addition rule with subtraction for non-mutually exclusive events, and the multiplication rule for independent events."You are going to use this chart until the last session of the term, and in the examination as well. If it is badly built it will let us down. It is worth arguing about it now."This will be the students' production for this session: the decision chart, copied individually into the notebook, and the eight classifications with their justification.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 9 minutes · Testing the chart.**Three new situations, not among the eight, will be resolved using the chart. Where the chart fails or produces doubt, it will be corrected on the spot and the correction will be written in.The teacher will close by asking the group to identify which of the three exits of the chart they consider most likely to be misused, and why.**[EVALUATION]:**The written justifications from Part One are the formative evidence of this session: what is assessed is the reasoning, not the answer. The teacher will sign the notebooks after verifying that the decision chart has been copied and that the eight classifications carry a reason.**SESSION 8: The counting principle and the order that matters.****Criterion C6 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students count the alternatives of a chained economic decision by building it by hand before generalising it, and distinguish a product of options from an arrangement in which order matters. They also learn to correct the count when some elements are interchangeable.**[CONTEXT]:** **How many products can the cooperative offer?**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the question of the session on the board: *In how many ways?*The teacher will present the design problem: the cooperative must define its credit product, and it has three possible repayment terms, four possible amounts and two types of guarantee."How many different products can the cooperative offer? Do not tell me from memory. Build it."**Fundamental counting principle:**If a decision is made in stages, and the first stage has *m* options, the second has *n* options and the third has *p* options, the total number of possible outcomes is m × n × p.Example: choosing between three terms, four amounts and two guarantees gives 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 different products.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 15 minutes · Counting by hand first.**Students will be organised in pairs and will receive counters in three colours, one colour per category, together with a blank sheet for the tree.Each pair will build the complete tree of the credit product with the counters before writing any multiplication. When the tree is finished, the teacher will ask the pairs to count the branches and then to explain how they could have known the number without building it.**To consider:** the generalisation must come from the students. If the teacher writes 3 × 4 × 2 before the tree is built, the principle becomes a formula to be applied and the session loses its purpose. The tree is what allows the multiplication to be understood rather than remembered.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 18 minutes · When order matters.**The teacher will change the problem: the fund has run out, and the cooperative must decide the order in which the remaining applications will be dealt with when new money arrives.**Permutation:**A permutation is an arrangement of elements in which the order matters. The number of ways of arranging *n* different elements is n! = n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) × … × 1.Example: arranging four applications in order of priority gives 4! = 24 different orders.Students will work individually on the ordering problem and will then compare their counting strategies with the pair beside them. The teacher will bring to the whole class two different strategies that reached the same number.The teacher will make the shift explicit:"Before, we were choosing one option from each category. Now we are arranging the same people. Is it the same kind of count?"**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Three · 12 minutes · When elements repeat.**The teacher will present a committee formed by five members in which two of the posts are interchangeable for the cooperative's purposes: two people will do exactly the same job.**Permutations with repeated elements:**When some of the elements are identical, arrangements that only swap identical elements are the same arrangement. The count is corrected by dividing by the factorial of the number of repetitions.Example: arranging five members in which two posts are interchangeable gives 5! ÷ 2! = 60 arrangements, not 120."If two of these are identical as far as the cooperative is concerned, are we counting too many? How many times too many?"This will be the students' production for this session: the tree of the credit product with its number of branches, and the two corrected counts.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 5 minutes.**The teacher will ask the group to state, in one sentence each, the difference between the three counts worked on today: the product of options, the arrangement of all elements, and the arrangement with repetitions.**[EVALUATION]:**Exit ticket: one situation from the cooperative in which order matters and one in which it does not, each with its justification. The teacher will sign the notebooks after verifying that the tree was built before the multiplication.**SESSION 9: When order does not matter.****Criterion C7 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students derive the combination formula from the exhaustive listing of a small case, distinguish a combination from a permutation by the role of order, and use counting to obtain a probability that could not be obtained by listing.**[CONTEXT]:** **Three out of eight.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write the question of the session on the board: *Does the order matter here?*The teacher will present the investment problem: the cooperative has a surplus and eight options in which to invest it, and it will choose three."First question, and it is not rhetorical: is choosing three the same as ranking three in order of priority? Because if it is not the same, the cooperative has a different number of possibilities depending on what it is doing, and it had better know which one."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 15 minutes · Listing a small case by hand.**The teacher will reduce the problem: choosing two options out of four. Students will work in pairs and will produce two lists by hand.The first list contains every possible **selection** of two options. The second contains every possible **ordering** of two options.Each pair must count both lists and state which number is larger and why.**To consider:** the reduced case is not a simplification for weaker students. It is the only way the relationship between the two counts becomes visible. With eight options the lists cannot be written out, and the formula would have to be given rather than discovered.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 15 minutes · Generalising.**With both lists in front of them, students will work out how many times each selection appears in the list of orderings. They will then generalise to three elements and check the pattern.**Combination:**A combination is a selection of elements in which the order does not matter. The number of ways of choosing *r* elements from *n* is:C(n, r) = n! ÷ [ r! × (n − r)! ]Example: choosing three investment options out of eight gives C(8, 3) = 56 possible portfolios. Ranking three out of eight in order of priority gives 336 possible orders.The teacher will ask the group to explain where the division in the formula comes from, using the two lists produced in Part One. The explanation must be the group's, not the teacher's.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Three · 15 minutes · From counting to probability.**Students will work individually on the question that gives the technique its purpose: if the cooperative chooses its portfolio at random from the eight options, what is the probability that a given option is included?"This is where counting earns its keep. We are not counting for the sake of counting. We are working out a probability we could not have obtained by listing, because listing 56 portfolios in an examination is not an option."This will be the students' production for this session: the two lists of the reduced case, the generalisation to three elements, and the probability obtained by counting.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 5 minutes.**The teacher will ask the group to identify, among the situations of the cooperative worked on so far, one in which order matters and one in which it does not, and to say what would change in the count if the decision were reversed.**[EVALUATION]:**Pairs will exchange notebooks and verify one thing in the other pair's work: that the reduced lists are complete and that no selection has been counted twice. The teacher will sign the notebooks.**SESSION 10: What the examiner is really asking for.****Criteria C5, C6 and C7 · Refraction and Evidence 2 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students work with released examination questions in order to identify what the wording requires, in what form and to what degree of accuracy, and to find where marks are lost in work that is otherwise correct. The session closes the pedagogical action with its refraction and with the delivery of the second piece of formal evidence.**[CONTEXT]:** **Half the marks are lost before the calculation.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will write on the board the three reasons why marks are lost in this strand: the method is not shown, the answer is rounded too early, or the answer is given in a form other than the one requested.The teacher will make the purpose of the session explicit, so that it is not read as extra exercises:"The upper cycle gives us more sessions than the lower one. We are not going to use them doing more of the same. We are going to use them finding out what the examiner is asking, because knowing how to calculate and not getting the marks is the most expensive mistake there is."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 10 minutes · Reading the command, without solving.**Each student will receive five released questions from strand E8. Solving them is explicitly forbidden during this part.For each question students must underline three things: what is being asked, in what form the answer must be given, and to what degree of accuracy.Two or three divergent readings will be compared with the whole class. Where students disagree about what a question is asking, that disagreement is examined rather than resolved by the teacher.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 15 minutes · Timed work.**Three items will be solved individually under real examination time. The instruction is to show the method even where the answer is immediate."Even if you can see it straight away, write it down. The examiner cannot give you marks for what you thought."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Three · 12 minutes · Self-marking against the mark scheme.**The teacher will hand out the mark scheme, which has been kept sealed until this point. Each student will mark their own work and will record, in the notebook, where marks were lost for method as distinct from marks lost for the answer.**To consider:** these are two different problems and they need two different remedies. A student who loses marks for not showing the method needs a habit; a student who loses marks for premature rounding needs a technique. The record made here will be revisited in the equivalent sessions of Pedagogical Action N3.**[ACTION]:** **· 13 minutes · Refraction of Pedagogical Action N2.****"How many options the cooperative really has."**The group will produce a one-page guide for the student council stating how many real alternatives the cooperative has when designing its credit product and when forming its investment portfolio and why knowing that number matters before deciding anything.Quality conditions, stated before the group begins:- For each figure, the guide states whether order was considered, and why.- Every count is traceable: the reader can reconstruct how the number was obtained.- The guide states at least one alternative the council had not considered.- The text is written for a reader who has not taken this subject.The guide will be handed to the student council, not displayed in the classroom."The council thinks it has three or four options. Today we can tell them exactly how many they have. That is worth more to them than any calculation we have done so far."**[EVALUATION]:** **Evidence 2 — Combinatorial analysis of alternatives.**The evidence for this learning process will be a formal assessment (test) designed to evaluate both conceptual and analytical skills related to event classification and counting techniques in economic and financial contexts. Students will distinguish between mutually exclusive, independent and non-mutually exclusive events, selecting the appropriate probability rule according to the structure of each situation; apply the fundamental counting principle and simple permutations, including permutations with repeated elements, to determine the number of possible arrangements in business and finance scenarios; and calculate combinations while distinguishing situations in which order matters from those in which it does not. Through these tasks, students are expected to demonstrate their ability to analyse the structure of probabilistic situations, select and apply appropriate counting techniques, justify whether permutations or combinations are required, and use these methods accurately to support probability calculations and decision-making in economic and financial contexts. |
| **PEDAGOGICAL ACTION:  N3**  |  **PEDAGOGICAL ACTION N3****And what if we already know something?****Criteria:** C8, C9, C10. **Sessions:** 11 to 14 (4 × 50 min). **PPDAC phase:** Analysis and Conclusion. **IGCSE:** E8.5.**What this action is and what it is for.**This action answers the question the group itself wrote in session 2 and which has been on the Risk Underwriting Desk ever since: **should the cooperative ask for a guarantor?**Conditional probability is the concept of greatest economic value in the term and the one that produces the costliest errors. Confusing the probability of defaulting given a certain profile with the probability of having that profile given a default is the error that sustains credit discrimination and poor risk assessment alike. The didactic research is unambiguous about the remedy: natural frequencies and two-way tables come before the formula. The formula arrives last, as the formalisation of something students can already read off the table.This action also closes the narrative arc of the term. In session 12 the answers written in session 2 are returned, and the group settles with evidence what it once decided by intuition.**Recipient of this action's refraction:** the members of the cooperative, that is, those who put money into the fund.**SESSION 11: The table that changes the probability.****Criterion C8 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students read joint, marginal and conditional probabilities off a contingency table by counting, before any formula is introduced, and then verify that the formula reproduces what they have already counted.**[CONTEXT]:** **One year of the cooperative's history.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will hang on the Risk Underwriting Desk the contingency table of one hundred fictitious cases, in the space that has been reserved and empty since session 2."In session two we left this space empty and I told you it would be filled in week eleven. Here it is. One year of the cooperative's history: who applied, who repaid, and what characteristic each applicant had. The question is no longer what the probability is that somebody does not repay. It is a different one: what is the probability that they do not repay **if I already know something about them**?"The teacher will point at the question about the guarantor, written by the students in session 2, and will state that today the group acquires the tool to answer it.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 15 minutes · Natural frequencies: count, do not calculate.**Formulae are explicitly forbidden during this part. Everything is answered by counting off the table.Students will work in pairs on a printed copy of the table and will answer, in this order:1. Out of the hundred, how many did not repay?2. Of those **with** a guarantor, how many did not repay?3. Of those **without** a guarantor, how many did not repay?4. Of those **who did not repay**, how many had no guarantor?**To consider:** questions 3 and 4 give different numbers, and most pairs will assume they are asking the same thing. The teacher must let the group notice this by counting, before it is named. If the difference is explained in advance, the session loses the only moment in which the students discover it themselves.**Joint, marginal and conditional probability:**The **joint** probability is the proportion of the total that satisfies two conditions at once. The **marginal** probability is the proportion of the total that satisfies one condition, regardless of the other. The **conditional** probability is the proportion of a **subgroup** that satisfies a condition. The subgroup is stated by the words "of those who…".**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 15 minutes · Formalising what has already been counted.**The teacher will introduce the notation and the definition, and will ask students to verify that the formula reproduces the counts of Part One.**Conditional probability:**P(A | B) is read as "the probability of A given B", and is calculated as:P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B)Example: if 10 out of 100 applicants had no guarantor and did not repay, and 40 out of 100 had no guarantor, then P(does not repay | no guarantor) = 0.10 ÷ 0.40 = 0.25."I am not giving you a new formula. I am giving you the name of what you have just done by counting."Each pair must take the four questions of Part One and rewrite them in notation, checking that the formula returns the same number they counted.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **·** **Part Three · 15 minutes · The same information in three representations.**Students will represent the same data as a two-way table, a tree diagram and a Venn diagram, and will decide which of the three makes each type of probability easiest to see.The teacher will ask which representation shows most clearly that P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different quantities. This question is the bridge to session 12 and must be left in the air.This will be the students' production for this session: the four questions answered by counting and rewritten in notation, and the three representations of the same data.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 5 minutes.**The teacher will ask the group whether the table, on its own, already answers the question they wrote in session 2 about the guarantor. The expected answer is that it does not: the table gives numbers, but the decision requires knowing which of those numbers is the relevant one. That is the work of session 12.**[EVALUATION]:**Exit ticket: three questions about the same table — one joint, one marginal and one conditional — with the working shown. The teacher will sign the notebooks after verifying that the counting preceded the notation.**SESSION 12: The reversal trap.****Criterion C9 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students detect and correct the inversion of a conditional in a real claim, interpret risk in credit and insurance contexts, and confront what they wrote in session 2 with what they can now argue with evidence.**[CONTEXT]:** **What we wrote nine weeks ago.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will place on the desk, in plain sight, the sheets written in session 2 in response to the problem of the applicant without a fixed income.The teacher will hand each student their own sheet."You wrote this nine weeks ago. Read it. Nobody is going to correct anybody, and nobody is going to read out anyone else's. I only want you to compare what you thought then with what you can argue now."**To consider:** this is the moment of greatest metacognitive value in the term, and it can be spoiled in one sentence. No sheet is read aloud, no student is asked to say whether they were right, and no comparison is made between students. What is being observed is whether each student recognises their own change of mind.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 18 minutes · Directed confrontation.**The two claims from session 2 will be displayed again, and the group will analyse them using the contingency table built in session 11.A neighbourhood lender advertises: "Only 8 out of every 100 of our clients fall into arrears." A rating agency reports: "Of every 100 clients who fell into arrears, 40 had no fixed income."Both conditionals will be calculated, so that the numerical difference is visible rather than asserted.The teacher will then state what the difference means in practice:"The two sentences use the same words in a different order. And they give different numbers. That difference is what causes somebody to be refused credit they deserved."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 15 minutes · Risk, base rate and justice.**The teacher will pose the question that the whole action has been approaching:"Suppose the applicants without a guarantor really do default more often. Is that enough to refuse them credit?"**Base rate:**The base rate is the proportion of the total population that belongs to a group. Ignoring it leads to overestimating the risk of small groups: a high conditional probability calculated over few cases is far less reliable than the same figure calculated over many.The teacher will **not** close this discussion. The positions of the group will be recorded on the Risk Underwriting Desk and labelled as material for the final refraction in session 14.**To consider:** if the group reaches agreement too quickly, the teacher will introduce a minority profile with a high conditional probability calculated over very few cases. The base rate reopens the discussion and prevents a premature conclusion.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 12 minutes · What changed in my head.**Each student will write two things: what changed in their thinking between session 2 and today, and what evidence made it change.The teacher will then take three or four voluntary contributions, without commenting on their correctness.This will be the students' production for this session: the written record of the change, filed together with the sheet from session 2.**[EVALUATION]:**The written record is the metacognitive evidence of the central learning of the term. It is not marked with a numerical grade, but it is read and returned with a written comment before session 14.**SESSION 13: The decision.****Criterion C10 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**Students integrate the probability rules, the counting techniques and conditional probability into a single economic decision, compare two alternatives with evidence, and state the assumptions and limits of their own analysis.**[CONTEXT]:** **Back to session two.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will take down the work plan built in session 2 and place it at the front of the room.Together with the group, the teacher will tick off with the reserved marker the questions that have been answered during the term, writing the date beside each one."Look how many of these we can now answer. In week one you wrote them and none of us could. Today comes the hard part: deciding, and defending it in front of the people who put the money in."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 20 minutes · Individual and silent work.**Each student will decide, individually and in silence, which credit and insurance policy they would recommend to the cooperative, and with which tools they support that recommendation.The recommendation must state three things: whom to lend to and under what conditions, how much to charge, and how much each member should contribute to the fund.**To consider:** this is the only individually guaranteed evidence before the final report, and it must be collected. Students must not confer during this part; what is needed is what each of them thinks before the group reaches agreement.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 15 minutes · Comparing assumptions.**The teacher will conduct a whole-class comparison, ordered from the simplest proposal to the most elaborate."I am not interested in who got it right. I am interested in what each of you assumed. Two people with the same number can be assuming completely different things."Each proposal presented must be accompanied by the assumption on which it rests: which data were used, from which population, and what was taken for granted.**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Three · 12 minutes · The adverse case.**The teacher will change one condition of the problem: arrears double next year.Each student must state in writing what would happen to their recommended policy under those conditions and must state **at least one limit** of their own analysis."A recommendation that only works if everything goes well is not a recommendation. It is a wish."This will be the students' production for this session: the individual decision with its justification, its assumptions and at least one stated limit.**[REFLECTION]:** **· 3 minutes.**The teacher will ask which question of the work plan remains open, and will note it as material for term 35.**[EVALUATION]:**The individual decision, with its justification and its stated limit, is the direct precursor of Evidence 3 and is marked against the same rubric.**SESSION 14: What probability cannot decide.****Criterion C10 · Refraction and Evidence 3 · 50 minutes.****Purpose:**The group presents its complete proposal to the student council with the evidence that supports it and the limits that qualify it, and hands over to the council the decision that the mathematics cannot take.**[CONTEXT]:** **The work leaves the classroom.**Once students are organised and while they write the agenda, the teacher will confirm the presence of the members of the student council and will remind the group of the recipient named in session 2."Everything we have built is for them. Today they are here."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part One · 18 minutes · Preparing the presentation.**Students will work in pairs. The requirement is that the evidence supporting each decision must be visible and traceable: for every figure stated, the pair must have to hand where it came from, over how many cases it was calculated, and which population it describes."If you say 'we charge eight per cent', somebody is going to ask you why. Have the answer ready before they ask."**[EXPERIENCE]:** **· Part Two · 22 minutes · Presentation to the student council.**The group will present the complete proposal: whom to lend to, how much to charge, and how much each member should contribute to the fund, with the probabilistic evidence and the limits declared.**[ACTION]:** **· Refraction of Pedagogical Action N3.****"What probability cannot decide."**The presentation must close with the warning the group has been preparing since session 12. It is not an addition: it is the culmination of the term."Finish by telling them this: the numbers tell you how much risk there is. They do not tell you whom it is fair to refuse a loan to. That you have to decide, and that is why we are handing it over to you."Quality conditions of the refraction:- Every recommendation is accompanied by the evidence that supports it and by at least one limit.- The presentation distinguishes clearly between what the data show and what the group is proposing.- The warning about the boundary of the analysis is stated explicitly and not implied.- No profile used resembles any identifiable member of the class, in keeping with the working rules agreed in session 2.**To consider:** this is the moment at which mathematical knowledge is handed over to a deliberation that exceeds it. The group is not renouncing its work: it is recognising its boundary, which is a different thing and a more demanding one.**[EVALUATION]:** **Evidence 3 — Decision report.**The evidence for this learning process will be the End-of-Term Assessment, which has two complementary purposes. First, it provides students with an opportunity to be reassessed on Criteria C1 to C7 when one or more of these criteria were not achieved in previous assessment evidence, provided that the student has fulfilled the established prerequisite of completing the corresponding Action to Advance, understood as the preparatory activity designed to address the identified difficulties and prepare for reassessment. Second, the End-of-Term Assessment will formally evaluate Criteria C8, C9 and C10, focusing on the application and integration of probability concepts in increasingly complex economic and financial contexts. Students will calculate joint, marginal and conditional probabilities from contingency tables, Venn diagrams and tree diagrams, applying the definition (P(A|B)=P(A \cap B)/P(B)); analyse conditional probability situations related to investment, credit and insurance, interpreting risk and uncertainty from different representations and probability statements; and model financial and economic decisions under uncertainty by integrating probability rules, counting techniques and conditional probability. Through these tasks, students are expected to demonstrate not only procedural accuracy, but also their ability to interpret probabilistic information, connect multiple concepts and representations, compare possible outcomes, assess risk and uncertainty, and justify informed decisions using mathematically sound reasoning. |

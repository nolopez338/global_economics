1.3   Example 2: repeating the cycling procedure
To illustrate how often the interval method captures a known population mean, a separate simulation used five
new groups of 49 trips , selected at random and independently of one another. The original population mean
remains fixed and unknown. The cycling trips used for this simulation had a known, fixed mean duration of
 41 minutes and a standard deviation of 14 minutes , with durations following a bell-shaped pattern.
Each group uses the same 95% procedure as Example 1. Samples 1, 2, 4, and 5 cover 41; sample 3
misses because its lower endpoint is 41.08. Thus Ncover ( 95% ) = 4 and N ( 95% ) = 5 , giving
 Cov ( 95% ) = (4/5) × 100% = 80% . The absolute error is | 80 − 95 | = 15 percentage points. The
full endpoint evidence and graph appear in Step 5 below.
This finite-run coverage is 15 percentage points below nominal confidence. Five intervals need not reproduce
the nominal level; the chosen C = 0.95 and α = 0.05 stay unchanged. Over many fresh samples under
the model, about 95% of intervals cover. Each realized interval either covers or misses the fixed simulation
mean; confidence does not describe individual trip durations inside the interval.
Quick check. Which can change in a new run: the sample means, the observed coverage, or the chosen
confidence level? The first two can change; the level stays fixed unless the procedure is changed.

1.4   Comprehensive worked example: investigate the cycling interval

We now unpack Example 1, then change one ingredient at a time. All six graphical investigations belong to
this one worked example.


Step 1. Identify what is fixed and what is sampled

The population is all cycling trips in the population represented by the sample. Its population mean µ is
one fixed, unknown number of minutes. The sample is the 49 trips selected at random. Their sample mean
 x̄ = 40 minutes is a point estimate : one number used to estimate that unknown mean. A different sample
would usually give a different estimate.
The known population standard deviation σ = 14 minutes measures variation among individual trip times.
Averaging independent observations reduces variation: the standard error describes the spread of sample
means over repeated samples of the same size, rather than the spread of the individual trips.

                                          σ    14    14
                                    SE = √   =p    =    = 2 minutes .
                                           n    49    7

Thus means of 49 trips vary less than single trips: 2 minutes versus 14 minutes . SE is a standard deviation,
not the observed error x̄ − µ, which we cannot calculate while µ is unknown.
Why a two-sided z interval? Assume a random, representative sample, independent trip times, a genuinely
known population SD, and normal trip times. These assumptions make the sample mean normal with standard
             √
deviation σ/ n. For a non-normal population, a sufficiently large sample can make this an approximation,
provided severe skew or outliers do not undermine it; n = 49 alone is not a guarantee. When sampling without
replacement, the population should be much larger than the sample (for example, at least ten times as large).
Two-sided means that uncertainty is allowed both below and above the estimate.

Step 3. Build the interval and interpret it

The normal cutoffs tell us how many standard errors to allow on each side. Multiplying by SE converts that
unitless multiplier into minutes:

                             MoE = z ∗ × SE = 1.960 × 2 = 3.92 minutes .

Centre this allowance on the observed mean, not on the unknown population mean:

                CI = [ L , U ] = [ x̄ − MoE , x̄ + MoE ]
                                  = [ 40 − 3.92 , 40 + 3.92 ] = [ 36.08 , 43.92 ] minutes.
                          Centre = (36.08 + 43.92)/2 = 40 minutes ,
                             W = U − L = 43.92 − 36.08 = 2 (3.92) = 7.84 minutes .

In context: we are 95% confident that the population’s mean cycling-trip time is between 36.08 and
 43.92 minutes . This is a range of plausible values for the mean under the assumptions. The confidence
belongs to the method: in many repetitions, about 95% of the intervals would contain the fixed mean.
For this particular interval, the fixed mean either lies inside or lies outside. We do not assign a 95% probability
to the fixed mean being inside this already calculated interval. Nor does this interval contain 95% of individual
trips.

The critical values at 90%, 95%, and 99% are approximately 1.645 , 1.960 , and 2.576 . The curve rises

Greater confidence requires a larger multiplier, a larger MoE, and a wider interval for the same sample. At
100%, no finite normal cutoff encloses all the area.
Read the investigations together. A smaller population spread, a larger sample, or a lower confidence level
can narrow an interval. A narrower interval means greater precision under the model; it does not guarantee
coverage. Increasing the sample size also does not eliminate sampling bias: consistently selecting unusually fast
cyclists can give a precise estimate of the wrong group.
Final check. Changing only the sample mean moves the interval; changing SE or confidence changes its width.
The fixed population mean does neither. The selected confidence level describes long-run coverage; the observed
coverage counts successes in one finite collection of intervals.

In the original estimation problem, µ is fixed but unknown. In a simulation, the model supplies a known fixed
 µsim so that coverage can be checked: an interval CI = [L, U ] covers exactly when L ≤ µsim ≤ U , including
equality at either endpoint.


                         Eabs ( C ) = | observed percentage − nominal percentage |.
For example, | 80 − 95 | = 15 percentage points, not |80 − 0.95|. This is an absolute percentage-point
difference, not relative percentage error. A smaller absolute error in one finite run does not establish superior
long-run coverage.
For narrative evidence, use the covering count, or subtract misses from the total. For a diagram, count the
horizontal intervals meeting or crossing the fixed-mean reference line, then count all intervals. For a table,
sum the relevant covering cells and use the total number of intervals for that procedure. Paired procedures
applied to 100 samples each have denominator 100, even though there are 200 intervals altogether.
The nominal confidence level C is chosen before sampling and sets α = 1 − C . Never calculate α from
 observed coverage . Under the model, α is the long-run fraction of intervals that miss the fixed mean, not
a probability that a particular realized interval misses it. Observed coverage is calculated after a finite run
and can differ from that level. Under the stated assumptions, confidence describes the procedure’s long-run
coverage of the fixed parameter. A realized interval either contains the parameter or does not; 95% confidence
does not assign a 95% probability to that fixed interval containing it, and does not describe the percentage of
individual observations inside it.

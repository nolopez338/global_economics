Orange connects quantities, values and units; related blues mark individual masses (PMF) and accumulated probabilities

A PMF separates numerical results; a CDF accumulates their probabilities from left to right. All data are hypothetical.
Amounts are in USD; probabilities have no units. Keep exact fractions or terminating decimals.


1.1   Introductory example 1: a payment fee

A shop receives one payment. The customer pays by cash with probability 0.25 , debit card with probability 0.45 , or
credit card with probability 0.30 . These are the only payment methods. Cash has no processing fee; either card costs
the shop 1 USD. The shop records the processing fee X , in USD , with support SX = {0, 1} .
Both card methods produce the same fee , 1 USD : its point probability is pX (1) = 0.75 . At a cutoff of 0.5 USD , only
cash is included, so the cumulative probability is FX (0.5) = 0.25 . Immediately before 1 USD, FX (1− ) = 0.25 ; at 1
USD all methods are included, giving FX (1) = 1 .


1.2   Introductory example 2: daily refund requests

A small online shop receives 0 , 1 , or 2 refund requests in a day, with probabilities 0.20 , 0.50 , and 0.30 , respectively.
These exhaust the possibilities. Here X is the daily number of requests , measured in requests rather than USD, and
SX = {0, 1, 2} .
The PMF value pX (1) = 0.50 refers to exactly one request; the CDF value FX (1) = 0.70 refers to at most one. A real
cutoff between 1 and 2 requests gives the same cumulative probability: no additional supported count has been included.
The symbol X is defined separately in each example.

The representation changes; the distribution does not: pX (0) = 0.25 and pX (1) = 0.75 in both drawings. To connect
these masses to the CDF, write
                                                 FX (a) − FX (a− ) = pX (a) .

Here FX (a− ) means the cumulative probability immediately before a: it includes all supported values strictly smaller

PMF graphs use separated bars at numerical values, with heights equal to probabilities. A bar represents mass at one
value, not an interval. All horizontal axes retain actual numerical spacing. Probabilities are unitless, even when the
variable measures dollars.
A CDF graph has horizontal steps. At each supported value, the filled dot gives the new height and the open dot gives
the excluded height below it. Never join jumps with sloping lines or fill the gaps between supported values. Arrows show
horizontal tails. For finite support, the CDF reaches 1 at the largest supported value. For infinite support, it can approach
1 without reaching it at any finite cutoff.

2.2   The two-task procedure

Task 1 — PMF table and graph. Write the probability mass function (PMF) as a table and represent it graphically.
Define the random variable, its units and support. Translate complete outcomes into values. Use independence to
multiply within ordered outcomes when justified, and add mutually exclusive outcomes sharing a value. Solve missing or
proportional probabilities using total probability 1. Show every finite-support entry and check nonnegativity and total
mass.
Task 2 — CDF table and graph. Write the cumulative distribution function (CDF) as a table and represent it
graphically. Sort the supported values, then accumulate their PMF probabilities. Give all real-line intervals, including
tails. Draw right-continuous steps with correctly included endpoints. Check increasing heights, limits and jumps against
the PMF. Interpret the graph in its setting.
For countably infinite support, show initial entries and a rule for every remaining value. Label any graph as a finite
preview, show continuation and identify omitted probability. Keep the original masses; never rescale the displayed part to
total 1.


2.3   Common errors to avoid

Outcomes and numerical values need not be the same list: group equal values before making the PMF. A negative
monetary value can have a positive probability. A CDF is defined between supported values and stays constant there. At
a jump, its value is the upper filled point. A finite preview of an infinite model is not its complete distribution.

4.3   Equipment maintenance costs: a hidden condition

A printing business inspects three printers after one operating day. Exactly one unobserved operating condition, routine
or harsh, applies to all three printers for the entire day. Let w be the unknown probability of a harsh day, with 0 ≤ w ≤ 1.
Given the day’s condition, the three printers fail independently. Each printer has failure probability 1/4 on a routine day
and the same unknown failure probability q on a harsh day, where 1/4 < q ≤ 1. This independence applies when the
common condition is fixed; independence is not assumed when the condition is unknown.
Without being told the day’s condition, the probability that any specified printer fails is 1/2. The probability that both
printers in any specified pair fail is 5/16, whether the third printer fails or works. These probabilities combine routine and
harsh days; they are not probabilities given either condition. They are exact model probabilities, not sample estimates.
A working printer has no repair charge. Each failed printer has a repair charge of 2 USD on a routine day or 5 USD on
a harsh day. First, add the repair charges for all three printers to obtain the raw bill. Next, a maintenance agreement
removes the first 1 USD from the raw bill, without reducing the charge below zero. Finally, limit the remaining charge to
6 USD. There are no other charges. Record the final charge paid for the day.

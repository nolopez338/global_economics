\subsection{Introductory example 1: a payment fee}\label{prob:payment-intro}
A shop receives one payment. The customer pays by \cTwoOutcome{cash} with probability \cTwoMassHL{0.25}, \cTwoOutcome{debit card} with probability \cTwoMassHL{0.45}, or \cTwoOutcome{credit card} with probability \cTwoMassHL{0.30}. These are the only payment methods. Cash has no processing fee; either card costs the shop 1 USD. The shop records the \cTwoValue{processing fee} $\cTwoValue{X}$, in \cTwoValue{USD}, with \cTwoValue{support} $\cTwoValue{S_X}=\cTwoValue{\{0,1\}}$.

Both card methods produce the \cTwoValue{same fee}, $\cTwoValue{1\text{ USD}}$: its \cTwoMassHL{point probability} is $\cTwoMassHL{p_X(1)}=\cTwoMassHL{0.75}$. At a \cTwoValue{cutoff} of $\cTwoValue{0.5\text{ USD}}$, only cash is included, so the \cTwoCDFHL{cumulative probability} is $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(0.5)}=\cTwoCDFHL{0.25}$. Immediately before 1 USD, $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(1^-)}=\cTwoCDFHL{0.25}$; at 1 USD all methods are included, giving $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(1)}=\cTwoCDFHL{1}$.

\subsection{Introductory example 2: daily refund requests}\label{ex:refund-intro}
A small online shop receives \cTwoValue{0}, \cTwoValue{1}, or \cTwoValue{2} refund requests in a day, with probabilities $\cTwoMassHL{0.20}$, $\cTwoMassHL{0.50}$, and $\cTwoMassHL{0.30}$, respectively. These exhaust the possibilities. Here $\cTwoValue{X}$ is the \cTwoValue{daily number of requests}, measured in requests rather than USD, and $\cTwoValue{S_X}=\cTwoValue{\{0,1,2\}}$.

The \cTwoMassHL{PMF value} $\cTwoMassHL{p_X(1)}=\cTwoMassHL{0.50}$ refers to exactly one request; the \cTwoCDFHL{CDF value} $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(1)}=\cTwoCDFHL{0.70}$ refers to at most one. A real cutoff between 1 and 2 requests gives the same cumulative probability: no additional supported count has been included. The symbol $\cTwoValue{X}$ is defined separately in each example.

and $\cTwoMassHL{p_X(1)}=\cTwoMassHL{0.75}$ in both drawings. To connect these masses to the CDF,
write
\[
\boxed{\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(a)}-\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(a^-)}=\cTwoMassHL{p_X(a)}.}
\]
Here $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(a^-)}$ means the cumulative probability immediately before $a$:
it includes all supported values strictly smaller than $a$, but not $a$
itself. Adding the mass at $a$ gives $\cTwoCDFHL{F_X(a)}$. For example, immediately
before 1 USD the cumulative probability is $0.25$; at 1 USD it becomes
$1$, a jump of $1-0.25=0.75=\cTwoMassHL{p_X(1)}$.

\par\smallskip
PMF graphs use separated bars at numerical values, with heights equal to probabilities. A bar represents mass at one value, not an interval. All horizontal axes retain actual numerical spacing. Probabilities are unitless, even when the variable measures dollars.

A CDF graph has horizontal steps. At each supported value, the filled dot gives the new height and the open dot gives the excluded height below it. Never join jumps with sloping lines or fill the gaps between supported values. Arrows show horizontal tails. For finite support, the CDF reaches 1 at the largest supported value. For infinite support, it can approach 1 without reaching it at any finite cutoff.

\subsection{The two-task procedure}
\taskheading{1}. Write the probability mass function (PMF) as a table and represent it graphically. Define the random variable, its units and support. Translate complete outcomes into values. Use independence to multiply within ordered outcomes when justified, and add mutually exclusive outcomes sharing a value. Solve missing or proportional probabilities using total probability 1. Show every finite-support entry and check nonnegativity and total mass.

\taskheading{2}. Write the cumulative distribution function (CDF) as a table and represent it graphically. Sort the supported values, then accumulate their PMF probabilities. Give all real-line intervals, including tails. Draw right-continuous steps with correctly included endpoints. Check increasing heights, limits and jumps against the PMF. Interpret the graph in its setting.

For countably infinite support, show initial entries and a rule for every remaining value. Label any graph as a finite preview, show continuation and identify omitted probability. Keep the original masses; never rescale the displayed part to total 1.
\subsection{Common errors to avoid}
Outcomes and numerical values need not be the same list: group equal values before making the PMF. A negative monetary value can have a positive probability. A CDF is defined between supported values and stays constant there. At a jump, its value is the upper filled point. A finite preview of an infinite model is not its complete distribution.

\subsection{School shop processing fees}\label{prob:fees}
A school shop receives one payment. Exactly four payment methods are possible: cash (10\%), debit card (20\%), credit card (30\%), or mobile wallet (40\%). The shop pays no processing fee for cash, 1 USD for either type of card, and 2 USD for a mobile wallet. These are the only processing charges. The quantity recorded is the processing fee for this payment.
\problemTasks{sec:general}{fees}
\stage{fees}{1}

\subsection{Fundraising draw prizes: selections and a bonus cap}\label{prob:prize}
A fundraising draw uses seven physically distinct tickets: three marked 0 USD, two marked 2 USD, and two marked 5 USD. The amount marked on a ticket is its face value. Tickets with the same face value are separate tickets. Select three tickets uniformly at random without replacement: a selected ticket cannot be selected again. Every set of three distinct tickets is equally likely. Selection order does not affect the payout.

Add the face values of the three selected tickets. Add one bonus of 3 USD if and only if the selection includes at least one 2 USD ticket and at least one 5 USD ticket. The bonus is added only once, regardless of how many tickets of either value are selected. After adding any bonus, limit the total payout to 10 USD: pay 10 USD if the total exceeds 10 USD; otherwise, pay the total. There are no other prizes or charges. Record the final payout for this selection.
\problemTasks{sec:advanced}{prize}
\stage{prize}{1}
$\cTwoValue{X}$ is the \cTwoValue{final three-ticket payout}, in USD. Use auxiliary counts $a,b,c$ for the numbers of 0, 2 and 5 USD tickets. Then $a+b+c=3$, $0\leq a\leq3$, $0\leq b,c\leq2$, and
\[
\cTwoValue{X}=\min\{10,\,2b+5c+3\mathbf{1}_{\{b\geq1,\ c\geq1\}}\}.
\]
Here the indicator $\mathbf{1}$ equals 1 when its stated condition holds, and 0 otherwise. Thus the bonus is added once, before applying the cap; $\cTwoValue{X}$ is not a constant multiple of the face-value total.

There are $\binom{7}{3}=35$ equally likely selections. For fixed $(a,b,c)$, choose tickets separately from each type, giving $\binom{3}{a}\binom{2}{b}\binom{2}{c}$ selections. No independent-draw multiplication is justified without replacement.

\subsection{Equipment maintenance costs: a hidden condition}\label{prob:maintenance}
A printing business inspects three printers after one operating day. Exactly one unobserved operating condition, routine or harsh, applies to all three printers for the entire day. Let $w$ be the unknown probability of a harsh day, with $0\leq w\leq1$. Given the day's condition, the three printers fail independently. Each printer has failure probability $1/4$ on a routine day and the same unknown failure probability $q$ on a harsh day, where $1/4<q\leq1$. This independence applies when the common condition is fixed; independence is not assumed when the condition is unknown.

Without being told the day's condition, the probability that any specified printer fails is $1/2$. The probability that both printers in any specified pair fail is $5/16$, whether the third printer fails or works. These probabilities combine routine and harsh days; they are not probabilities given either condition. They are exact model probabilities, not sample estimates.

A working printer has no repair charge. Each failed printer has a repair charge of 2 USD on a routine day or 5 USD on a harsh day. First, add the repair charges for all three printers to obtain the raw bill. Next, a maintenance agreement removes the first 1 USD from the raw bill, without reducing the charge below zero. Finally, limit the remaining charge to 6 USD. There are no other charges. Record the final charge paid for the day.
\problemTasks{sec:advanced}{maintenance}

\textbf{Recover the hidden probabilities first.} Write $w=P(\text{harsh})$, so $P(\text{routine})=1-w$. Split the stated events by condition. Conditional independence gives $q^2$ for a pair failing on a harsh day, and $(1/4)^2$ on a routine day. Thus
\[
(1-w)\frac14+wq=\frac12,\qquad
(1-w)\frac1{16}+wq^2=\frac5{16}.
\]
Subtract the routine contributions and factor:
\[
w\left(q-\frac14\right)=\frac14,\qquad
w\left(q-\frac14\right)\left(q+\frac14\right)=\frac14.
\]
The first product is positive, so division is valid. It gives $q+1/4=1$, hence $q=3/4$ and $w=1/2$. This is the unique solution under $q>1/4$, and both are valid probabilities. Substitution recovers $1/2$ and $5/16$ as required.

\textbf{Why conditioning matters.} The printers are independent only after fixing the common condition. Without conditioning, two fail with probability $5/16$, which differs from $(1/2)^2=1/4$. Using three independent failure probabilities of $1/2$ would discard information about shared operating conditions.

\subsection{Waiting for a sale--failure--sale pattern}\label{prob:calls}
A school enterprise calls customers one at a time. Each call results in either a sale (S) or no sale (N). The calls are independent. On every call, the probability of S is $1/2$ and the probability of N is $1/2$. Here, ``failure'' means no sale.

Continue calling until three consecutive calls first give S, N, S in that order. Stop immediately after the S that completes this first pattern. From the third call onward, check the most recent three calls after every call. Candidate groups of three may share calls. An incomplete or unsuccessful attempt does not restart the call sequence or discard calls that could be part of a later match. There is no fixed limit on the number of calls. Record the total number of calls from the first call through the stopping call, including all three calls in the completed pattern and every earlier call.
\problemTasks{sec:advanced}{calls}
\stage{calls}{1}
$\cTwoValue{X}$ is the number of calls through the first completed SNS, in calls. A first sale alone does not end this experiment. We must remember partial progress: an S can start a candidate pattern, and a later S can replace that start.

\textbf{Define three memory states.} Only retain sequences that have not yet contained SNS. Their relevant memory is the longest ending segment that is also a beginning of SNS: no matching segment (state A), S (state B), or SN (state C). An ``ending segment'' is a suffix of the calls already made. This memory suffices because a future completion can use past calls only through such a segment.
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{clcc}\toprule
State & Remembered suffix & Next call N & Next call S \\\midrule
A & none & A & B \\
B & S & C & B \\
C & SN & A & stop \\\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
For example, SS retains S and stays in B; SNN retains no initial part of SNS and returns to A. These transitions account for overlapping attempts without treating successive three-call windows as independent.

\textbf{Derive a counting recurrence.} Let $a_n,b_n,c_n$ count length-$n$ sale/no-sale strings that avoid SNS and end in A, B, C. Initially there is one empty string, in A:
\[
(a_0,b_0,c_0)=(1,0,0).
\]
Append one symbol to each surviving string and use the transition table:
\[
\boxed{a_{n+1}=a_n+c_n,\qquad b_{n+1}=a_n+b_n,\qquad c_{n+1}=b_n\quad(n\geq0).}
\]
For example, the strings counted by $a_{n+1}$ arise by appending N to A or C; the two source sets are disjoint. A completion on call $n$ arises only by appending S to one of the $c_{n-1}$ surviving strings ending in SN. Each length-$n$ string has probability $2^{-n}$ by independence. Hence
\[
\boxed{\cTwoMassHL{p_X(n)}=\frac{c_{n-1}}{2^n}\quad\text{for every integer }n\geq3,}
\qquad \cTwoMassHL{p_X(x)}=\cTwoMassHL{0}\text{ otherwise.}
\]
This recurrence with its initial values uniquely specifies every PMF entry. All integers $n\geq3$ have positive mass: the string consisting of $n-3$ N's followed by SNS first completes at $n$. Thus $\cTwoValue{S_X}=\cTwoValue{\{3,4,5,\ldots\}}$.

\subsection{School enterprise: the smallest opening reserve}\label{prob:reserve}
A school enterprise tests a cash-handling plan using ten distinct transaction slips. Five slips each record a receipt, meaning 1 USD enters the cash box. The other five slips each record a payment, meaning 1 USD leaves the cash box. Arrange all ten slips uniformly at random, so every ordering of the ten distinct slips is equally likely. Process the slips one at a time in that order. Each receipt immediately adds 1 USD to the balance. Each payment immediately removes 1 USD. Transactions cannot be delayed or rearranged. There are no fees, other transactions, or later additions of cash apart from the five receipts.

For each realized order, determine the smallest nonnegative opening reserve, in whole USD, needed in the cash box before the first transaction. The opening reserve is the entire starting balance. The balance must remain nonnegative after every transaction, so every payment can be made when its slip is processed. A balance of zero is allowed. Record the minimum reserve required for that particular order. The recorded reserve may differ between orders.
\problemTasks{sec:advanced}{reserve}
\stage{reserve}{1}
$\cTwoValue{X}$ is the \cTwoValue{smallest sufficient opening reserve} for the shuffled order, in USD. For calculations, write R for a receipt and P for a payment, and let $b_j$ be receipts minus payments among the first $j$ slips, with $b_0=0$. With reserve $r$, the actual balance after $j$ transactions is $r+b_j$. Therefore
\[
\cTwoValue{X}=-\min\{b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_{10}\}.
\]
The minimum is at most zero because $b_0=b_{10}=0$. At worst all five payments come first, so $0\leq \cTwoValue{X}\leq5$. Every integer $k$ in this range is possible: start with $k$ P's, then $k$ R's, and finish with $5-k$ RP pairs. The lowest balance is $-k$. Thus $\cTwoValue{S_X}=\cTwoValue{\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}}$.

\textbf{Find an equally likely sample space.} Each R/P string with five of each type comes from exactly $5!\,5!$ distinct-slip orderings: assign the five distinct receipt slips to its R positions and the payment slips to its P positions. Hence all $\binom{10}{5}=252$ such strings are equally likely. The successive types are not independent.

\textbf{Discover a simpler event to count.} Counting strings with a prescribed lowest balance directly forces us to control all ten intermediate balances. Instead, first count strings needing \emph{at least} $k$ USD, for $1\leq k\leq5$. Such a string reaches balance $-k$. Since every change is $+1$ or $-1$, there is a unique first time it reaches $-k$. At that time its initial segment has exactly $k$ more P's than R's. Swapping R and P in that segment turns its net change from $-k$ into $+k$, increasing the final balance by $2k$. This suggests replacing a restriction on intermediate balances by a restriction on the final total.

\textbf{Prove the correspondence in both directions.} In a string with $\cTwoValue{X}\geq k$, swap R and P through its first visit to $-k$ and leave the rest unchanged. Before the swap point the original balances are greater than $-k$; their negatives are less than $+k$. The transformed string therefore first reaches $+k$ at that same point and ends at $2k$.

Conversely, take \emph{any} ten-symbol R/P string ending at $2k$. Its unit steps must pass through $+k$. Swap R and P through its first visit to $+k$. The new string ends at zero, has five of each type, and first reaches $-k$ at the swap point. Thus it needs at least $k$ USD. These two operations undo each other, including the choice of swap point. Every relevant original string is counted once, every target string has an original, and no two originals share a target. The transformed strings are counting devices, not additional possible transaction orders.

\textbf{A concrete swap (Task 1 continued).} For $k=2$, the order PPRRRRPPPR first reaches $-2$ after its initial PP. Swapping just those two symbols gives RRRRRRPPPR, which ends at $+4$. Its first visit to $+2$ is after the initial RR, so the inverse swap recovers the original order.

\textbf{Count and form the PMF (Task 1 continued).} A transformed string ending at $2k$ has $5+k$ R's and $5-k$ P's: their counts sum to 10 and their difference is $2k$. Choosing the P positions gives exactly $\binom{10}{5-k}$ strings. Let $N_k$ count original orders with $\cTwoValue{X}\geq k$. The correspondence proves
\[
N_k=\binom{10}{5-k}\quad(1\leq k\leq5),\qquad N_0=252,\quad N_6=0.
\]

Orders needing exactly $k$ USD are those needing at least $k$ but not at least $k+1$. These events are nested, so their counts subtract:
\[
\cTwoMassHL{p_X(k)}=\frac{N_k-N_{k+1}}{252}\quad(k=0,1,\ldots,5),
\qquad \cTwoMassHL{p_X(x)}=\cTwoMassHL{0}\text{ elsewhere}.
\]
For example, $\cTwoMassHL{p_X(2)}=(120-45)/252=75/252$. A reserve of 2 USD suffices for these orders, whereas a reserve of 1 USD fails at some payment.

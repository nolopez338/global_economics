                                          Permutations and Combinations I
                                                      1. Progressive Problems
1. Six books have different titles. In how many ways can all six be placed in a single row on a shelf? Each book is used once, and two
   rows are different if their left-to-right orders differ.
2. A club has nine distinct members. In how many ways can it choose a committee of four different members? There are no assigned
   roles, and the order in which members are chosen does not matter.
3. Eight distinct students enter a contest with three awards: first, second, and third place. In how many ways can the awards be
   assigned if each award goes to one student and no student receives more than one award?
4. In how many different left-to-right strings can all the letters of BALLOON be arranged? Use each letter exactly as often as it appears
   in the word. Copies of the same letter are indistinguishable, and the strings need not be English words.
5. Six distinct books, labelled A, B, C, D, E, F , are placed in a row, each exactly once. How many left-to-right arrangements have A
   and B next to each other, but C and D not next to each other? Either order within an adjacent pair is allowed.
6. Four girls, Ada, Bella, Cleo, and Dina, and three boys, Eli, Finn, and Gus, are available for a photograph. Choose five different
   students and arrange them in a row. The row must include Ada, at least two girls, and at least two boys, and no two boys may
   stand next to each other. How many different left-to-right rows are possible? All seven students are distinguishable, and each
   selected student appears exactly once.

                                                      2. Olympiad Challenges
7. Three different numbers are chosen from {1, 2, . . . , 10}. No two chosen numbers may differ by 1. How many unordered sets can be
   chosen? Each number can be used at most once.
8. Arrange the numbers 1, 2, . . . , 7 in a row, each exactly once. Call an index i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , 6} a success if i is immediately followed by
   i + 1, in that order. How many permutations have exactly two successes? Successes may overlap: for instance, the consecutive
   segment 3, 4, 5 contributes successes at both i = 3 and i = 4. Two permutations are different if their left-to-right orders differ.
                                                             3. Solutions
Problem 1
Order matters because the shelf has a left-to-right order. There are six choices for the first position, five for the second, and so on.
Every book is distinct and used once, so there is no division for identical objects and no repetition of choices. The multiplication
principle gives
                                                      6 · 5 · 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 6! = 720 .

Problem 2
A committee is an unordered group. First count ordered selections of four different members: there are 9 · 8 · 7 · 6 choices. Every
committee appears 4! times in this count, once for each order of its four members. Dividing by this common overcount gives
                                                      
                                                      9     9·8·7·6
                                                          =             = 126 .
                                                      4          4!
The members are distinguishable; the division removes selection order, not differences between members. No member is selected twice.

Problem 3
The awards are distinct positions, so order matters: exchanging the first- and second-place winners changes the result. Choose the
first-place winner in eight ways, the second in seven, and the third in six. The decreasing numbers of choices enforce the rule that a
student cannot win twice. Thus
                                                                   8!
                                                         8·7·6=       = 336 .
                                                                   5!
There is no division by 3!, since the three award assignments are distinct.

Problem 4
There are seven letter positions. Temporarily distinguish the two L’s as L1 , L2 and the two O’s as O1 , O2 . These seven labelled objects
have 7! ordered arrangements, with no object used twice.
In an actual string, exchanging the labels on the L’s or on the O’s makes no visible change. Every actual string therefore corresponds
to exactly 2! 2! labelled arrangements. All other letters occur once, so
                                                              7!
                                                                   = 1260 .
                                                             2! 2!
Order still matters; only exchanges of indistinguishable copies are removed.

Problem 5
Count all rows with A, B together, then subtract those in which C, D are also together. Treat A, B as one block. Together with
C, D, E, F , this gives five distinct objects, arranged in 5! ways. The block can be AB or BA, giving 2 · 5! rows.
For the unwanted rows, treat both pairs as blocks. The four objects are the two blocks, E, and F . Each block has two internal orders,
so there are 2 · 2 · 4! unwanted rows. Hence the required count is
                                                    2 · 5! − 4 · 4! = 240 − 96 = 144 .
Each row has a unique decomposition into these blocks, even when the blocks touch, so there is no overcount. All six books remain
distinct and appear once; both their order and each block’s internal order matter.

Problem 6
Since five students are selected with at least two of each gender, there are exactly two possible compositions: two girls and three boys,
or three girls and two boys. These cases are disjoint and exhaust the possibilities. In each case, choose the students first, then arrange
them; no student is chosen twice.
Case 1: Two girls and three boys. Ada must be one of the girls, so choose her companion in 3 ways. All three boys must be
chosen. Arrange the two girls in 2! ways. They determine three gaps: before, between, and after them. To separate three boys, put
exactly one in every gap, in 3! orders. This gives
                                                              3 · 2! · 3! = 36.
Case 2: Three girls and two boys. Choose two of the other three girls to join Ada, and choose two of the three boys. Arrange the
girls in 3! ways. Their row has four gaps; choose two different gaps for the boys and assign the boys to them in 2! orders. This gives
                                                          
                                                        3 3         4
                                                                 3!    2! = 648.
                                                        2 2         2

girls’ order, the occupied gaps, and the boys’ order, so every valid row is counted once. Selection is unordered, but the final positions
are ordered and all students are distinguishable. Adding the cases gives
                                                             36 + 648 = 684 .

Problem 7
Key insight: remove the compulsory spaces between chosen numbers. Write the three chosen numbers in increasing order as
a < b < c. This is a unique way to represent an unordered set; it does not add an ordering choice. The restriction says b ≥ a + 2 and
c ≥ b + 2.
Define
                                                   x = a,      y = b − 1,      z = c − 2.
Then 1 ≤ x < y < z ≤ 8. Thus every allowed set produces an ordinary three-element subset of {1, 2, . . . , 8}.
This correspondence is reversible. From any 1 ≤ x < y < z ≤ 8, set a = x, b = y + 1, and c = z + 2. These numbers lie between 1 and
10, and their successive differences are at least 2. The two constructions undo each other, so this is a bijection: nothing is lost or
counted twice. All numbers are distinct and none is repeated. Therefore the answer is
                                                         
                                                          8    8·7·6
                                                             =         = 56 .
                                                          3      3!

Problem 8
Key insight: required consecutive links can be contracted into blocks, even when they overlap. For each i ∈ {1, . . . , 6},
let Ei mean that i is immediately followed by i + 1. If a specified set of k links is required, merge each chain of those links into one
block. For example, requiring E3 and E4 creates the single block (3, 4, 5).
The links follow the path 1, 2, . . . , 7, so each required link reduces the number of blocks by one. There are exactly 7 − k distinct blocks,
each with a fixed internal order. Their (7 − k)! permutations give all rows satisfying the required links. Extra successes are allowed in
this intermediate count. Every number is used once, so no indistinguishability factor is needed.
Require two specified successes and exclude the other four. Fix a two-element set S of success indices. There are 5!
permutations satisfying its two required links. To make these the only successes, apply inclusion–exclusion to the four indices outside
S:                                                                         
                                                      4        4        4         4
                                                5! −     4! +     3! −     2! +     1!
                                                      1        2        3         4
                                                      = 120 − 96 + 36 − 8 + 1 = 53.

                                       Introduction: Counting Choices
Multiplication principle. Count a process one step at a time. If the number of available choices at each
step is the same for every possible sequence of earlier choices, multiply these numbers to count the complete
outcomes. Order matters means that exchanging positions changes the outcome.
Notation and when to use it. Let n be the total number of objects, a nonnegative integer, and let r be the
number selected, an integer with 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
• To arrange all n distinct objects in a row, there are n choices for the first position, then n − 1, and so on.
  Their product is called factorial : n! = n(n − 1) · · · 2 · 1 for n ≥ 1. By convention, 0! = 1: there is one empty
  arrangement.

Worked example 1: Three distinct roles. Five people volunteer for an event. Assign a coordinator,
a treasurer, and a secretary, with no person holding more than one role. Order matters: exchanging the
coordinator and treasurer changes who does each job. Choose the coordinator in five ways, the treasurer in
four remaining ways, and the secretary in three remaining ways. Multiplying gives
                                                      5!
                                         5·4·3=             = 60 .
                                                   (5 − 3)!
Worked example 2: A committee without roles. Choose three of the same five volunteers for a committee
with no assigned roles. Order does not matter: exchanging the positions in a list of members leaves the
committee unchanged. Each committee corresponds to 3! = 3 · 2 · 1 = 6 ordered role assignments: its three
members can fill the coordinator, treasurer, and secretary roles in six ways. Thus the previous count includes
each committee six times. Dividing gives
                                      
                                      5          5!       60    60
                                          =             =    =      = 10 .
                                      3      3!(5 − 3)!   3!     6
Worked example 3: Together, but with a forbidden pair. Seven distinct runners, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V ,
line up for a photograph. How many rows put P next to Q, but keep R away from S? Either order within a
pair is allowed. All seven runners appear once; order matters because the photograph has a left and a right.
First enforce the required adjacency. Replace P, Q by a single block. We now arrange six distinct units:
the block and the other five runners. The block has two internal orders, P Q and QP . For example, contraction
and expansion relate these descriptions:
                           T     QP     R   V    S   U     ←→      T    Q P      R    V   S       U.
There are 6! orders of the units and two choices inside the block, so 2 · 6! = 1440 rows meet the required
adjacency.
Then subtract the forbidden adjacency. Among those rows, the unwanted ones also put R, S together.
Contract both pairs.

                               Rows counted            Units to arrange        Number
                               P, Q together           [P Q], R, S, T, U, V    2 · 6! = 1440
                               Both pairs together     [P Q], [RS], T, U, V    2 · 2 · 5! = 480
 The brackets denote pairs in either internal order. Thus the answer is
                                          2 · 6! − 4 · 5! = 1440 − 480 = 960 .
 Every row with P, Q adjacent has a unique contracted row and a unique internal order. The same is true
 when both pairs are contracted, even if their blocks touch. Expanding recovers the original row, so each count
 includes each relevant row exactly once. The unwanted rows form a subset of the first count; subtracting leaves
 exactly the valid rows.
                                            1. Progressive Problems
 1. Six books have different titles. In how many ways can all six be placed in a single row on a shelf? Each
    book is used once, and two rows are different if their left-to-right orders differ.
 2. A club has nine distinct members. In how many ways can it choose a committee of four different members?
    There are no assigned roles, and the order in which members are chosen does not matter.
 3. Eight distinct students enter a contest with three awards: first, second, and third place. In how many ways
    can the awards be assigned if each award goes to one student and no student receives more than one award?
 4. In how many different left-to-right strings can all the letters of BALLOON be arranged? Use each letter exactly
    as often as it appears in the word. Copies of the same letter are indistinguishable, and the strings need not
    be English words.
 5. Arrange all the letters of BALLOON in a left-to-right string, but do not allow the two L’s to be adjacent.
    Copies of the same letter are indistinguishable; each letter must be used as often as it appears in the word.
    How many strings are possible? Explain how to place the repeated letters without counting a string more
    than once.
 6. Six distinct books, labelled A, B, C, D, E, F , are placed in a row, each exactly once. How many left-to-right
    arrangements have A and B next to each other, but C and D not next to each other? Either order within
    an adjacent pair is allowed.
 7. Four girls, Ada, Bella, Cleo, and Dina, and three boys, Eli, Finn, and Gus, are available for a photograph.
    Choose five different students and arrange them in a row. The row must include Ada, at least two girls, and
    at least two boys, and no two boys may stand next to each other. How many different left-to-right rows are
    possible? All seven students are distinguishable, and each selected student appears exactly once.
 8. Three different numbers are chosen from {1, 2, . . . , 10}. No two chosen numbers may differ by 1. How many
    unordered sets can be chosen? Each number can be used at most once.
    For a useful representation, write a chosen set as a < b < c. Find a reversible transformation that removes
    the compulsory spaces between these numbers, and use it to count the sets.

                                             2. Olympiad Challenges
 9. Choose an unordered five-element subset of {1, 2, . . . , 12} with exactly two consecutive pairs. Here a
    consecutive pair means {i, i + 1} with both numbers selected. Overlap is allowed: selecting 4, 5, 6 creates
    two pairs, {4, 5} and {5, 6}. How many such subsets are there? Explain how to include overlapping and
    separate pairs without double counting.
10. Arrange the numbers 1, 2, . . . , 7 in a row, each exactly once. Call an index i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , 6} a success if i is
    immediately followed by i + 1, in that order. How many permutations have exactly two successes? Successes
    may overlap: for instance, the consecutive segment 3, 4, 5 contributes successes at both i = 3 and i = 4.
    Two permutations are different if their left-to-right orders differ.
11. A robot travels from (0, 0) to (6, 6) using exactly six steps R = (1, 0) and six steps U = (0, 1), in some order.
    At every point of its route it must satisfy y ≤ x: it may touch the diagonal y = x but may never go above
    it. How many different routes are possible? Two routes are different if their step sequences differ. Find a
    reversible way to count the forbidden routes rather than listing all allowed routes.

Solution 1
Order matters because the shelf has a left-to-right order. There are six choices for the first position, five for the second,
and so on. Every book is distinct and used once, so there is no division for identical objects and no repetition of
choices. The multiplication principle gives

                                               6 · 5 · 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 6! = 720 .

Solution 6
Count all rows with A, B together, then subtract those in which C, D are also together. Treat A, B as one block.
Together with C, D, E, F , this gives five distinct objects, arranged in 5! ways. The block can be AB or BA, giving
2 · 5! rows.
For the unwanted rows, treat both pairs as blocks. The four objects are the two blocks, E, and F . Each block has two
internal orders, so there are 2 · 2 · 4! unwanted rows. Hence the required count is

                                           2 · 5! − 4 · 4! = 240 − 96 = 144 .

Each row has a unique decomposition into these blocks, even when the blocks touch, so there is no overcount. All six
books remain distinct and appear once; both their order and each block’s internal order matter.

Key insight: remove the compulsory spaces between chosen numbers. Write the three chosen numbers in
increasing order as a < b < c. This is a unique way to represent an unordered set; it does not add an ordering choice.
The restriction says b ≥ a + 2 and c ≥ b + 2.
Define
                                         x = a,     y = b − 1,     z = c − 2.
Then 1 ≤ x < y < z ≤ 8. Thus every allowed set produces an ordinary three-element subset of {1, 2, . . . , 8}.
This correspondence is reversible. From any 1 ≤ x < y < z ≤ 8, set a = x, b = y + 1, and c = z + 2. These numbers lie
between 1 and 10, and their successive differences are at least 2. The two constructions undo each other, so this is a
bijection: nothing is lost or counted twice. For example, the compulsory spacings are removed as follows:

                                          first second third

Solution 9
Order does not matter: we count subsets, each represented by its increasing list. Split a chosen list into maximal runs
of consecutive numbers. For instance, {2, 3, 4, 8, 11} has runs (2, 3, 4), (8), (11). Maximal means that two different runs
have at least one unselected number between them.
Translate pairs into runs. A run of length ℓ contributes ℓ − 1 consecutive pairs, including overlaps. If five selected
numbers form r runs, the total is 5 − r. Exactly two pairs therefore means exactly three runs. The possible lengths,
read from left to right, are:

                          Type                           Ordered run lengths                    Number of orders
                          Two overlapping pairs          (3, 1, 1), (1, 3, 1), (1, 1, 3)        3
                          Two separate pairs             (2, 2, 1), (2, 1, 2), (1, 2, 2)        3

These are all positive triples summing to five: after giving each run one number, the two remaining numbers either
extend one run twice or two runs once each. These types are disjoint, and together exhaustive.

number must separate each of the two neighboring runs. Reserve these two separators. The other five unselected

Solution 10
Key insight: required consecutive links can be contracted into blocks, even when they overlap. For each
i ∈ {1, . . . , 6}, let Ei mean that i is immediately followed by i + 1. If a specified set of k links is required, merge each
chain of those links into one block. For example, requiring E3 and E4 creates the single block (3, 4, 5).
For comparison, requiring E1 , E4 gives the five units [1, 2], 3, [4, 5], 6, 7, whereas requiring E3 , E4 gives 1, 2, [3, 4, 5], 6, 7.
Both leave five units; the overlap changes their lengths, but not their number. The directed links force each block’s
internal order, so there is no factor of two for reversing a block.
The links follow the path 1, 2, . . . , 7, so each required link reduces the number of blocks by one. There are exactly 7 − k
distinct blocks, each with a fixed internal order. Their (7 − k)! permutations give all rows satisfying the required links.
Contracting the specified links uniquely recovers the block order, and expansion reverses the operation. If two blocks
create an extra success at their boundary, they remain separate units in this count: only specified links are contracted.
Extra successes are allowed in this intermediate count. Every number is used once, so no indistinguishability factor is
needed.
Require two specified successes and exclude the other four. Fix a two-element set S of success indices. There
are 5! permutations satisfying its two required links. To make these the only successes, apply inclusion–exclusion to
the four indices outside S:                                        

Translate the geometric restriction into a condition on a string. A route is an ordered string containing six
R’s and six U’s. The copies of each step are indistinguishable, but their positions matter. After any initial segment, x
is the number of R’s used and y the number of U’s used. Thus a route is valid precisely when every initial segment
contains at least as many R’s as U’s.
Ignoring this restriction gives                                    
                                                        12!        12

                                                        6! 6!      6
step sequences. We will subtract those that go above the diagonal.
Reflect the prefix ending at the first violation. Take any forbidden route and stop the first time it reaches
y = x + 1. Such a time exists: a single step changes y − x by one, so a route cannot go from y ≤ x to y > x without
reaching that line. If this prefix has a R’s, it has a + 1 U’s. Exchange R and U throughout this prefix and leave the
rest unchanged. Geometrically, this reflects the prefix across y = x:

                             prefix: a R’s, (a + 1) U’s           ←→   prefix: (a + 1) R’s, a U’s .
                             |           {z           }                |           {z           }
                                  ends at the first y=x+1                 ends at the first x=y+1

The whole transformed string has seven R’s and five U’s: one U has become an R overall. There is no restriction on
the rest of this string.
Prove that the construction is reversible. Start with any string of seven R’s and five U’s. Since it ends with
x − y = 2, it must have a first prefix ending at x = y + 1. Swap R and U in this prefix. The resulting string has six
of each step. Before the chosen endpoint the original prefix has x ≤ y; after reflection this becomes y ≤ x. At the
endpoint it becomes y = x + 1. Thus this is exactly the first violation of the resulting forbidden route.
Conversely, before the first violation of a forbidden route we have y ≤ x, so its reflected prefix has x ≤ y until it first
reaches x = y + 1. Each construction therefore identifies the same stopping point and undoes the other. This is a
one-to-one correspondence: every forbidden route is counted once, and every seven-R, five-U string corresponds to a
forbidden route.

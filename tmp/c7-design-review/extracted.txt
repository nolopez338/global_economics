Evaluated criteria

C7 Compares confidence intervals constructed from samples of different sizes and confidence levels, analysing
    the trade-off between confidence and precision.

How to practise. Attempt each linked C7 task before opening its solution. Identify what changes and what
stays fixed; show calculations supporting a comparison, explanation or justified decision. Retain unrounded
values until the final report. Return arrows lead to the contents, family list and problem.
Connections. C4 supplies known-σ mean intervals and inverse recovery of centre, n or σ; C5 supplies
population-proportion intervals and their interpretation. C6 supplies minimum sizes for SE, margin, total
width, or minimum confidence with fixed precision. Here these tools support design comparisons.

C7 – Interpret the trade-off

The estimated SE depends on the observed share and n, both fixed. Raising confidence increases z ∗ ,
hence margin and width . The 99% procedure has greater approximate long-run coverage but less precision
for this same sample.
In repeated random samples from this registry, about 99% of intervals constructed by this procedure would
contain the fixed share, if its assumptions hold. It is not a claim that 99% of riders lie inside a numerical
interval, nor a probability assigned to p after observation. If a policy requires margin below 5 percentage points
and at least 95% confidence, the 95% analysis meets both; the 90% analysis misses confidence and the 99%
analysis misses precision.

nested; fixed variability and n make the width conclusion hold.

Thus 99% confidence can accompany a narrower projected interval than 95%: widths are about 6.3095 versus
6.4012 percentage points. The size reduction in SE slightly outweighs the larger critical value. Family 3 also
supplies an observed-data counterexample.
What width alone cannot tell us. Width cannot identify size, confidence, centre, variability, population,
interval method or sampling quality without other information. The mean reports and differing observed shares
in Family 4 show why attributing a narrow interval to a large sample can fail. With different observed shares,
use their actual variability factors rather than the fixed-variability ratio.
A narrow interval quantifies sampling uncertainty only within its model. It does not establish absence of
sampling bias: an online-only convenience survey could systematically miss riders with limited digital access.
Random selection from a complete registry and attention to nonresponse matter even when n is large. A
narrower interval also does not guarantee that its observed centre is closer to the true parameter; random error
can put a narrow interval away from truth.
Overlap or non-overlap of separate intervals is not used here as a formal significance test. A comparison of
population parameters needs an appropriate model for their difference, including any dependence between
samples. Confidence describes long-run coverage, not certainty that a particular interval contains its target.
Recommendation in context. Choose B for the original decision because it meets confidence and precision
constraints at the lowest candidate cost. For tighter precision, plan 1,025 usable responses at 95% under
p0 = 0.40, check actual counts and precision after collection, and address coverage and nonresponse separately.

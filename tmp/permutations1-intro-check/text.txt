                                             Introduction: Counting Choices
Multiplication principle. Count a process one step at a time. If the number of available choices at each step is the
same for every possible sequence of earlier choices, multiply these numbers to count the complete outcomes. Order matters
means that exchanging positions changes the outcome.
Notation and when to use it. Let n be the total number of objects, a nonnegative integer, and let r be the number
selected, an integer with 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
• To arrange all n distinct objects in a row, there are n choices for the first position, then n − 1, and so on. Their product
  is called factorial : n! = n(n − 1) · · · 2 · 1 for n ≥ 1. By convention, 0! = 1: there is one empty arrangement.

                                                  1. Progressive Problems

1. Six books have different titles. In how many ways can all six be placed in a single row on a shelf? Each book is used
   once, and two rows are different if their left-to-right orders differ.
2. A club has nine distinct members. In how many ways can it choose a committee of four different members? There are
   no assigned roles, and the order in which members are chosen does not matter.
3. Eight distinct students enter a contest with three awards: first, second, and third place. In how many ways can the
   awards be assigned if each award goes to one student and no student receives more than one award?
4. In how many different left-to-right strings can all the letters of BALLOON be arranged? Use each letter exactly as often as
   it appears in the word. Copies of the same letter are indistinguishable, and the strings need not be English words.
5. Six distinct books, labelled A, B, C, D, E, F , are placed in a row, each exactly once. How many left-to-right arrangements
   have A and B next to each other, but C and D not next to each other? Either order within an adjacent pair is allowed.
6. Four girls, Ada, Bella, Cleo, and Dina, and three boys, Eli, Finn, and Gus, are available for a photograph. Choose five
   different students and arrange them in a row. The row must include Ada, at least two girls, and at least two boys, and
   no two boys may stand next to each other. How many different left-to-right rows are possible? All seven students are
   distinguishable, and each selected student appears exactly once.

                                                  2. Olympiad Challenges

7. Three different numbers are chosen from {1, 2, . . . , 10}. No two chosen numbers may differ by 1. How many unordered
   sets can be chosen? Each number can be used at most once.
8. Arrange the numbers 1, 2, . . . , 7 in a row, each exactly once. Call an index i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , 6} a success if i is immediately
   followed by i + 1, in that order. How many permutations have exactly two successes? Successes may overlap: for
   instance, the consecutive segment 3, 4, 5 contributes successes at both i = 3 and i = 4. Two permutations are different if
   their left-to-right orders differ.

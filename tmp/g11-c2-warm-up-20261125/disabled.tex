\section{Warm-up}
\begin{frame}[t]{A cafeteria report}
Each day, a school samples lunch receipts and reports a range for that day's
\CtwoMean{average purchase amount}.

Over many comparable days, about \CtwoCoverage{9 in 10 reports} included the
true daily average, later checked using all receipts.
\medskip

Today's report gives \CtwoEndpoint{\$7} to \CtwoEndpoint{\$9}.
The full receipt audit later finds an average of \CtwoMean{\$6}.
\medskip

\textbf{Is this one missed range enough to reject the reporting method?}
Explain your reasoning.
\medskip

{\color{GETeal}\textbf{Discuss with a partner: 55 seconds.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Check and connect}
\textbf{One miss is not enough on its own.}

The true average, \CtwoMean{\$6}, is outside \CtwoEndpoint{\$7} to
\CtwoEndpoint{\$9}: this report missed. But a method that has succeeded in
about \CtwoCoverage{9 out of 10 reports} has also had occasional misses.
\medskip

One failure does not establish that the method has become unreliable;
neither does its past record guarantee today's success.
\medskip

\textbf{In the lesson:} compare a single estimate with a range, then investigate
how often ranges from repeated samples contain a fixed population average.
This will make the meaning of ``confidence'' more precise.
\end{frame}

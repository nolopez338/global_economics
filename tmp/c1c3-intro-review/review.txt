1.1   From a decision situation to a profit table

Start with the situation

A school enterprise organises services for community events. Before bookings are confirmed, its students must choose
either a basic service plan or an expanded service plan with more equipment and capacity. Demand may turn out to be
high or low. The enterprise chooses its plan first; demand is revealed later. This timing makes the decision uncertain.

The uncertain process is customers making bookings. High demand and low demand are the two possible states of that
process in this model. The students control the service plan, but they cannot choose the demand state. A consequence
can first be described in words: “the basic plan operates under high demand, earns sales income, and uses staff and
supplies.” We then assign a monetary payoff to that consequence. Here the payoff will be the profit remaining after
costs. Describing what happens and measuring its financial result are related, but different, tasks.


Name the choices, then the states

The basic and expanded plans are the alternatives: the available choices. We now shorten their names to A1 , read
“alternative one,” and A2 , read “alternative two.”


                                      Symbol   Choice made by the enterprise

                                      A1       Choose the basic service plan.
                                      A2       Choose the expanded service plan.

High and low demand are possible outcomes outside the enterprise’s control. We label them S1 , read “state one,” and
S2 , read “state two.”


                                 Symbol    Demand outcome revealed after the choice

                                 S1        High demand.
                                 S2        Low demand.

The small numbers below the letters are subscripts. They identify choices and states; they do not rank them or give
their monetary values.

Check. Can the enterprise choose S1 ? Suggested answer: No. It chooses a plan; customers’ bookings determine which
demand state occurs.




                                                          2
Match one choice with one state

To describe a possible result, name both the chosen plan and the demand outcome. The pair (A1 , S1 ), read “alternative
one with state one,” means the basic plan under high demand. There are four combinations:


                                        Pair         Contextual meaning

                                        (A1 , S1 )   Basic plan, high demand.
                                        (A1 , S2 )   Basic plan, low demand.
                                        (A2 , S1 )   Expanded plan, high demand.
                                        (A2 , S2 )   Expanded plan, low demand.

Only one pair will be realised for this particular decision. We analyse all four beforehand so that the financial consequence
of each possible choice–state combination is recorded.


Give the consequence a monetary value

Revenue is money earned from the activity. Cost is money spent or resources charged to the activity. Profit is
revenue minus cost. Suppose the basic plan under high demand earns 20 thousand USD and costs 9 thousand USD.
Its profit is 20 − 9 = 11 thousand USD. One thousand USD means 1,000 US dollars. Throughout these introductory
examples, monetary totals are in thousand USD. Revenue alone is not the payoff in this activity: profit is.

We introduce a separate symbol for each total. First,

                                                      R(A1 , S1 ) = 20.

Read this as “total revenue for alternative one in state one is 20 thousand USD.” The capital R names total revenue; A1
identifies the basic plan and S1 identifies high demand. The parentheses identify the inputs to this quantity; they do
not mean multiplication.

Next, write the corresponding total cost as
                                                       C(A1 , S1 ) = 9.

Read this as “total cost for alternative one in state one is 9 thousand USD.” Capital C names total cost, with the same
two inputs.

Finally, capital P names profit, which is our payoff:

                                        P (A1 , S1 ) = R(A1 , S1 ) − C(A1 , S1 ) = 11.

Read this as “profit for alternative one in state one equals its revenue minus its cost: 11 thousand USD.” All three
quantities refer to the same plan and demand state. The result is positive because revenue exceeds cost. In separate
small illustrations, revenue 9 and cost 9 give zero profit (9 − 9 = 0); revenue 8 and cost 9 give a loss (8 − 9 = −1
thousand USD).




                                                              3
Organise the four profits

A profit payoff table puts alternatives in rows and states in columns. For example, take the basic plan’s high-demand
revenue of 20 and matching cost of 9, subtract to obtain 11, then put 11 in row A1 , column S1 . Repeat that process for
the other three pairs. The next problem consolidates this process using directly supplied totals. Its input table records
the data; the paired calculation tables match each symbolic subtraction to its arithmetic; the clean final table contains
profits only.

Check. Could we subtract the cost for (A2 , S1 ) from the revenue for (A1 , S1 )? Suggested answer: No. That would mix
two plans. Both amounts in a subtraction must belong to the same alternative–state pair.


1.2   Introductory General Problem (C1–C3)

1.3   Build totals from quantities, rates, and fixed amounts

Generalise a familiar calculation

The first problem has two choices and two states. To refer to any one of them without writing a separate rule for every
cell, use Ai , read “alternative i,” and Sj , read “state j.” The letters i and j are indices: they tell us which alternative
and which state to use. For example, substituting i = 2 and j = 1 gives A2 , S1 , and the expanded-plan, high-demand
pair (A2 , S1 ). In the first problem, R(A2 , S1 ) = 18, C(A2 , S1 ) = 10, and P (A2 , S1 ) = 18 − 10 = 8 thousand USD. The
indices 2 and 1 are identifiers; the number 8 is a monetary value. The number of alternatives is a count, not an index or
a profit. Later problems may have more choices or states.

The relationship already used in every cell can now be written generally:

                                              P (Ai , Sj ) = R(Ai , Sj ) − C(Ai , Sj ).

This says “profit for a chosen alternative and a particular state is the revenue for that pair minus the cost for that same
pair.”


Why build revenue and cost?

A direct model, such as the first problem, supplies total revenue and total cost. Use those totals as given; we cannot
infer a unique set of prices, quantities, or fixed payments from them. A component model instead supplies ingredients:
quantities, rates per unit, and fixed amounts. We must use those ingredients to build total revenue and total cost
independently, then subtract.

We now study a new version of the same school service enterprise. Its plans and demand labels keep their meanings,
but its financial arrangements will be stated component by component. These are new assumptions, not a breakdown of
the first problem’s directly supplied totals. Each extension below explicitly adds an arrangement to this new version.


Quantity: how much service is completed?

Suppose the basic plan completes two service batches under high demand and one under low demand. A service batch
is one package of work for an event; it is the quantity unit throughout this running example. The expanded plan can
complete three batches under high demand and two under low demand. Write q(A1 , S1 ) = 2 batches, read “quantity for
alternative one in state one is two batches.” More generally, q(Ai , Sj ) means the quantity for the named pair.


                            Quantity (service batches)        S1 : high demand       S2 : low demand

                            A1 : basic plan                            2                   1
                            A2 : expanded plan                         3                   2

Demand affects bookings, while the chosen plan affects capacity, so quantity can depend on both inputs. A quantity of
2 batches is not 2 thousand USD.

A revenue rate determined by the plan

Assume initially that the only revenue is payment for each completed batch. The basic plan earns 10 thousand USD per
batch; the expanded plan earns 11 thousand USD per batch for its larger service package. These are earning rates,
rather than total amounts. Write r(A1 ) = 10 and r(A2 ) = 11 thousand USD per batch. Read r(A1 ) as “revenue rate for
alternative one.” More generally, r(Ai ) is the rate set by the plan.

Multiply the number of batches by the payment for each batch:

                                                  R(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )r(Ai ).

For the basic plan under high demand, substitute the quantity and rate:

                                                           2 × 10
                                           R(A1 , S1 ) =              (thousand USD).
                                                           = 20

The units are batches times thousand USD per batch, giving thousand USD. The basic plan’s rate stays at 10 across its
row; under low demand its revenue is 1 × 10 = 10 thousand USD because quantity changes. Dependence tells us which
input determines a component. It does not require different inputs to have different values: two plans could happen to
have equal rates.


A cost rate determined by the plan

Now add a cost assumption: each basic-plan batch uses 3 thousand USD of resources, while each expanded-plan batch
uses 4 thousand USD. These are costs per batch. Write c(A1 ) = 3, read “cost rate for alternative one,” and c(A2 ) = 4, in
thousand USD per batch. The general plan-based cost rate is c(Ai ). If these are the only costs,

                                                  C(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )c(Ai ).

Keep the revenue and cost calculations separate before finding profit:

All three results are in thousand USD. Lowercase r and c name rates per batch; uppercase R and C name monetary

Check. Why is 20 − 3 not the profit for two basic-plan batches? Suggested answer: 20 is total revenue, but 3 is cost
per batch. First find total cost, 2 × 3 = 6, then subtract 20 − 6 = 14.

The result is in thousand USD. The rate is the same down a state column, but total revenue need not be: three batches
under A2 earn 3 × 5 = 15 thousand USD in that same state.

Return now to the plan-based pricing version and extend it: assume the booking scheme’s payment is an addition to
the plan payment, so both rates apply to each batch. Then

                                       R(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )r(Ai ) + q(Ai , Sj )r(Sj )

For the basic plan under high demand, 2 × 10 + 2 × 5 = 2(10 + 5) = 30 thousand USD. Factoring out quantity works
because the same two batches earn both rates. We add 10 and 5 because both use thousand USD per batch; multiplying
the combined rate by two batches gives money.

Check. Under the version with both revenue rates and both cost rates, what is the basic plan’s high-demand profit?
Suggested answer: Revenue is 30, cost is 8, and profit is 30 − 8 = 22 thousand USD. Equal state rates across plans do
not imply equal totals.

Assemble the complete models

We have now taught every ingredient. When all the arrangements just introduced apply together, their complete models
are
                              R(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )[r(Ai ) + r(Sj )] + fr (Ai ) + fr (Sj ),
                                  C(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )[c(Ai ) + c(Sj )] + fc (Ai ) + fc (Sj ),
                                  P (Ai , Sj ) = R(Ai , Sj ) − C(Ai , Sj ).
The four additive revenue components are q(Ai , Sj )r(Ai ), q(Ai , Sj )r(Sj ), fr (Ai ), and fr (Sj ). The four additive cost
components are q(Ai , Sj )c(Ai ), q(Ai , Sj )c(Sj ), fc (Ai ), and fc (Sj ). Each is a monetary amount. Quantity q(Ai , Sj ) is a
multiplier, not an extra monetary component.




                                                                  9
The later sections count components within the revenue and cost models. A one-component example can have one
revenue component and one cost component. The two models need not use matching types. For example, if the stated
arrangements provide only plan-based per-batch revenue and state-based handling cost, then

                              R(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )r(Ai ),        C(Ai , Sj ) = q(Ai , Sj )c(Sj ).

If instead there is only a plan sponsorship payment and a state administration bill, the fixed-only models are

                                       R(Ai , Sj ) = fr (Ai ),        C(Ai , Sj ) = fc (Sj ).

A two-component version might add a fixed plan payment to per-batch plan revenue; a three-component version could
add a fixed state grant as well. Use only components included by the stated arrangements. An excluded component can
be treated as zero. A necessary input that is merely missing is unknown, not automatically zero.


Keep the meaning of a signed adjustment

Some financial descriptions may explicitly give a reduction. For example, a 2 thousand USD rebate on a 9 thousand
USD bill gives total cost 9 + (−2) = 7, whereas an additional charge of 2 gives 9 + 2 = 11. A reduction and a further
cost have opposite effects. If a stated model actually gives a negative total cost, subtract it with parentheses: revenue 5
and total cost −2 give profit 5 − (−2) = 7 thousand USD. These signed cases do not change the basic rule: build the
stated totals, then subtract cost once.

1.4   Advanced General Problem (C1–C3)

A community business must choose the basic operating plan A1 or expanded operating plan A2 before demand is
revealed as high (S1 ) or low (S2 ). Its demand study indicates that plan A1 will complete 2 units in state S1 and 1 in
state S2 , whereas plan A2 will complete 3 and 2 units, respectively. Each completed service batch counts as one unit.
Revenue and cost rates are in thousand USD per unit; fixed amounts and profits are in thousand USD. Each variable
rate applies to every unit completed.

The plans command different base selling rates because they offer different services: each unit under A1 earns an
alternative-based revenue rate of 10, while each unit under A2 earns 11. Demand conditions add a market-based revenue
rate of 5 per unit in S1 or 3 per unit in S2 . The signed agreements also provide plan-based fixed revenue of 7 for A1
and 9 for A2 , while community funding adds state-based fixed revenue of 6 in S1 and 7 in S2 .

The business’s operating budget assigns an alternative-based cost of 3 per unit to A1 and 4 per unit to A2 , reflecting
the resources used by each plan. Demand-related handling adds a further state-based cost of 1 per unit in S1 and 2 per
unit in S2 . Preparing the plans also creates fixed costs of 8 for A1 and 10 for A2 , and state-specific administration
creates fixed costs of 5 in S1 and 3 in S2 . The business must combine all four revenue components and all four cost
components for each pair, then subtract total cost from total revenue.

Practical solving procedure.

  1. Identify who chooses, the alternatives, the uncertain process, its states, the timing, and the consequences in their
     economic context (C1).
  2. Map choices and states to symbols. List all alternative–state pairs.
  3. Record inputs in a Symbol | Value | Meaning in this problem table, with units. Distinguish direct totals, quantities,
     rates, and fixed amounts; check that necessary inputs are supplied.
  4. Calculate revenue for each pair from its stated components, or record the directly supplied total. Match quantity
     units to rate units.
  5. Calculate cost independently for the same pair, or record the supplied total. Add each applicable fixed amount
     once.
  6. Subtract total cost from total revenue (C3). Match the symbolic calculation to the full numerical substitution and
     its result.
  7. Construct the profit payoff table (C3): alternatives in rows, states in columns, profits only in cells, and monetary
     units stated. Keep the same row and column order throughout.

• Is 10 thousand USD per batch a total revenue? No: it is a rate. Two batches earn 20 thousand USD at that rate.
• Does a fixed payment of 7 thousand USD become 14 for two batches? No: add it once. Only per-batch amounts are
  multiplied by quantity.
• With revenue 43 and cost 21 thousand USD, what enters the payoff table? Profit: 43 − 21 = 22 thousand USD.
